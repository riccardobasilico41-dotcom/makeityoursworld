## Apertura personale
Día de los Muertos is Mexico's most photographed festival, and also the one that's hardest to understand from Italy. It isn't a Mexican Halloween: for a few nights a year, families wait for their dead to come back, set up an altar at home with their photos, the dishes they loved, mezcal, pan de muerto and a trail of cempasúchil petals, the orange marigold that shows the way. Then they go to the cemetery, clean the graves, cover them with flowers and candles and stay there, sometimes until dawn, eating, drinking and playing music together with those who are no longer here. UNESCO added it to the list of Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2008.

This itinerary follows the festival from north to south. It starts in Mexico City, with the Catrinas procession and the big public ofrendas; goes through Puebla and Cholula, where a colonial church sits on top of a pyramid with the Popocatépetl volcano behind it; reaches Oaxaca for the three nights that matter, in the cemeteries of Xoxocotlán, at the muerteada of San Agustín Etla and in the markets full of flowers; and ends on the Pacific Ocean, at Puerto Escondido, to work off the festival by the sea. It's meant for people who want to experience the occasion where it's most deeply felt, accepting that for those three nights you sleep little, pay more and book months ahead.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** October 24 to November 8, with the three nights of the dead (October 31, November 1 and 2) all in Oaxaca
- **The festival calendar:** the dates are fixed. On **October 28** many places remember those who died in accidents, **October 31** and **November 1** are dedicated to children (angelitos) and **November 2** to adults. In practice the cemetery vigils are concentrated in the nights between October 31 and November 2, and every town has its own "main" night. In 2026 October 31 falls on a Saturday; in **2027** it will fall on a Sunday, with November 1 on a Monday and November 2 on a Tuesday.
- **Where to be, night by night (this itinerary's version):** **October 31**, the cemetery of **Santa Cruz Xoxocotlán**, south of Oaxaca; **November 1**, the **muerteada of San Agustín Etla**, the biggest comparsa in the Central Valleys, which starts in the evening and goes on until morning; **November 2**, the **Panteón General of Oaxaca** and the last comparsas in the city. The alternatives (Janitzio in Michoacán, Mixquic in Mexico City, the capital's parade) are explained further down under **Variations**.
- **When to book:** lodging in Oaxaca **4-6 months ahead**; in the nights between October 30 and November 2, prices in the city go up two or three times and central places sell out. Evening tours to the cemeteries and the muerteada should be booked at least a few weeks ahead, ADO buses to Oaxaca a few days ahead.
- **Documents:** passport valid for at least 6 months on arrival. No tourist visa for up to 180 days, but the number of days granted is decided by the border agent: keep your accommodation booking and your onward ticket handy, as they may be asked for on arrival. At the Mexico City and Cancún airports you go through the e-gates, which issue the entry permit automatically; save the receipt or the entry stamp and register with the Farnesina's **Dove Siamo nel Mondo** before leaving. E-cigarettes and refill liquids are banned in Mexico.
- **Currency and payments:** Mexican peso (MXN), roughly **€1 = 20 MXN** in 2026. In the city cards are accepted, but at markets, on colectivos, at cemeteries, in mezcal palenques and in the towns of the Central Valleys you need cash in pesos, preferably small bills. Withdraw only from ATMs inside banks or shopping centers.
- **Getting around:** **ADO** buses between cities (Mexico City-Puebla about 2 hours, 200-300 MXN; Puebla-Oaxaca about 4.5 hours, 450-800 MXN depending on class); **colectivos** and taxis for the towns around Oaxaca; **organized evening tours** for the cemeteries and for Etla, because on those nights taxis and Uber almost disappear. From Oaxaca to Puerto Escondido, the new Barranca Larga-Ventanilla highway has cut the trip to about **3 to 3.5 hours** by bus or van; alternatively there's a flight on small planes (about 35 minutes). Return from Puerto Escondido by direct flight to Mexico City (about 1 hour).
- **From the airport to the centre:** at **AICM (MEX)** the **authorized taxi** is prepaid at the desks inside the terminal, about 400-450 MXN to Roma and Condesa, 30-60 minutes depending on traffic · **Uber** and **DiDi** pick up in the marked zones, about 200-300 MXN · **Metrobús line 4** runs from terminals 1 and 2 to the Centro Histórico, about 30 MXN with the card bought at the machines · the metro (Terminal Aérea) costs 5 MXN, but with luggage at rush hour it's impractical. Never take taxis from outside the terminal. At **Oaxaca (OAX)** the official colectivo to the centre costs about 135 MXN per person, a taxi about 490 MXN, tickets at the arrivals desk.
- **Weather:** at the end of October the rainy season has just ended. Mexico City (7,349 ft / 2,240 m) and Oaxaca (5,085 ft / 1,550 m) have mild days and cool nights, under 50 °F (10 °C) in the cemeteries after midnight: bring a jacket. On the coast it's hot and humid, 86 °F (30 °C), and the Pacific hurricane season officially ends on November 30.
- **Health:** no mandatory vaccinations; hepatitis A and typhoid recommended. Bottled water only, no ice. Repellent and long sleeves on the Pacific coast: Viaggiare Sicuri reports dengue sharply on the rise and cases of chikungunya in the state of Oaxaca as well. Public hospitals don't treat foreigners and private ones are very expensive: health insurance with repatriation is essential. In Mexico City you feel the altitude for the first few days: little exertion and no alcohol excess on arrival.
- **Safety:** the stops on this itinerary (Mexico City, Puebla, Oaxaca, Puerto Escondido) are well-established tourist areas, and the state of Oaxaca is not among the areas of particular caution for the Farnesina. The concrete risks are pickpockets in festival crowds, the metro and bus terminals, street-hailed taxis (Viaggiare Sicuri recommends taxis de sitio, booked through the hotel, or Uber), and in Oaxaca the **bloqueos**, road blockades by unions and communities, which can stop a bus or close a site without notice. For getting around, use only first-class buses and don't travel by road at night. **Michoacán** (Pátzcuaro and Janitzio), on the other hand, is among the states where the Farnesina recommends avoiding non-essential travel: it's covered in the dedicated variation.
- **Respect in the cemeteries:** the graves belong to grieving families, they aren't a set. Ask before photographing people and altars, no flash a few inches away, don't sit on the graves, stay quiet near people who are praying. Bringing a candle or a bunch of cempasúchil to leave is a gesture that's appreciated.
- **Time zone:** UTC-6 all year (central Mexico hasn't used daylight saving time since 2022): 7 hours behind Italy at the end of October, 6 after the return to standard time.
- **Electricity:** 127 V, type A and B plugs, you need an adapter.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Arrival in Mexico City
We arrived at Benito Juárez airport (AICM), which you leave by authorized taxi at a prepaid fare, bought at the counters inside the terminal, or by app. For the first few days the best base is **Roma** or **Condesa**, two tree-lined, safe neighborhoods full of cafés, 20-30 minutes from the historic center. From the airport it takes anywhere from half an hour to more than an hour, depending on traffic. Before leaving the terminal it's worth withdrawing a few thousand pesos from an ATM inside the airport and buying a local SIM. We kept the first afternoon light: at 7,349 feet, after an intercontinental flight, tiredness hits twice as hard.

In the evening a stroll in Parque México and a first dinner of tacos al pastor in a neighborhood taquería were enough. In these days the city is already dressing up for the festival: shop windows with sugar calaveras, bakeries with pan de muerto, the first altars in the stores. For the following days the metro is cheap and fast, but very crowded at rush hour: for short hops between Roma, Condesa and the center, Uber and DiDi work well too. *With the altitude, on the first evening drink plenty of water and leave the mezcal for later.*

**In practice:** authorized prepaid taxi or app from AICM to Roma and Condesa, about 400-450 MXN by taxi and 200-300 MXN by app · metro about 5 MXN a ride · Uber and DiDi widely available across the city · withdraw cash only from ATMs inside banks, malls or the airport · book Roma or Condesa well ahead for the festival weeks.

### Day 2 — The Centro Histórico and the Mega Procesión de Catrinas
We spent the morning in the **Centro Histórico**: the **Zócalo**, where at the end of October the city sets up a big public ofrenda, the Metropolitan Cathedral, the **Templo Mayor** with the remains of the Aztec capital of Tenochtitlán, the Palacio Nacional with Diego Rivera's murals, and then Madero street to the **Palacio de Bellas Artes**. Lunch is at a market or in one of the center's historic cantinas.

The afternoon belongs to the **Mega Procesión de Catrinas**, which in 2026 was set for Sunday, October 25 from about 6:00 pm, from the Ángel de la Independencia along the Paseo de la Reforma to the Zócalo. It's free and anyone can take part dressed and made up as a Catrina: in the afternoon, around the Ángel, there are street makeup artists who paint your face for a few hundred pesos. The date changes every year: check it on the city government's channels.

**In practice:** Templo Mayor: closed Mondays, INAH ticket · Palacio Nacional: free admission with ID, hours vary and there are lines; you can only enter with your passport · procession: arrive an hour early to find a spot along Reforma; backpack in front, phone in a closed pocket.

### Day 3 — Teotihuacán at dawn
We dedicated the day out of town to **Teotihuacán**, about an hour to the north-east: the "city of the gods," with the Pyramid of the Sun, the Pyramid of the Moon and the Calzada de los Muertos. It's best to arrive at opening, at 8, when the light is soft and the organized groups aren't there yet; in the middle hours the sun beats down and there's no shade. The classic route starts from the **Ciudadela**, with the Temple of the Feathered Serpent, and walks up the Calzada to the **Pyramid of the Sun** and the **Plaza of the Moon**: a good couple of kilometers on foot, plus detours. If you want to see it from above, you can book a hot-air balloon flight at dawn, roughly 2,500-4,000 MXN per person.

You get there by direct bus from the Terminal del Norte (about an hour, a little over 100 MXN) or on a tour. From 2026 admission for foreigners went up to about **210 MXN**. Bring a hat, sunscreen and at least a liter and a half of water each: there are plenty of vendors on site, but no shade. *After the shooting in April 2026 the site reopened with tighter checks and for a while the Pyramid of the Moon stayed closed: check beforehand which structures are open.* We got back to the city in the early afternoon, with the evening free in Condesa.

**In practice:** archaeological zone open daily from 8 am to 5 pm, last entry 4:30 pm · ticket about 210 MXN for foreigners · bus from the Terminal del Norte about an hour · book a balloon flight a few days ahead, departure before dawn · small backpack and no drones.

### Day 4 — Anthropology Museum, Chapultepec and Coyoacán
The **Museo Nacional de Antropología**, in Chapultepec Forest, is the key to understanding everything you see in the rest of the trip, from the Mexica gods of death to the Zapotec and Mixtec cultures of Oaxaca. You need at least three hours; it's closed Mondays and from 2026 the ticket for foreigners costs about **210 MXN**.

In the afternoon we went to **Coyoacán**: the old colonial center, the market with the tostadas and Frida Kahlo's **Casa Azul**, to be booked online a few days ahead because at the end of October it sells out. In these weeks the nurseries of **Xochimilco** and the neighborhood markets fill up with cempasúchil: if you still have energy you can stop by one of the big flower markets, or spend the evening at a historic cantina with mariachis in Plaza Garibaldi, going by taxi and not on foot.

**In practice:** Anthropology Museum closed on Mondays, about 210 MXN, at least three hours · Casa Azul closed on Mondays, foreigners' ticket about 320 MXN, online only with a fixed entry time and no large backpacks · Chapultepec to Coyoacán by Uber about 30-45 minutes depending on traffic · Plaza Garibaldi at night only by taxi or Uber, both ways.

### Day 5 — Puebla, the city of talavera
On the ADO bus from the TAPO or the Terminal del Sur we reached **Puebla** (about 2 hours). The historic center, a UNESCO site, is one of the most beautiful in the country: facades covered in **talavera** tiles, the Cathedral with the tallest towers in Mexico, the **Capilla del Rosario** in the church of Santo Domingo, all gilded stucco, and the **Biblioteca Palafoxiana**, the first public library in the Americas. The center is easily walked: start from the **Zócalo** and the Cathedral, move on to the Palafoxiana next door, then head up toward Santo Domingo, leaving the afternoon for the streets of workshops.

Puebla is also one of the capitals of Mexican cooking. Here you eat **mole poblano**, the dark sauce of chiles, spices and chocolate, **cemitas** and evening chalupas, camote sweets on the Calle de los Dulces. In these days the Zócalo and the Barrio del Artista host ofrendas and altar contests. If you want to take home a piece of talavera, buy it from certified workshops. *Authentic talavera carries a designation of origin: the pieces sold for a few pesos on street stalls are almost always industrial.*

**In practice:** ADO bus Mexico City-Puebla about 2 hours, 200-300 MXN, frequent departures · Biblioteca Palafoxiana closed on Mondays, admission a few dozen pesos and free on Tuesdays · Capilla del Rosario open outside mass times · sleep in the historic center around the Zócalo, or straight in Cholula for tomorrow's stop.

### Day 6 — Cholula, the church on the pyramid and the Popocatépetl
**Cholula** is 20-30 minutes from Puebla and it's worth sleeping there at least one night. The **Great Pyramid** is the largest in the world by volume, even though today it looks like a grass-covered hill: you visit the tunnels dug by archaeologists and the site at its foot. On top the conquistadors built the **Santuario de la Virgen de los Remedios**, the yellow church that appears in almost every photo of Cholula.

The view depends on the weather. On clear mornings, frequent in early November, behind the church you can see the **Popocatépetl**, the active 17,700-foot (5,400 m) volcano that often smokes, and the Iztaccíhuatl. The best light is at dawn and at sunset, walking up the pyramid's ramp. In the afternoon you can add the indigenous baroque churches of **Tonantzintla** and **Acatepec**, a few miles away.

*Good to know: the Popocatépetl is monitored by CENAPRED with an alert "traffic light"; in phases of greater activity the ash can close the Puebla or Mexico City airport for a few hours. It doesn't affect the visit to Cholula, but it's useful to know it happens.*

**In practice:** Cholula is 20-30 minutes from Puebla by Uber or taxi · archaeological zone and tunnels closed on Mondays, inexpensive INAH ticket · the climb to the sanctuary is on foot and free, best at dawn or sunset · Tonantzintla and Acatepec a few minutes away by taxi · check the Popocatépetl's status on CENAPRED's alert "traffic light".

### Day 7 — Toward Oaxaca: markets and the first comparsas
The ADO bus from Puebla (CAPU) to **Oaxaca** takes about 4.5 hours across the sierra, and should be booked in advance because in the days before the festival seats run out. We arrived in the early afternoon in a city already in full ferment: arches of flowers over doorways, altars in the shops, the square of Santo Domingo full of people.

In the afternoon we went to the **Mercado 20 de Noviembre** and the **Benito Juárez**, where you buy pan de muerto, chocolate to dissolve in water, candles and sugar figurines. In the evening, in the barrios of the center like **Jalatlaco** and Xochimilco, the first **comparsas** start to roam: parades of masked neighbors with a brass band, dancing through the streets and sometimes offering mezcal to anyone who joins.

*Good to know: in these days Oaxaca is as full as at any other time of the year. Barrio comparsas almost never have published schedules; you find them by asking at the hotel or following the music.*

**In practice:** ADO bus Puebla (CAPU)-Oaxaca about 4.5 hours, 450-800 MXN depending on class, book a few days ahead · the 20 de Noviembre and Benito Juárez markets are open daily, a few blocks from the Zócalo · cash in small bills · for neighborhood comparsas, ask at your hotel the same evening.

### Day 8 — October 31: Monte Albán and the night at Xoxocotlán
In the morning we climbed **Monte Albán**, the Zapotec capital built by leveling the top of a mountain, 20 minutes from the city. Better to get there early, because in the middle hours it's hot and there's no shade, and then head back to the city to rest: the night is long.

The evening of October 31 belongs to **Santa Cruz Xoxocotlán**, the most famous cemetery in the Central Valleys, about six miles south. There are two cemeteries: the **Panteón Viejo**, around the ruins of a roofless church, the more intimate, and the **Panteón Nuevo**, bigger and more crowded, with a fair outside. Families arrive after sunset and stay until late at night among candles, flowers and bands playing beside the graves.

**In practice:** evening tours from Oaxaca: roughly 600-1,200 MXN per person, with transport and often other cemeteries in the area · by taxi: agree on the return trip too beforehand, because after midnight finding a free one is hard · clothing: jacket, closed shoes (the ground is uneven) and a flashlight · entry to the cemeteries is free: no tour sells an "access."

### Day 9 — November 1: Teotitlán del Valle and the muerteada of San Agustín Etla
After a slow morning we went into the Tlacolula valley: the **Árbol del Tule**, the Montezuma cypress with one of the widest trunks in the world, and **Teotitlán del Valle**, the Zapotec town of wool rugs dyed with natural colors. In these days families keep altars at home; in the weaving workshops you sometimes get to see one, but you only go in if invited.

The night of November 1 belongs to the **muerteada of San Agustín Etla**, about 40 minutes north of Oaxaca. It's the biggest and most famous comparsa in the Central Valleys: hundreds of people in masks and costume, with bands, firecrackers and mezcal, move through the town from house to house all night. In past years the parade started around 10 pm and went on until morning; for 2026 the program should be checked on the municipality's channels. It's loud, very boozy and crowded, and the simplest solution is a tour with return transport.

*Good to know: if you'd rather have a quieter night, you can stay in the city and visit the **Panteón General**, or the smaller cemeteries of the valley with a tour.*

**In practice:** Árbol del Tule about twenty minutes from Oaxaca, a few pesos to enter the enclosure · Teotitlán del Valle about 30-40 minutes by colectivo or taxi · when November 1 falls on a Sunday, as in 2026, the big weekly Tlacolula market is on the same road · for Etla, a tour with the ride back included, cash, a jacket and earplugs.

### Day 10 — November 2: the Panteón General of Oaxaca
November 2 is the day of the adult dead and of goodbyes. After the night at Etla, you sleep in the morning. In the afternoon we wandered the center: the ofrendas and the tapetes, the colored-sand carpets in front of churches and in public buildings, the **Templo de Santo Domingo** with its museum and the ethnobotanical garden, which can only be visited on a guided tour at fixed times. Spanish tours run almost daily, English ones only on some days of the week. *The Museo de las Culturas, inside the former convent of Santo Domingo, is closed on Mondays like nearly all INAH museums: in years when November 2 falls on a Monday, as in 2026, visit it on one of the previous days.*

In the evening the meeting point is the **Panteón General** (San Miguel), the city's monumental cemetery, with the graves lit by candles. It's a fifteen- to twenty-minute walk from the center, the easiest cemetery of the whole festival, with no need for a tour or a taxi. The last comparsas roam the city. After three nights in a row it's better not to stay out too late, because tomorrow you leave early for the mezcal road. From the next day the altars start to be taken down and the festival ends.

**In practice:** Museo de las Culturas de Oaxaca closed on Mondays, INAH ticket about 100 MXN · ethnobotanical garden by guided tour only, ask about times and languages at the gate the day before · Panteón General on foot from the center, free entry · book tomorrow's tour by the evening.

### Day 11 — The mezcal road: Santiago Matatlán, Mitla and Hierve el Agua
A full day heading east on highway 190, on a tour or with a car and driver. The first stop is **Santiago Matatlán**, which calls itself the "world capital of mezcal": dozens of **palenques**, the artisanal distilleries where the agave is cooked in underground stone ovens, crushed with a stone wheel pulled by a horse and distilled in copper or clay stills. Almost all offer a visit and tasting free or for a few pesos, and there's no obligation to buy.

Then **Mitla**, the Zapotec religious center with geometric stone mosaics laid without mortar, unique in Mesoamerica. The last stop is **Hierve el Agua**, the "petrified waterfalls" of calcium carbonate above a valley, with natural pools overlooking the void. The site has been disputed for years between two communities, San Lorenzo Albarradas and San Isidro Roaguía, and has had sudden closures, one of which lasted from 2020 to October 2021. In 2026 visitors describe it as open, but check the week before, by asking at the hotel or a local operator. If it's closed, the day still works with Matatlán, Mitla and the ruins of **Yagul**.

**In practice:** group tour from Oaxaca: roughly 600-900 MXN per person, admissions not included · admission to Hierve el Agua, when open: 50-100 MXN between community fee and ticket, cash only · Mitla: INAH ticket · if you drive: no mezcal for whoever's at the wheel, there are checks.

### Day 12 — From Oaxaca to Puerto Escondido
The transfer to the coast changed with the **Barranca Larga-Ventanilla highway**, fully opened in 2024: where before it took six or seven hours of curves, today by bus or van it takes about 3 to 3.5. You drop from Oaxaca's 5,085 feet (1,550 m) to sea level, and the temperature rises by fifteen degrees. Leave in the morning, with a seat booked the day before, and ask at the hotel whether there are bloqueos on the road: arriving in the early afternoon leaves time to settle in and get down to the beach.

**Puerto Escondido** is more a collection of neighborhoods than a town. **Zicatela** is the long surfers' beach, with the "Mexican Pipeline" wave, and has the nightlife; **La Punta**, at the end of Zicatela, is the younger, hostel-heavy side; **Rinconada** and **Carrizalillo** are quieter. We watched the first sunset from the bay of **Carrizalillo**, which you reach by going down about 160 steps. *The climb back up in the heat is tiring: wear comfortable shoes and bring water.* In the evening you eat fish along Zicatela's main strip or at La Punta.

**In practice:** van or bus Oaxaca-Puerto Escondido about 3 to 3.5 hours, 300-500 MXN · check for bloqueos before leaving · Carrizalillo about 160 steps · sleep at La Punta on a budget, in Zicatela for nightlife, in Rinconada and Carrizalillo for quiet.

### Day 13 — Puerto Escondido: surf, beaches and turtles
Morning in the water. At Zicatela the big wave is only for experienced surfers; for a first lesson you go to Carrizalillo or La Punta, with softer waves (group lessons roughly 600-900 MXN). **Playa Manzanillo** and **Puerto Angelito** are coves where you can swim and snorkel well.

At sunset, on **Playa Bacocho**, some conservation projects release newly hatched turtles from their nurseries into the sea, in exchange for a small donation. At the end of October and in November the hatching season is in full swing. It's a regulated activity: you don't touch the hatchlings, you don't use flash and you stay behind the marked line.

*Good to know: Zicatela has strong rip currents and isn't a swimming beach. Every year there are drownings: swim only in the sheltered coves.*

**In practice:** group surf lessons about 600-900 MXN, best early in the morning · Manzanillo and Puerto Angelito reachable on foot or in a few minutes by taxi from the center · turtle releases at Bacocho at sunset, for a donation, ask about times on the day · no swimming at Zicatela.

### Day 14 — Manialtepec Lagoon and the bioluminescence
The **Manialtepec Lagoon** is about 20 minutes west of town, and you go twice: at dawn, by boat among the mangroves, for the birds (herons, cormorants, kingfishers), and at night for the **bioluminescence**, the plankton that lights up blue when the water moves. Moonless nights are the best, and after rain the effect is stronger. The peak is in the rainy season, from July to September; in November the glow is weaker and changes from one night to the next, so ask the operator how the previous nights went.

The rest of the day was free: beach, hammock, a pescado a la talla in a palapa. For the lagoon, choose an operator that uses kayaks or rowboats for the last stretch, because the motor and sunscreen in the water disturb a fragile ecosystem. Tours cost roughly 500-900 MXN per person. *Crocodiles live in the lagoon too: get in the water only where and when the guide says.* The night trip leaves after sunset and lasts a couple of hours, back at the hotel in the evening.

**In practice:** lagoon about 20 minutes' drive west, almost all tours include hotel pickup · dawn and night tours about 500-900 MXN · check the lunar calendar, with a full moon the glow is hard to see · no sunscreen or repellent on your skin if you swim.

### Day 15 — Mazunte, Zipolite and Punta Cometa
A day trip east, about an hour and a quarter by road: **Mazunte**, the village that's home to the Centro Mexicano de la Tortuga, **San Agustinillo** and **Zipolite**, the historic free and nudist beach of the Mexican Pacific. Leave in the morning and start with the **Centro Mexicano de la Tortuga**, with its tanks and its route dedicated to sea turtles, then have lunch in San Agustinillo and spend the afternoon at Zipolite. *Zipolite's currents are as strong as Zicatela's: swim only when the sea is calm and stay close to shore.*

We watched the sunset from **Punta Cometa**, the headland above Mazunte, climbing a short path through the dry vegetation. You need a flashlight for the way down, which is in the dark. You get there by colectivo or shared taxi along coastal highway 200. If you prefer, you can make it a change of base and sleep in Mazunte for the last two nights, flying home directly from Huatulco or Puerto Escondido airport. If you return to Puerto Escondido the same day, keep in mind that colectivos thin out after sunset: arrange a taxi for the way back in advance.

**In practice:** Puerto Escondido-Mazunte about an hour and a quarter along highway 200, by colectivo or shared taxi · Centro Mexicano de la Tortuga closed Monday and Tuesday, admission a few dozen pesos · flashlight for the walk back from Punta Cometa · if you sleep in Mazunte, fly from Huatulco, about an hour's drive away.

### Day 16 — Return via Mexico City
Direct flight from Puerto Escondido to Mexico City (about an hour; Aeroméxico, Volaris and Viva) and an international connection. If the flight is in the early afternoon, the morning is free for a last swim at Carrizalillo or breakfast on the beach: the airport is a few minutes by taxi from Zicatela and the center. Mezcal bought in Matatlán goes in checked luggage, well wrapped. *When re-entering the European Union, the duty-free allowance is one liter of spirits per person.*

Leave at least four hours between the two flights: Puerto Escondido's airport is small, domestic flights pile up delays and at AICM you go through security again for the second flight. At AICM Volaris and Viva use Terminal 1 and Aeroméxico Terminal 2: if your intercontinental flight leaves from the other terminal, you transfer on the aerotrén, reserved for passengers with a boarding pass, or by shuttle bus. Check the terminal on both bookings before you set off.

**In practice:** Puerto Escondido airport a few minutes from the beaches, arrive at least an hour and a half early · flight to Mexico City about an hour · at least four hours between domestic and intercontinental flights · at AICM Terminal 1 for Volaris and Viva, Terminal 2 for Aeroméxico · mezcal in checked luggage, one liter duty-free into the EU.

## Variations and Alternative Night Calendar
**Why Janitzio isn't in the main itinerary.** The island of **Janitzio**, in Lake Pátzcuaro (Michoacán), is the most famous image of Día de los Muertos: a fishing village on an island, with a cemetery that on the night of **November 1** lights up with thousands of candles while boats arrive from the shores. It has two problems for this trip. The first is geography: Pátzcuaro is west of Mexico City (4-5 hours by bus), Oaxaca south-east (6-7 hours), and Janitzio's night coincides with Etla's. The second is safety: Viaggiare Sicuri (card updated May 2026) recommends avoiding non-essential travel in Michoacán, the scene of frequent shootouts between security forces and criminal groups; in 2026 the state also remains at level 4 ("do not travel") in US advisories and is among those advised against by Switzerland. The lake area is touristy and very busy in those days, but anyone choosing this variation is going against an explicit Farnesina recommendation.

**Michoacán + Mixquic variation (Janitzio before Oaxaca).** If you choose Janitzio, the right order is to put it **before** Oaxaca: from Mexico City you go to Pátzcuaro, return to Mexico City on November 2 for the night at **Mixquic** and only then head down to Puebla and Oaxaca. The calendar becomes:
- **October 29-30:** Mexico City → Morelia → Pátzcuaro by bus (highway 15D), the market and the artisan tianguis of the Noche de Ánimas in the square.
- **October 31:** vigil in the cemetery of **Tzintzuntzan**, the old Purépecha capital on the lakeshore.
- **November 1:** the night of **Janitzio**, by launch from Pátzcuaro's Muelle General; alternatively the cemeteries of Ihuatzio or Jarácuaro, less crowded.
- **November 2:** return by bus to Mexico City (about 5 hours) and evening at **San Andrés Mixquic**, south-east of the capital, for the **Alumbrada**, the main night of its cemetery; you get there by bus from Taxqueña in 1.5-2 hours or on a tour.
- **From November 3:** Puebla and Cholula, then Oaxaca after the festival is over, with the altars still in many homes and the mezcal road, then Puerto Escondido.
The price of this variation is that you see Oaxaca after the festival. It's the choice of someone who wants the lake and the cemetery on the island more than anything else.

**Parade variation.** Mexico City's **Gran Desfile de Día de Muertos**, born in 2016 after the opening sequence of a James Bond film, in 2026 was on Saturday, October 31 from 2:00 pm, ending at the Zócalo. Anyone who wants it at all costs has to stay in the capital on the 31st and reach Oaxaca on November 1 (one-hour flight), missing Xoxocotlán. The date changes every year.

**If you have 10 days.** Three days in Mexico City, a flight to Oaxaca (about an hour), five days in Oaxaca from October 29 to November 2 with the mezcal road on the 3rd, and a return from Oaxaca via Mexico City. You cut Puebla and the sea.

**If you have 3 weeks.** Add two nights in **Mazunte** and a trip to the **Chacahua Lagoon** (a national park with a fishing village reachable by boat), or, before Oaxaca, a night in **Huaquechula** (Puebla), famous for the monumental white and gold altars dedicated to the year's dead.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
Indicative ranges per night for a double room, outside the festival nights; in Oaxaca between October 30 and November 2 count on double or triple.
- **Mexico City:** Roma or Condesa. Budget: hostels and private rooms 600-1,200 MXN. Mid-range: boutique hotels 1,800-3,500 MXN. High-end: design hotels over 5,000 MXN. The Centro Histórico is convenient by day but emptier and less pleasant in the evening.
- **Puebla and Cholula:** in Puebla the historic center around the Zócalo (mid-range 1,200-2,500 MXN, with many hotels in colonial buildings); in Cholula there are hostels and small hotels at the foot of the pyramid, from 700 MXN, and a few hotels with a terrace overlooking the volcano.
- **Oaxaca:** the center between Santo Domingo and Jalatlaco, everything on foot. Budget: hostels 400-800 MXN per bed on festival nights. Mid-range: casas de huéspedes and small hotels 1,500-3,500 MXN. High-end: hotels in colonial houses with a patio over 5,000 MXN. Book 4-6 months ahead.
- **Puerto Escondido:** La Punta for hostels and budget (rooms from 600 MXN), Zicatela for mid-range and nightlife (1,500-3,000 MXN), Carrizalillo and Rinconada for quieter hotels and a few high-end places.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **Mexico City:** tacos al pastor and de suadero in the taquerías, tamales and atole in the morning, churros with chocolate, chilaquiles for breakfast. For chef-driven cooking, the best-known restaurants in Roma and Polanco need to be booked weeks ahead.
- **Puebla:** mole poblano, cemitas, chalupas and molotes in the evening; camote sweets and tortitas de Santa Clara. Chiles en nogada are an August-September dish: in November few places make them.
- **Oaxaca:** the seven moles (mole negro is the festive one), **tlayudas** cooked over the embers, quesillo, chapulines (toasted grasshoppers) at the market, **tejate** (a cold corn and cacao drink), chocolate in water with pan de muerto. The **pasillo de humo** at the Mercado 20 de Noviembre, where you choose raw meat and have it grilled on the spot, is the simplest meal in the city.
- **Mezcal:** tasted at Matatlán with the producers and in Oaxaca at the mezcalerías in the center. Artisanal mezcal is sipped in small sips, not shot.
- **Puerto Escondido:** pescado a la talla (fish opened and grilled with chile sauce), ceviche and tiritas, fish tacos, fresh fruit on the beach.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Book Oaxaca first**, as soon as the dates are set: it's the bottleneck of the trip.
- **Don't take street-hailed taxis in Mexico City**: only taxis de sitio, prepaid at the airport or by app.
- **Cash in your pocket for the festival nights**, in small bills: ATMs far away, lines and cards that won't go through.
- **Tours for the nights in the towns**: not for the entry, which is free, but for the return. After midnight the taxis disappear.
- **Respect in the cemeteries**: ask before photographing, no flash in faces, don't sit on graves, don't enter home altars without an invitation.
- **Check for bloqueos in Oaxaca** before every long trip, by asking at the hotel: road blockades are frequent and can last hours.
- **Check Hierve el Agua the week before**: closures from disputes between communities are frequent and not always announced.
- **Don't swim at Zicatela**: strong rip currents, swim only in the coves.
- **Leave a four-hour margin between the domestic flight and the intercontinental flight** on the way home.

## Scams and Common Annoyances
- **Unauthorized taxis** at the airport and bus stations, with inflated fares or, in the worst cases, robberies: use only the prepaid counters or apps.
- **Street money changers** and isolated ATMs with skimmers: change money at a bank or a casa de cambio, withdraw inside banks.
- **"Cemetery tours with exclusive access"**: entry to the panteones is free, you only pay for transport and guide.
- **"Artisanal" mezcal sold in the center** at producer prices but diluted or industrial: better to buy from the palenques or from mezcalerías with a label and lot number.
- **Tourist prices at the markets on festival nights**: for flowers, candles and food, ask the price first.
- **Pickpockets in the crowd** of the Catrinas procession, in markets and in the metro: backpack in front, no phone in the back pocket.

## Indicative Budget 2026
Per person, sharing a double room, intercontinental flights excluded. On festival nights in Oaxaca the cost of lodging goes up a lot.
- **Budget (hostels, street food, buses):** 1,200-1,800 MXN a day, about €60-90.
- **Mid-range (boutique hotels, restaurants, a few tours):** 2,500-4,000 MXN a day, about €125-200.
- **High-end (upscale hotels, private driver, chef-driven cooking):** over 6,000 MXN a day, from €300 up.
- **Individual items:** Teotihuacán and the Anthropology Museum about 210 MXN each for foreigners; Mexico City-Puebla bus 200-300 MXN; Puebla-Oaxaca 450-800 MXN; Oaxaca-Puerto Escondido 300-500 MXN by bus or van; evening cemetery tour 600-1,200 MXN; mezcal road tour 600-900 MXN; Puerto Escondido-Mexico City flight roughly 1,200-3,000 MXN booked in advance.

## Chiusura personale
Día de los Muertos surprises anyone who arrives expecting a macabre festival: in the cemeteries you hear talking, laughing, music, and children run among the graves. This itinerary is built so that the three nights of the festival all fall in Oaxaca, where the observance is most deeply rooted and most varied, and so that beforehand there's time to understand the country in Mexico City and Puebla and afterward time to rest by the ocean. It works for people who accept sleeping little three nights in a row, booking months ahead and entering the cemeteries as a guest.
