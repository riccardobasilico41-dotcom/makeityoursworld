# The Sicilian Islands: Egadi, Pantelleria and Lampedusa in Two Weeks

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 in progress (itinerary and practical information verified; the first-person passages are written as a draft to be reread and personalized)
**Periodo del viaggio:** **late May-June or September**
**Durata:** 12 days, 11 nights — three archipelagos that don't connect with each other
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independent, by domestic flights, ferries, bike and scooter
**Categoria:** Europa · Italia · Sicilia · isole · mare · vulcani

---

## Apertura personale
These are not the Aeolian Islands. The Aeolians are a compact, connected archipelago that you can do in a week with a single loop. Favignana, Pantelleria and Lampedusa are **three separate worlds, hundreds of kilometers from one another, that don't connect directly** and have almost nothing in common except the administrative province.

The thing to understand before booking is geographic, and it changes everything: **Pantelleria is closer to Tunisia than to Sicily**, and **Lampedusa sits on the African continental shelf**: geologically it isn't Europe. It's not a trivia detail: you see it in the landscape, you feel it in the wind, you taste it on the plate.

So the itinerary that follows is honest about one thing: **there is one day that is almost entirely transit**, and it's unavoidable. If you don't feel up to it, do two archipelagos out of three, or just one, and do it well. But those who do all three in a row get something rare: they cross the Strait of Sicily in stages and watch Europe turn into Africa one island at a time.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** **late May, June and September.** July and August are packed, expensive and windy. **April, May and October** are excellent for Pantelleria (which isn't a beach island) and for hiking, less so for swimming. **In winter** almost everything closes and connections thin out.
- **Logistics, which is the critical point:**
  - **Egadi:** frequent hydrofoils from **Trapani**, twenty minutes to Favignana. It's the easiest archipelago in Italy to reach.
  - **Pantelleria:** **flights** from Palermo, Trapani and Catania (under **territorial continuity**, with discounted fares for residents and extra seasonal flights from the mainland), or the **overnight ferry from Trapani**, five to six hours, **which is often canceled when the sea is rough**.
  - **Lampedusa:** **flights** from Palermo and Catania under territorial continuity, plus seasonal connections from the mainland; or the **ship from Porto Empedocle**, about eight hours.
  - **Pantelleria and Lampedusa are not connected to each other.** To get from one to the other you go back to Sicily. **That's the reason for the transit day**, and it should be factored in beforehand rather than discovered on the spot.
- **The wind decides.** On all three, and especially on Pantelleria, which calls itself "the island of the wind," **the scirocco and the mistral cancel sailings, move boats around and close exposed coves**. Rule of thumb: **don't schedule a return flight on the same day as the last ferry**, and keep a margin.
- **You need a vehicle on the islands:**
  - **Favignana** is toured **by bicycle**, and that's the right choice: it's flat in the eastern part, small, and the distances are all under six kilometers.
  - **Pantelleria** requires a **car or scooter**: it's big (about 83 km²), mountainous, and the distances are real. **In season book ahead**, because few vehicles are available.
  - **Lampedusa** is toured by scooter or car; it's small, but the beaches are at the extremes.
- **Fresh water:** on **Pantelleria and Lampedusa** water is scarce, comes from desalination plants and cisterns and is used sparingly. The same goes for the smaller Egadi islands.
- **Protected areas:** the Egadi are **the largest marine protected area in Europe**, with zones of differentiated protection in which fishing, anchoring and navigation are regulated. On **Lampedusa** the **Isola dei Conigli Nature Reserve** has **capped access and strict rules tied to the nesting of *Caretta caretta* turtles**: **hours, off-limits zones, a ban on bringing certain items onto the beach and temporary closures of the islet**. **The rules change every season: it's worth checking them beforehand.**
- **Food, and it changes three times:**
  - **Egadi:** **bluefin tuna**, in all its parts (ventresca, bottarga, mosciame, lattume), because here there was one of the most important tuna traps in the Mediterranean.
  - **Pantelleria:** land and garden cooking, not sea. **Ravioli amari** with ricotta and mint, **insalata pantesca** (potatoes, tomato, capers, oregano, onion), raw **pesto pantesco**, the **capers** and the **Passito**.
  - **Lampedusa:** fish from the Strait of Sicily, **red prawns**, groupers and amberjack, with a cuisine that looks south.
- **Wines:** **Passito di Pantelleria** from **Zibibbo**, sweet and amber; the **Moscato di Pantelleria**; and on the Egadi the Trapani whites, **Grillo** and **Catarratto**.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Trapani and landing at Favignana
We arrived in **Trapani** (you land at Birgi, or at Palermo with a transfer) and took the hydrofoil to **Favignana**, twenty minutes.

**Favignana is shaped like a butterfly**: two wings separated by an isthmus on which the town sits, above which rises **Monte Santa Caterina** with the fort on top. The island is made of **calcarenite** (what they call **tufo** here) and was for centuries an **open-air quarry**: the building stones of half of western Sicily and part of Tunisia came out of here.

The result is that **the island is riddled with holes**. There are former quarries dug vertically into the rock, some twenty meters deep, with walls cut like a blade and the tool marks still visible. Many have become **underground gardens**, sheltered from wind and sun, where citrus and plants grow that wouldn't take root in the open. Walking inside them is the island's most particular experience and it's like nothing else.

We spent the first evening in town, and **tuna**.

### Day 2 — Favignana by bicycle
We did it the way everyone does, because it's the right way: **bicycle**, and a full loop.

**The eastern wing** has the most famous coves. **Cala Rossa** is a stretch of cut white rock with turquoise water below; the name comes from the naval Battle of the Egadi Islands in 241 BC, in which the Romans annihilated the Carthaginian fleet, ending the First Punic War, and the sea, tradition says, turned red. Then **Bue Marino**, a former quarry that drops straight into the water, and **Cala Azzurra** and **Lido Burrone**, the only sandy ones.

**Practical warning:** **almost no cove on Favignana is sandy.** You go down onto cut rock, often with a small jump into the water, and **rubber shoes are necessary**. Anyone looking for the classic beach is thrown.

**The western wing** is wilder and higher: **Cala Rotonda**, **Cala Grande**, the **Faraglione**, with the wind and almost nobody.

In town there's the **former Florio plant**, the great nineteenth-century tuna cannery turned museum: the tanks, the machinery, the processing rooms and the story of the **mattanza**. It was the system of fixed nets with successive chambers in which the tuna were driven to the "chamber of death," a practice of Arab origin that lasted centuries and is **no longer practiced today**. The museum tells it without rhetoric, and the visit is worth it.

### Day 3 — Levanzo and the Grotta del Genovese
A short crossing and we arrived at **Levanzo**, the smallest of the Egadi: **a single little village of white houses**, one road, and the rest is rock and scrub.

You come for the **Grotta del Genovese**, one of the most important prehistoric art sites in Italy. Inside are **Upper Paleolithic rock engravings**, figures of animals, deer and bovids, traced on a wall with surprising realism, and **black and red Neolithic paintings**, more schematic, with human figures and tuna. **The two phases are thousands of years apart in the same cave**, and seeing them side by side shows an entire change in mentality.

**You can only get there with a guide**, by reservation: by sea or by off-road vehicle along a track, depending on conditions. **It's not a visit to improvise on the spot**, and in low season there are few departures.

We toured the rest of Levanzo on foot. There's **Cala Minnola** with its pine grove and, on the seabed a few meters deep, the remains of a **Roman wreck with the amphorae still in position**, which can be visited by snorkeling with a guide.

### Day 4 — Marettimo
It is the farthest, the highest and the most different. **Marettimo** is an hour by hydrofoil from Trapani, it's mountainous, covered with scrub and pines, and **has no cars**: only the village, the port and the trails.

It's the island for walking. The routes climb from the village up to **Pizzo Falcone** (686 meters) and cross the ridge, with a view over the whole archipelago and, on clear days, of the African coast. Halfway along are the **Roman Houses**, a complex with a small Byzantine church built against Roman-era structures, in the middle of nowhere.

From the sea we did the **tour of the caves** (Grotta del Cammello, Grotta del Presepe, Grotta Perciata): there are dozens, some enormous, and inside the water takes on different colors depending on the hour. Then there's the **Castle of Punta Troia**, on an isolated spur, a former Bourbon prison known for the legendary harshness of its conditions of detention, reached by a steep climb.

Marettimo has very few beds and almost no evening services: it's the right place for those who seek this, and the wrong one for everyone else.

In the evening we went back to Trapani.

### Day 5 — Transfer to Pantelleria and first tour
For **Pantelleria** there's the flight or the ferry; on arrival you pick up the vehicle you booked.

The first impact is stark: **the island is black.** It's a volcano (the last documented eruption was underwater and dates to 1891): the rock is dark lava, the walls are dry-stone lava rock, the ground is black. **There are no sandy beaches**, practically anywhere: you enter the water from cliffs, platforms and slides of rock.

The first loop helped us get oriented: the west coast with **Scauri** and **Nikà**, the **Balata dei Turchi** on the south side, a slab of rock plunging down along a wild stretch of coast, and the **Elephant Arch**, the lava formation that enters the sea in the shape of a trunk and is the symbol of the island.

To sleep, if you can, a **dammuso**: the traditional Pantescan houses, with **lava-stone walls more than a meter thick** and a **white domed roof**, designed to **collect rainwater** and channel it into a cistern. They aren't an architectural whim: they're the answer to an island without springs, where the wind always blows and the sun cracks the ground.

### Day 6 — Specchio di Venere, Benikulà and Montagna Grande
It was the thermal and volcanic day.

**Lake Specchio di Venere** is a basin in a caldera, with **high-temperature water in the spring zones** and **white thermal mud** on the shores: people smear it on, let it dry in the sun and then rinse off in the lake. The water changes color with the light and the depth, from green to turquoise.

Then we went to the **natural sauna of Benikulà**, a cave on the flank of Montagna Grande, reached by a short trail, where **hot steam comes out of the cracks in the rock**. You sit on a stone bench and in a few minutes you're in an open-air sauna, with a view of the valley through the opening. **It's open and free**, and you stay a few minutes at a time.

In the afternoon we climbed **Montagna Grande** (836 meters), the highest point, with the pine forest and a view that on clear days reaches the **Tunisian coast, about seventy kilometers away**: less than the distance from here to Sicily. Along the road are the **favare**, fumaroles where steam comes straight out of the ground.

### Day 7 — Vineyards, capers and Passito
This is the day that explains Pantelleria better than any panorama.

Here the vine is grown with a technique called **alberello pantesco**: the plant is kept very low and placed **inside a basin dug into the ground**, so that the wind, which here blows almost constantly, passes over it without tearing it up, and the basin collects the night's moisture. **This agricultural practice was recognized by UNESCO as Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2014**, the first case in the world for an agricultural technique.

Alongside are the **Pantescan gardens**: **circular dry-stone lava walls, several meters high, built around a single citrus tree** to protect it from the wind and create a humid microclimate. It's an enormous amount of work for one plant, and that's exactly the point: they tell you what it costs to live here.

The grape is **Zibibbo** (Muscat of Alexandria), which is **dried in the sun on racks** and then vinified: out comes **Passito di Pantelleria**, amber, dense, with notes of dried apricot and honey. The **wineries are visited by appointment** and the visit is an important part of the trip.

The other crop is **capers**, which here have the **IGP**: they're picked by hand before the flower opens, preserved under sea salt and have an aroma that has nothing to do with the industrial ones.

In the late afternoon we swam in the coves. At **Gadir** **thermal springs gush into natural pools at the water's edge**; **Cala Levante** and **Cala Tramontana** are used in turn depending on which way the wind is blowing, and it's normal here to choose the cove according to the wind direction of the day.

### Day 8 — Pantelleria from the sea
We circled the island by boat, the only way to see the coast that otherwise can't be reached: the caves, the lava flows running down into the water, the **underwater thermal waters** where you swim in zones that suddenly turn hot, and **the south coast** with its cliffs.

Alternatively, for those who prefer walking, there are the inland trails among the terraced vineyards and the abandoned dammusi, and **Cala Cinque Denti** on the northeast side, with its pointed lava rocks.

We closed our last Pantescan evening with the **ravioli amari** and the Passito.

### Day 9 — Transit day
This is the honest day of the itinerary: **you go back to Sicily and set off toward Lampedusa.** A flight to **Palermo** or **Catania** and a connection to Lampedusa or, with less fortunate schedules, a night in transit in Sicily.

**Plan it with a margin.** A wind cancellation at Pantelleria breaks the connection, and flights to Lampedusa are not frequent. **Don't buy non-changeable tickets just a few hours apart.**

Anyone with a long layover in Palermo uses it well: Monreale is twenty minutes from the airport in the direction of the city.

Then arrival in **Lampedusa** and settling in.

### Day 10 — Lampedusa and the Rabbit Beach
**Lampedusa is not geologically Europe**: it sits on the **African continental shelf**, separated from Sicily by a deep channel, and it is limestone and white like the Tunisian coast facing it, a little over a hundred kilometers away. The vegetation, the light and the color of the water are African, not Sicilian.

You come for the **Spiaggia dei Conigli** (Rabbit Beach): a closed bay with the **Isola dei Conigli** in front, connected by a tongue of sand and a very shallow seabed that, depending on the tide, you cross on foot. The water is so clear it's hard to judge the depth.

**It is inside a nature reserve and access is regulated**: **capped numbers or time slots**, **bans on what you can bring onto the beach**, and **closure of the islet and part of the shore during the nesting season of the *Caretta caretta***, which lays its eggs here (it is one of the very few nesting sites in Italy). **The rules change every year: it's worth checking them before going.** It's not a quibble: the beach is the way it is precisely because it's protected like this.

The other coves: **Cala Pulcino**, reached on foot by a trail in a gully; **Cala Creta** and **Cala Galera**; and **Mare Morto**, sheltered, on the harbor side.

In the late afternoon we went to the **Porta d'Europa** (Gateway to Europe), a ceramic and stone monument overlooking the sea on the south of the island, made in 2008 in memory of those who died crossing this stretch of sea. For decades Lampedusa has been the landing point of the central Mediterranean migration routes, and this is as much a part of its identity as the little coves. Going there, staying five minutes and looking in the direction the boats come from is the minimum so as not to pretend nothing is happening.

### Day 11 — By boat, or Linosa
For the last full day there are two options.

**Option A — The boat tour of the island.** It's the right way to see Lampedusa: the southern coast has the caves and inlets, and above all there's the **Tabaccara**, a bay with a brilliant white seabed where **the boats seem suspended in midair**, because the water is so clear you can see the hull's shadow on the bottom several meters down. **At the Tabaccara you can't land and anchoring is regulated**: you look and you swim.

**Option B — Linosa.** An hour's crossing to the north and you change island entirely: **Linosa is volcanic and black**, where Lampedusa is limestone and white. It has about five hundred inhabitants, **houses painted in full colors with white trim**, three volcanic cones to climb on foot and **Pozzolana di Ponente**, a red-sand beach that is **also a turtle nesting site** and therefore regulated. There are no comfortable beaches, there's almost nothing to do, and those who go on purpose know it.

### Day 12 — Departure
The last morning, then the flight. **With a margin**, because around here the wind has the last word.

## Short versions
- **Egadi only (4-5 days):** the easiest, the cheapest and the best for a first time. Base in Favignana, Levanzo and Marettimo as day trips. You arrive from Trapani and leave from Trapani.
- **Pantelleria only (5-6 days):** the island that rewards those who stay the most. **It's not a classic seaside destination**: you go for the volcanic landscape, the dammusi, the wine and the silence. Anyone looking for a beach should choose something else.
- **Lampedusa only (5 days):** the most beautiful water in Italy, no argument, and not much else. It's fine to say it that way.
- **Egadi plus Pantelleria (8-9 days):** the most logical combination, because both are reached from **Trapani** and the transfer is straightforward.
- **What not to do:** try to squeeze Pantelleria and Lampedusa into a few days counting on tight connections. The wind decides, and it's best to have anticipated that.

## What to see / what to do
- **The gardens in the former tufo quarries** on Favignana.
- **Favignana by bicycle**, with rubber shoes for the rock coves.
- **The former Florio plant** and the story of the mattanza.
- **The Grotta del Genovese** on Levanzo, with the Paleolithic engravings and Neolithic paintings.
- **The trails of Marettimo** up to Pizzo Falcone.
- **The natural sauna of Benikulà**, free, inside the rock.
- **The mud of the Specchio di Venere.**
- **The bush-trained vines in their basins**, UNESCO intangible heritage, and the Pantescan gardens.
- **A Passito winery by appointment.**
- **The Spiaggia dei Conigli**, respecting the reserve's rules.
- **The Tabaccara from the boat**, with the boats that seem suspended.
- **The Porta d'Europa**, five minutes, looking south.

## Possible extensions
- **Western Sicily (+5-7 days):** Trapani is the gateway to the Egadi, and from there Erice, Segesta, the salt pans, Mozia and Palermo open up. It's the most natural add-on.
- **Aeolian Islands (+7-8 days):** the other Sicilian archipelago, something else entirely, best by boat.
- **All of Sicily (+17 days):** the loop around the island, with a dedicated itinerary.
- **Tunisia:** geographically an hour's flight away, historically inside the same story: Carthage, the Phoenicians, the capers, the couscous. There are no direct connections from the islands, but the cultural link is there.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **Favignana in town**, to be within walking distance of the port and the bike rentals. In high season the beds run out early: it's the most requested of the three islands.
- **Marettimo and Levanzo** have very few accommodations, almost all small: **book months ahead** or do them as a day trip.
- **Pantelleria in a dammuso**, which isn't folklore but the housing solution that works on this island: cool in summer, sheltered from the wind, with the cistern. The most convenient areas are the southeast side and the Scauri coast.
- **Lampedusa** has many accommodations concentrated in town and along the road to the coves: outside town you still need a scooter or car.
- **Note:** on Pantelleria and Lampedusa **prices go up enormously in August** and beds are limited anyway, because they're small islands with little lodging capacity.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **Egadi:** **bluefin tuna** in every part, and bottarga.
- **Pantelleria:** **ravioli amari** with ricotta and mint, **insalata pantesca**, raw **pesto pantesco**, the **IGP capers** and the **Passito** at the end of the meal.
- **Lampedusa:** fish from the Strait of Sicily, **red prawns**, amberjack and groupers.
- **To drink:** Passito and Moscato di Pantelleria, Trapani's Grillo and Catarratto, and Sicilian whites in general.
- **General rule:** on all three islands **almost everything arrives by sea**, and it shows in the prices. Eating what the island really produces (tuna on the Egadi, garden produce and capers on Pantelleria, fish on Lampedusa) is also the way to spend better.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Don't schedule tight connections.** The wind cancels ferries and flights, and on these islands it happens often.
- **Book a car or scooter on Pantelleria ahead of time**: there are few vehicles and they run out.
- **Rubber shoes on the Egadi and Pantelleria**: almost no cove is sandy.
- **Check the reserve's rules**, which change every season, before going to the Spiaggia dei Conigli.
- **Book the Grotta del Genovese in advance**: if you show up on the spot you won't get to visit.
- **On Pantelleria choose the cove according to the wind of the day**, as the Pantescans do.
- **Book the Passito wineries**, which aren't shops.
- **Use water sparingly**: it comes from desalination plants and cisterns.
- **Don't expect sandy beaches on Pantelleria.** There aren't any, and it's right to know that before booking.
- **Don't treat Lampedusa as just a beach.** It's something else too, and five minutes at the Porta d'Europa costs little.

## Chiusura personale
What stays with me about these islands is that, going from north to south, Europe gradually ends and nobody says so.

In Favignana you're still in Sicily, with the tuna cannery, the little baroque and the tufo of the churches of Palermo. In Pantelleria, a hundred kilometers further on, you sleep in a domed house with walls a meter thick designed to collect rainwater, you eat a vegetable couscous, you grow the vine in a hole because the wind would tear it up, and on clear days you can see Africa. In Lampedusa you are **on the African continental shelf**, literally: geologically, that island was never Europe. It got there only by way of political maps.

And then you go down to the Porta d'Europa, which looks south toward the open sea from which the boats arrive, and you realize that the border everyone talks about constantly isn't a line: it's a stretch of water that some people cross by plane in an hour, with a reserved seat, and others don't.
