# Turin in 2 Days: Egypt, the Mole and the Arcades

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 work in progress (itinerary and practical information verified; the first-person passages are written as a draft to reread and personalize)
**Periodo del viaggio:** **April-June and September-November**; great in winter too
**Durata:** 2 days, 1 night — extendable to 3 with the Venaria or the Langhe
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independent, on foot and by metro
**Categoria:** Europa · Italia · Torino · musei · architettura · gastronomia

---

## Apertura personale
Turin is the great Italian city almost nobody puts on the list, and it remains the most underrated in the country by a wide margin. It has the second-largest Egyptian collection in the world, a cinema museum inside a building that looks like nothing else, eighteen kilometers of arcades you can walk under in any weather, the Alps at the end of the streets and a cuisine that has no rival in northern Italy.

The reason it isn't visited is probably that for a century it was perceived as an industrial city, and the reputation stuck after the reason was gone. Anyone arriving today finds an orderly Baroque capital, laid out on a grid, with big squares and historic cafés, and it takes half a day to realize they had the wrong expectations.

Two days are enough for the center. The risk is wanting three.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** **spring and autumn**, and also **Turin works beautifully in winter**: the arcades let you walk for kilometers in the rain, the museums are indoors, and clear January days show **the whole Alpine arc at the end of the streets**, something that in summer, because of the haze, almost never happens. July and August are hot and the city empties.
- **Bookings:**
  - **Egyptian Museum:** a time-slot reservation is strongly recommended, essential on weekends and long weekends.
  - **Mole Antonelliana elevator:** separate ticket or combined with the Cinema Museum, **with slots**, and it sells out on clear days.
- **The Shroud:** **it is not on display.** It is kept in the Cathedral, in a sealed case, and shown to the public only in **extraordinary expositions decided by the Holy See**, many years apart. Anyone going to Turin thinking they'll see it ends up disappointed: what you visit is Guarini's **Chapel of the Shroud** (a Baroque masterpiece, damaged by the fire of 1997 and reopened after a twenty-year restoration) and the **Museum of the Shroud**, which is something else and is located elsewhere.
- **Torino+Piemonte Card:** a day pass that includes museums and transport. **It pays off from three or four museums**, and that's realistic to reach here, where museums are the main reason to come. Formulas and durations change: check those in force before buying.
- **Transport:** **a single automatic metro line**, but well centered, plus trams and buses. The center is explored on foot and **under the arcades**: there are about **eighteen kilometers of arcades**, a good part of them connected without interruption, a system designed so the Savoy court could move around without getting wet. It still works.
- **Airport:** **Caselle**, connected to the center by train and bus. **Porta Nuova** and **Porta Susa** put Turin about an hour from Milan by high-speed rail.
- **Food:** Turin is a city where you eat very well and drink even better. **Agnolotti del plin**, pinched by hand and also served **in a napkin, undressed**, which is the traditional way; **vitello tonnato** in the historic version, with the sauce based on anchovies and capers; **tajarin** with lots of egg yolks; **bagna cauda** in winter, which is a collective rite more than a dish; **bollito misto** with its sauces. **Grissini were born here.**
- **The sweet and liquid chapter:** the **bicerin** is the Turin drink: coffee, chocolate and milk cream **in layers that are not to be mixed**, served in a glass. The **gianduiotto** was born of Napoleon's Continental blockade, when cocoa was scarce and Turin's chocolatiers stretched it with **Langhe hazelnuts**: a shortage produced something better than the original. **Vermouth** was invented in Turin at the end of the eighteenth century, and all of Italian aperitivo culture descends from there; here it's done seriously, with spreads laid out in the historic cafés.
- **Wine:** you are **an hour from the Langhe**: Barolo, Barbaresco, Barbera, Nebbiolo, Moscato. Wineries are visited **by appointment**.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Egyptian Museum, Squares and Cafés
In the morning we went to the **Egyptian Museum**, and gave it time: **it is the most important Egyptian collection in the world after Cairo's** and **the oldest museum in the world entirely devoted to Egyptian civilization**, founded in 1824 on the core collected by consul Drovetti. The entire building is dedicated to Egypt.

The pieces not to miss: the **tomb of Kha and Merit**, found **intact** in 1906, with the complete funerary goods (furniture, food, clothes, everyday objects) of a couple from the 18th dynasty: the most complete domestic portrait we have of ancient Egypt; the **Temple of Ellesiya**, saved from the waters of the Aswan dam and donated to Italy, rebuilt inside the museum; the **Papyrus of the Gold Mines**, considered the oldest known map based on topography; and the **Gallery of the Kings**, staged with lights and mirrors by a film set designer, openly theatrical, and it works wonderfully.

We spent the afternoon in the Baroque center, all on foot and almost all under the arcades. In **Piazza Castello** is **Palazzo Madama**, which has a Juvarra facade of an eighteenth-century palace on one side and **a medieval castle with Roman towers built in** on the other: walking around the building you pass from one era to another. Then the **Royal Museums**, which bring together the **Royal Palace**, the **Galleria Sabauda**, the **Royal Armory** (one of the most important collections of arms and armor in Europe) and Guarini's **Chapel of the Shroud**, with a dome of superimposed hexagonal structures that rises like a stone lattice and should be looked at from below, in silence.

We passed through **Piazza San Carlo**, the city's drawing room, with its two twin churches and arcades on both sides, and through the **Galleria Subalpina**. Here you go into a historic café and have a **bicerin**, without stirring it.

Late in the afternoon we reached the **Roman Quarter**, the oldest part of the city, on a Roman orthogonal grid. There are the narrow streets, the **Porta Palatina** (a 1st-century Roman gate with its towers still standing) and the **Porta Palazzo** market, **the largest open-air market in Europe**, which in the morning is a spectacle.

We had aperitivo in the Quadrilatero, the area where people go out in the evening.

### Day 2 — The Mole, the River and the Hill
In the morning we went to the **Mole Antonelliana**, the symbol of the city, built for quite another purpose: **it was begun in 1863 as a synagogue** for Turin's Jewish community, newly emancipated, and the architect Antonelli let it grow well beyond the plan and the budget until the community abandoned it and ceded it to the city. It is **one of the tallest masonry buildings ever built**.

Inside is the **National Cinema Museum**, arranged in a spiral along a ramp that climbs around the central hall, with the themed rooms opening off to the sides and the **red reclining chairs in the middle** where you lie back to watch the screens projected on the high walls. It's one of the best-designed museums in Italy and appeals even to those who know nothing about cinema.

Then **the panoramic elevator**: a **glass cabin that rises through the void of the temple hall**, with no structure around it, up to the little temple at the top. Anyone with vertigo feels it. From above, on a clear day, you see **the whole grid city and the Alpine arc behind**, with Monviso recognizable.

Afternoon, two possibilities.

**Option A: the river and the hill.** You go down to the **Po**, cross at **Piazza Vittorio Veneto** (one of the largest arcaded squares in Europe) and climb to **Monte dei Cappuccini**, ten minutes on foot, from which you get **the classic view of Turin with the Mole and the Alps behind**. Then the **Valentino Park** along the river, with the **Medieval Village**, a nineteenth-century reconstruction made with the rigor of a philological study: an openly declared and very pleasant fake. Anyone with time left takes the **rack funicular** to **Superga**: Juvarra's basilica on the hill, with the **Savoy royal tombs** in the crypt and, at the back, the **Grande Torino memorial**, at the exact spot where the plane carrying the team home crashed into the embankment in 1949. It's still a place of pilgrimage today.

**Option B: industrial twentieth century.** The **National Automobile Museum** tells the story of the car as social history and not as a gallery of models, and is among the best in the world of its kind. Then the **Lingotto**: the former factory with **the test track on the roof**, where newly assembled cars came off the line and went up to circle at altitude, and the **Pinacoteca Agnelli** in the structure suspended on the roof: a small, very high-quality collection, with the track running around it that you can walk.

We closed the last evening with a proper Turin aperitivo and a Piedmontese dinner.

## If You Have a Third Day
- **Reggia di Venaria Reale:** outside the city, reachable by bus or shuttle. It's an enormous complex, restored after decades of abandonment as a barracks, with Juvarra's **Grand Gallery** (a nave of light eighty meters long) and the gardens. **It takes a full half day**, and can't be done well in a rush.
- **Langhe:** an hour by car and you're at **Barolo, Barbaresco, La Morra, Alba**. Wineries **by appointment**, and **from October to December the Alba white truffle season**, which pushes up all prices in the area. It's the natural link with the itinerary on the great wine routes.
- **Sacra di San Michele:** the abbey perched on Mount Pirchiriano at the mouth of the Susa Valley, with the **Staircase of the Dead** and the church built on the summit of the rock. It's the symbol monument of Piedmont and inspired the setting of *The Name of the Rose*.

## What to see / what to do
- **The Egyptian Museum**, with the intact tomb of Kha and Merit.
- **Guarini's Chapel of the Shroud**, looked at from below.
- **Palazzo Madama**, which is Baroque on one side and medieval on the other.
- **The Cinema Museum in the Mole**, lying back in the red chairs.
- **The glass elevator** that rises through the void of the Mole.
- **The Porta Palazzo market** in the morning.
- **Monte dei Cappuccini** for the view with the Alps.
- **Superga**, with the royal tombs and the Grande Torino memorial.
- **The track on the Lingotto roof.**
- **A bicerin in a historic café**, without stirring it.

## Possible extensions
- **Langhe and Roero (+2 days):** Barolo, Barbaresco, Alba and the white truffle in autumn.
- **Susa Valley and Sacra di San Michele (+1 day).**
- **Milan (+2 days):** an hour by high-speed rail, and it's the most instructive contrast northern Italy offers.
- **Snow (+3-4 days):** the Via Lattea ski areas and the Olympic valleys are a little over an hour away, and it's the link with the section dedicated to skiing.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **Center, between Piazza Castello and Piazza San Carlo**, to be under the arcades and within walking distance of everything.
- **Roman Quarter** for the nightlife, with a bit more noise.
- **San Salvario**, behind Porta Nuova, for the liveliest neighborhood and the best prices.
- **Borgo Po**, across the river, for the quietest, most residential part.
- Turin has, on average, **much lower prices than the other major Italian art cities** for the same category: it's one of the reasons it's worth it.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **Agnolotti del plin**, preferably also in the **napkin** version.
- **Vitello tonnato** in the historic recipe with anchovies and capers.
- **Bagna cauda** in winter, knowing it's a commitment.
- **Bollito misto** with the sauces.
- **The bicerin** and the **gianduiotti**, which were born here.
- **Aperitivo with vermouth**, in the city that invented it.
- **To drink:** Barolo, Barbaresco, Barbera d'Asti, Nebbiolo, and Moscato d'Asti at the end of the meal.
- **Note:** Turin has a tradition of **historic cafés** that are real places and not reconstructions, and going in is part of the visit even just for a coffee at the counter.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Book the Egyptian Museum** and give it at least three hours: it's the main reason for the trip and can't be dispatched in an hour.
- **Don't expect to see the Shroud**, which is exposed only in very rare expositions.
- **Book the Mole elevator slot**, which sells out on clear days.
- **Make use of the arcades** instead of avoiding the rain: the city is designed for it.
- **Go to Porta Palazzo in the morning**, which is when the market exists.
- **Don't underestimate winter**, which in Turin is the season with the best view.
- **Book the Langhe wineries**, if you go down, and don't show up without an appointment.
- **Check the dates of expositions and closures** of the Royal Museums, which have their own calendars.

## Chiusura personale
What stays with you from Turin is a question of disproportion. You enter a museum that from the outside is a street palace like many others, in a city that in the collective imagination is associated with factories, and inside is Egypt: not a section, not a collection of nineteenth-century curiosities, but the second Egyptian collection in the world, with an intact tomb containing the bread two people left there thirty-five hundred years ago.

Then you go out, walk under an arcade for twenty minutes without ever seeing the sky, arrive in a Baroque square and at the end of the street, if the day is right, there are the Alps with snow. Turin is all like that: it announces nothing, doesn't put itself in the window, and every time you turn a corner there's something it makes no sense to find there. It's the reason people who go for two days come back.
