# Switzerland by van: the Bernese Alps from Interlaken to the Matterhorn

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 in progress (trip made by Rick; itinerary and practical information checked)
**Periodo del viaggio:**
**Durata:** modular, from a long weekend of 3-4 days around Interlaken and the Jungfrau Region to a week that reaches Zermatt. Two ready-made proposals below.
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independently, in a camper or equipped van
**Categoria:** Europa · Svizzera · Alpi Bernesi · van life · roadtrip · montagna

## Apertura personale
Switzerland is not a country you visit to save money, and it is better to say so before the first line of the itinerary: it is consistently among the most expensive nations in Europe, and anyone arriving with Italian or French prices in mind finds a daily budget double or triple what they expected. That's why the costs come first below, even before the zones.

That said, the Interlaken-Jungfrau region, in the Bernese Alps, remains one of the densest stretches of mountains in Europe: two turquoise lakes, a valley of seventy-two waterfalls closed in by vertical walls, a train that climbs to 3,454 meters inside the mountain, and, for those with a week, a road of Alpine passes that reaches the foot of the Matterhorn. It is also a country made for the van: impeccable roads, short distances, scenery that changes every hour of driving. The van, however, has stricter rules in Switzerland than in the neighboring Alpine countries: it is the most common source of fines and misunderstandings for those arriving from Italy or France used to a margin of tolerance that doesn't exist here.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** June to September, with July-August as the most reliable window for the Alpine passes
- **From the airport to the centre:** if you are not driving a van up from Italy, you usually land at Zurich (ZRH), where campervans can also be rented · SBB train from the station under the terminal to Interlaken Ost, about 2 hours with a change in Bern, every half hour, full 2nd-class fare about CHF 75-80, less with Supersaver fares in the SBB app · from Geneva (GVA) the train to Interlaken takes about 3 hours, and at baggage claim you can pick up a free ticket for Geneva public transport valid 80 minutes · taxis are very expensive and unnecessary · the most common mistake is buying the ticket at the last minute at full price.

### What it really costs: the part to read first
Switzerland is not in the European Union and has not adopted the euro: you pay in Swiss francs, and the exchange rate with the euro is historically unfavorable, which inflates every price expressed in euros. The items that weigh most on a van trip:

- **Motorway vignette:** a mandatory annual sticker to drive on Swiss motorways and semi-motorways, **about 40 CHF**, valid from December 1 of the previous year to January 31 of the following year: a single purchase easily covers a summer trip. There are no daily or weekly vignettes, you pay for the whole year even for a week's vacation. Since 2025 there is also a digital **e-vignette**, at the same price, tied to the license plate instead of the windshield. Driving without one earns a fine of **about 200 CHF** and you still have to buy the vignette (indicative figure, subject to periodic revision).
- **Fuel:** among the most expensive in Europe. Gasoline roughly **2.00-2.15 CHF/liter**, diesel **2.25-2.45 CHF/liter** (indicative figures, subject to periodic revision and sharp swings). With a van that uses more than a car, and a week of transfers between zones, it is a serious budget item.
- **Mountain trains and cable cars:** here Switzerland has no rival. The train to the Jungfraujoch (Top of Europe) costs **roughly 224-261 CHF round trip from Interlaken Ost** depending on the season (indicative figure, subject to periodic revision): it is probably the single most expensive tourist ticket in the Alps. The cable car to the Schilthorn from Stechelberg costs **about 115 CHF round trip**, the First one in Grindelwald **72-76 CHF round trip**, the cog railway to Schynige Platte **68 CHF round trip**. Every price should be factored in before leaving, not discovered on the spot.
- **Campsites:** a pitch for a van with two people costs roughly **35-50 CHF per night** at campsites in the Interlaken area, with electricity often included or a few francs extra. It isn't cheap, but it still costs less than a hotel, and in low season TCS campsites have "Stop & Go" offers around 25 CHF a night for two people.
- **Parking:** in the centers of tourist towns hourly parking for a van runs around **2 CHF/hour**, with daily rates easily exceeding 15-20 CHF in the busiest areas (for example the large covered car park in Täsch, for those leaving the van outside Zermatt, a town closed to private traffic, costs from **17 CHF per day**).

**In short:** for a week in a van that touches four or five zones with a couple of cable cars or mountain trains, the budget for two people (fuel, campsites, parking, a couple of ascents, groceries) rarely falls below 150-200 CHF a day, not counting the one-off vignette. It is an order of magnitude above an equivalent road trip in Italy or France, and should be budgeted that way, not discovered along the road.

### Opening hours and closures: the second thing to know before leaving
In Switzerland opening hours are much stricter than those Italy and France accustom you to, and it is one of the most common surprises for those arriving in a van thinking they will do their shopping whenever.

- **On Sundays almost everything is closed.** The federal labor law prohibits Sunday opening of shops, with very limited exceptions: outlets in large railway stations (Interlaken included), in airports and in some motorway service areas. A village Coop or Migros is closed on Sundays, period. Those traveling in a van must do the big shop on Saturday, or know in advance where the nearest station with an open outlet is.
- **Even on weekdays hours are tighter than in Italy**: outside the big cities many shops close at 6:30-7:00 p.m., in the smaller villages often with a midday break, and on Monday mornings some open late. Outside a station, at ten at night, it is hard to find anything open.
- **High-altitude Alpine passes close in winter**, and for a van trip the useful window is narrower than it looks on paper. Grimselpass and Sustenpass open roughly between late May and early June and close around late October; the Furkapass follows a similar calendar, with a shuttle train (Furka Autoverlad) as an alternative when the pass is closed to ordinary traffic. Exact dates depend on residual snow and should be checked every season, for example on the TCS website.
- **Cable cars and mountain trains have precise operating seasons**, often shorter than you'd expect: Schynige Platte, for example, opens in mid-June and closes in late October. The Jungfraujoch stays open all year, but with different departure times and frequencies between high and low season. It is worth checking the timetables before planning the day.

### The van in Switzerland: the rules that change most compared with the neighbors
This is the point on which those arriving from Italy, France or Germany go wrong most often, countries where free camping is tolerated with more margin: **in Switzerland free camping is, in substance, prohibited or heavily restricted almost everywhere**, and the fines are real.

- **There is no single federal law**: the matter is regulated at the cantonal and often municipal level, and the rules change when crossing a cantonal border with no obvious sign. In some cantons (for example Obwalden) a one-night bivouac at altitude, above the tree line, is tolerated; in others (Schwyz, for example) it is prohibited without exception.
- **In the Lauterbrunnen valley, one of the central zones of this guide, free camping and overnight stops outside authorized areas are explicitly prohibited**, with fines of up to **200 CHF**; in the Canton of Bern in general penalties for illegal camping can reach **2,000 CHF** in the most serious cases. The rule is enforced, which is why in this guide each zone points out where to sleep legally.
- **The practical difference that matters**: sleeping inside the van in a public car park, without setting out tables, chairs or tents, is generally tolerated for one night in many (not all) of the car parks without signs to the contrary. By contrast **setting up a real outdoor camp (chairs, table, clothes rack)** is prohibited almost everywhere, even where simply spending the night in the van is tolerated, and it is exactly this difference that the local police check.
- **The practical solution is the network of equipped areas**: Switzerland has over 400 campsites and more than 200 **Wohnmobilstellplatz** (motorhome stopover areas, often with services, some free or at a modest price), listed by apps such as Park4Night or on the TCS portal. Planning the night before arriving, instead of looking on the spot, avoids both the fine and the evening lost driving around.
- **Crossing the border with a van from Italy (or from France or Germany)**: Switzerland is in the Schengen area, so there are no systematic passport checks, but it is **not part of the EU customs union**, and the border is a real customs border. Since 2025 the value allowance for goods carried with you has been halved to **150 CHF per person** (600 CHF for a family of four): above this threshold everything must be declared, not just the excess, and VAT applies on the entire value. There are also specific quantity allowances, for example 5 liters of wine or beer per person. A van full of supplies bought in Italy for the whole week can, in theory, exceed these limits: better to know before filling the fridge.

### Reservations and cash
The main cable cars (Jungfraujoch, Schilthorn, First) don't require mandatory reservations outside peak summer days, but **online tickets generally cost less than those bought at the counter**, and on July-August weekends it is still worth booking the time slot in advance. Cards are accepted almost everywhere, including small businesses and car parks, because Switzerland is a country very oriented toward electronic payment. **It is still worth having some cash in Swiss francs** for the less structured motorhome stopover areas and for the vending machines at some mountain car parks, which don't always accept foreign cards.

### Two road trip proposals already put together

**Long weekend: 3-4 days, the heart of the Jungfrau Region.** For those with a bridge holiday or a long weekend who want to see the best of the Interlaken area without going further. Fixed logistics base recommended in Interlaken or at one of the campsites along Lake Brienz. Day 1: arrival, setting up, first tour of the lakes of Thun and Brienz. Day 2: Lauterbrunnen valley, the waterfalls and in the afternoon the ascent to Mürren or the Schilthorn. Day 3: Grindelwald and the First, with the panoramic Cliff Walk. Those staying three days return here and skip the Jungfraujoch, to contain time and budget (with only three full days it is the sharpest cut to make). Those with a fourth day devote it to the star day: the train to the **Jungfraujoch**, leaving early to perhaps use the discounted Good Morning Ticket, and returning in the afternoon. Three zones in the three-day version, four in the four-day one, no Alpine pass: the minimum to get a real idea of the region without the cost and time of a longer trip.

**Full week: 7 days, from the Jungfrau to the Matterhorn.** It adds the road of Alpine passes and an extension to Zermatt, and turns the weekend around Interlaken-Jungfrau into a real crossing of the Swiss Alps. Day 1: arrival in Interlaken, lakes of Thun and Brienz. Day 2: Lauterbrunnen valley and its waterfalls. Day 3: Grindelwald and the First. Day 4: Jungfraujoch, the most expensive and fullest day of the trip. Day 5: Mürren and the Schilthorn, with the optional Mürren-Gimmelwald via ferrata for strong walkers. Day 6: transfer toward the Valais for Grimselpass and Furkapass, **doable only in season, roughly from June to October**, with the Furka rail shuttle as an alternative if the pass is already closed. Day 7: Zermatt, leaving the van in the Täsch car park and taking the train for the last stretch (Zermatt is closed to private traffic), for a day under the Matterhorn before heading home.

## Itinerario per zone

### Interlaken and the lakes of Thun and Brienz
The name says it all: Interlaken sits "between the lakes", on a strip of land separating Lake Thun to the west from Lake Brienz to the east, both a milky turquoise due to suspended glacial sediments. It is the natural logistics hub of the whole region (main railway station, supermarkets, outdoor gear rentals) and also the most practical base for a van, with several campsites within a few kilometers. The town has no major monuments: it serves as a starting point, every morning, toward the surrounding valleys.
*Good to know: a trip on a historic boat on one of the two lakes, with steamboats still in service on both, is probably the most relaxing way to see the Bernese Alps from below, and costs a fraction of any cable car.*

### Lauterbrunnen and the valley of waterfalls
A glacial gorge with vertical walls and **seventy-two waterfalls** counted along its twelve kilometers. The most famous, the Staubbach, which plunges 300 meters almost in the village center, inspired Goethe and, according to local tradition, the valley of Rivendell in Tolkien's "Lord of the Rings". It is also the starting point for Mürren, Wengen and Gimmelwald, three car-free villages reachable only by cable car or cog railway. Inside the mountain, the **Trümmelbachfälle** are a system of ten glacial waterfalls seen from tunnels and elevators carved into the rock: the only place in the world where you can see up close all the meltwater of the Jungfrau massif channeled into a single gorge.
*Good to know: **free camping in this valley is explicitly prohibited**, with fines of up to 200 CHF (see the section on the van above). On the valley floor there are equipped campsites, and that is where you sleep.*

### Grindelwald and the First
At the foot of the Eiger's north face, Grindelwald is the most "postcard" village of the region and the starting point of the cable car to the First. Up there the **Cliff Walk**, a cantilevered metal walkway on the rock face, offers one of the most photographed views of the Bernese Alps without a single demanding step of walking. More serious trails also leave from here toward the Bachalpsee, a small alpine lake that on windless days reflects the peaks of the Eiger, Mönch and Jungfrau.
*Good to know: the Cliff Walk is included in the cable car ticket and can be repeated as many times as you like; extra activities, like the First Flyer zipline, are paid separately, on site.*

### Jungfraujoch, the Top of Europe
The highest railway station in Europe, at 3,454 meters, reached by a cog line dug almost entirely inside the rock of the Eiger and the Mönch between 1896 and 1912, an engineering feat that at the time seemed impossible. At the top there are the Aletsch Glacier, the longest in the Alps, an ice palace carved into the permafrost and, on clear days, a view reaching as far as the Black Forest. It is also, plainly, **the most expensive tourist ticket in this guide**: it should be treated as the star day of the trip, not as just any stop, and booked at the right time, that is with an early morning departure, to have a better chance of clear sky before the afternoon clouds form.
*Good to know: the "Good Morning Ticket", for those leaving before 8 and returning by early afternoon, lowers the price a lot compared with the full fare. It is the only real structural discount on this route for those who don't already have a Swiss rail pass.*

### Mürren and the Schilthorn
Mürren is one of the car-free villages of the Lauterbrunnen valley, reachable only by cable car or cog railway, with a view of the Eiger-Mönch-Jungfrau among the most open in the region precisely because there is no wall in front to block it. Above the village the cable car climbs to the **Schilthorn**, where the panoramic revolving restaurant Piz Gloria, made famous by the James Bond film "On Her Majesty's Secret Service", offers 360 degrees over more than two hundred peaks. From Mürren also starts the **Mürren-Gimmelwald via ferrata**, an equipped route of about 2.2 km that, unusually, goes downhill instead of uphill along the valley walls, to the nearby village of Gimmelwald. The highlight is a 90-meter suspension bridge over the Mürrenbach waterfall.
*Good to know: the via ferrata is open only from June to late October and requires an approved via ferrata kit; those without experience should do it with a guide, or at least after testing their abilities on a simpler route.*

### Grimselpass and Furkapass
The road linking the Bernese Alps to the Valais crosses two of the most scenic passes in Switzerland: the **Grimselpass** (2,164 m), among emerald-colored reservoirs set in gray rock, and the **Furkapass** (2,429 m), made famous by the Aston Martin chase in "Goldfinger" and overlooking the terminal tongue of the Rhône Glacier. It is the pure road trip day of this guide (few long stops, lots of scenic driving) and the stretch where the van performs at its best, as a means of crossing the Alps rather than just visiting them.
*Good to know: **both passes close in winter**, roughly from late October to late May-early June, depending on residual snow. For the Furka, when the pass is closed, there is a shuttle train (Furka Autoverlad) that loads cars through the base tunnel: at the edges of the season it is worth checking its timetables.*

### Zermatt and the Matterhorn
The southernmost point of this guide, and the only one where the van doesn't reach the center: Zermatt has been **closed to private traffic** since 1961, and those arriving by car or camper leave the vehicle in the large covered car park in Täsch, five kilometers earlier, and take a shuttle train that leaves every twenty minutes. The reward is the Matterhorn, 4,478 meters of an almost perfect rock pyramid, visible from the village center and from very many viewpoints reachable by cable car, Gornergrat above all, with the highest open-air cog railway in Europe.
*Good to know: compared with the rest of the trip it is a heavy addition in terms of driving (over two hours from Interlaken even going via Grimsel-Furka), which is why in this guide it appears only in the seven-day proposal.*

## Hikes: for those who walk and for those who just want the view

As in any mountain guide, elevation gains and difficulties below are **indicative**: weather, residual snow and personal fitness change everything, especially above 2,000 meters, where the weather can worsen in half an hour.

**For strong walkers:**
- **Mürren-Gimmelwald via ferrata**: 2-3 hours, about 400 meters of descent, difficulty K3-K4 (medium-high) on the Hüsler scale, with a 90-meter suspension bridge as the highlight. Open from June to October, approved via ferrata kit mandatory.
- **Bachalpsee trail from First**: 2-3 hours round trip, moderate elevation gain and an already high starting altitude thanks to the cable car. It is one of the easiest ways to reach a true high-mountain landscape without serious climbing.
- **Schynige Platte-First traverse**: several hours, for fit walkers, along the panoramic ridge overlooking the Eiger-Mönch-Jungfrau chain the whole way.

**For those who want the view without suffering:**
- **First Cliff Walk**: no elevation gain on foot, the cable car does it all, then the cantilevered walkway on the rock face.
- **Boat trip on the lakes of Thun and Brienz**: no walking, the view of the Bernese Alps from below, with stops in lakeside villages like Iseltwald.
- **Jungfraujoch**: the snow and the Aletsch Glacier at 3,454 meters while staying mostly indoors, with short stretches outdoors on the panoramic terraces.
- **Schilthorn by cable car from Mürren**: minimal elevation gain on foot, 360-degree view from the revolving restaurant Piz Gloria.

**The first time on a via ferrata, if you have no experience, it is best done with a guide.** In Switzerland mountain rescue is paid for, and it is an expense nobody wants to find themselves facing.

## Where to sleep
Rule number one is the one explained in the van section: **free camping is prohibited or heavily restricted almost everywhere**, and in Lauterbrunnen in particular the fines are real. In the Interlaken area the network of equipped campsites is dense, on the two lakes, a few minutes from the center, with pitches at about 35-50 CHF a night and electricity often included. Alternatively, the motorhome stopover areas (**Wohnmobilstellplatz**) listed on Park4Night and on the TCS portal often offer the essential services at a lower price; they should be identified and checked before leaving, not looked for on the spot in the evening. For those who want to sleep at altitude at least one night, the huts of the Bernese Alps work like those of the Italian Alps (half board, booking in advance in high season), but they are not designed for those traveling by van: you arrive on foot and the vehicle stays in the valley.

## Where to eat
The cuisine of the Bernese Alps is Swiss-German mountain food: **rösti** (the grated, browned potatoes that go with almost every dish), **fondue** and **raclette** with the local alpine cheeses, **Älplermagronen** (baked pasta with potatoes, cheese and caramelized onions, the hut dish par excellence) and the **Berner Platte**, a platter of smoked meats and cold cuts with sauerkraut. Like everything else, eating out costs more than in Italy or France: a simple meal in a mid-range restaurant rarely falls below 25-30 CHF per person. For those who cook in the van, shopping at the Coop and Migros supermarkets remains the cheapest option, **done on Saturday, not Sunday**, for the reason explained above.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Buy the motorway vignette before entering the motorway**, or expect a fine of about 200 CHF on top of the cost of the vignette itself.
- **Do the big shop on Saturday**: on Sundays almost all shops are closed, except outlets in large stations.
- **Don't set up a table and chairs outside the van outside an authorized area**, even where simply spending the night inside the vehicle is tolerated: it is the difference the local police check, especially in Lauterbrunnen.
- **Book Jungfraujoch and Schilthorn tickets online on high-season weekends**, and consider the Good Morning Ticket for the Jungfraujoch if you leave early.
- **Check that Grimselpass and Furkapass are open before scheduling the transfer day**, especially in early June or late October, at the edges of the season.
- **Carry cash in Swiss francs** for the less structured stopover areas and for some mountain car parks.
- **Don't exceed the customs allowance of 150 CHF per person** when returning to Italy with purchases made in Switzerland, or expect VAT on the entire value, not just on the excess.
- **Leave the van in Täsch, without trying to enter Zermatt**: the town has been closed to private traffic since 1961.
- **Work out the daily budget before leaving, not on the spot**: between vignette, fuel, campsites and even just a couple of ascents, Switzerland always costs more than it seems looking at the prices of neighboring countries.
