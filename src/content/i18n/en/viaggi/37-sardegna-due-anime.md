# Sardinia, the Two Souls: Two Weeks from the Costa Smeralda to the Sulcis

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 work in progress (itinerary and practical information verified; the first-person passages are written as a draft to reread and personalize)
**Periodo del viaggio:**
**Durata:** 14 days, 13 nights — by car, crossing the island on the diagonal
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independent, with a rental car
**Categoria:** Europa · Italia · Sardegna · mare · archeologia · trekking

---

## Apertura personale
The Sardinia everyone knows is a forty-kilometer strip of north-eastern coast, built from the 1960s onward according to a precise urban plan, with a coherent look and prices that in August bear no relation to the rest of Italy. It is beautiful, it is artificial in the literal sense of the word, and it is **less than two percent of the island**.

The other Sardinia begins twenty kilometers further on and is one of the least touristy regions of the western Mediterranean: limestone mountains with canyons five hundred meters deep, an inland plateau where they speak a language that is not an Italian dialect, seven thousand nuraghi, most of which have never even been excavated, abandoned mines hanging over the sea, and sand dunes as tall as apartment blocks.

This itinerary puts both of them in a row, in the right order: **the north-east first**, which is what you come for, **then the diagonal toward the south-west**, which is what you come back for. Anyone who does only the first part has seen a beautiful resort. Anyone who does only the second has missed a sea that has no equal in Europe.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** **late May, June and September.** The water is already (or still) good, the beaches are accessible without heavy quotas, and prices are half. **August is to be avoided** in every respect: prices, ferries, capped-entry beaches already full by nine in the morning, roads. **April, May and October** are excellent for the interior, the nuraghi and the canyons, with a cool sea.
- **Getting there:** **flights** to **Olbia** (north-east), **Cagliari** (south) and **Alghero** (north-west). For this itinerary, **open in Olbia and close in Cagliari** with a one-way rental. The alternative is the **ferries** from Civitavecchia, Livorno, Genoa and Naples, which only make sense if you bring your own car and which **in August have prices and availability that are off the scale**: book them months ahead.
- **A car is mandatory.** Sardinian public transport covers almost nothing of what is here. The main roads are good; the interior is made of slow, winding state roads, and **map travel times need to be padded**. **Distances are longer than the map suggests:** the island is the third largest in the Mediterranean.
- **Capped-entry beaches.** This is the point that most often ruins plans. **Many of the most famous beaches have limited access, with mandatory online booking, a daily visitor cap and in several cases a ticket**: this includes, among others, **Cala Goloritzé**, **La Pelosa** in Stintino, **Tuerredda**, **Cala Coticcio** on Caprera and various others. **In high season the spots sell out days in advance**, and without a reservation you don't get in. **Rules, fees and the list of beaches involved change every season: check them before leaving.** In some cases (La Pelosa) a **mat under your towel is mandatory**, to limit the removal of sand.
- **Don't take sand away.** Taking sand, pebbles or shells from Sardinian beaches is **against the law and heavily fined**, with real checks at airports and ports. It applies in particular to quartz beaches like **Is Arutas**, where kilos of stolen sand are returned every year.
- **Cala Goloritzé**, in detail: it is a **natural monument** and can be reached **only on foot** from the Golgo plateau (about an hour and a quarter downhill, and the same uphill on the way back, on a rocky path) **or by sea, but in certain conditions without being able to land directly**. **Access is capped, with a reservation and a fee**, and conditions change: recheck them every year. **You need hiking shoes**, not flip-flops.
- **The Pink Beach of Budelli** has been **off limits since 1994** and can only be looked at from a boat. Anyone who promises to let you land is lying.
- **Su Gorropu Gorge:** it is **one of the deepest canyons in Europe**, with walls over four hundred meters high. Entry is paid; **beyond the first stretch you need equipment, experience or a guide**, and whether it is passable depends on the water level. The descent from the plateau is long, and the way back is all uphill.
- **Food:** Sardinia has **a shepherds' cuisine, not a fishermen's one**, the opposite of what you'd imagine. **Porceddu** (spit-roasted suckling pig), Ogliastra **culurgiones** (potato, pecorino and mint dumplings, closed by hand with a wheat-ear seam), Campidano-style **malloreddus**, **fregola** with clams, **pane carasau** (paper-thin and good for months, born because shepherds stayed away for weeks) and its seasoned version, **guttiau**. **Seadas** fried and drizzled with bitter strawberry-tree honey. **Cabras mullet bottarga**, **pecorino sardo** and **fiore sardo**, and **bottarga** grated over everything.
- **Wine:** **Cannonau** (the red of the interior, linked to the longevity studies of the area), **Vermentino di Gallura**, which is **the island's only DOCG**, **Vernaccia di Oristano**, **Carignano del Sulcis**, **Malvasia di Bosa**. And **mirto**, drunk ice cold at the end of a meal. Wineries are visited **by appointment**.
- **The language:** **Sardinian is not an Italian dialect**, it is an independent Romance language, the most conservative relative to Latin among those that survive. In the interior you hear it spoken, and signs are often bilingual.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Arrival in Olbia and the Costa Smeralda
We landed in **Olbia**, picked up the car and headed into **Gallura**.

We spent the afternoon on the **Costa Smeralda**, which has to be seen and has to be understood. It's not a place that grew by chance: in **1962** a consortium led by the **Aga Khan** bought an uninhabited shepherds' coast and developed it under **a strict architectural code**: no tall buildings, no exposed concrete, low roofs, the colors of the Mediterranean scrub and a formal vocabulary invented at a desk, mixing Sardinian, Ligurian and North African elements. The result is a **fake carried off to perfection**, which sixty years on works better than almost all the genuine coastal building in Italy.

The beaches are **Liscia Ruja**, the biggest, **Capriccioli**, **Romazzino**, **Cala di Volpe**. The pink granite eroded into rounded shapes, the juniper bent by the wind and the water in bands of color are real, and marketing has nothing to do with it.

Then **Porto Cervo** at sunset, to understand the phenomenon: the little square, the shop windows, the boats. You spend an hour there and you've seen it all.

### Day 2 — The La Maddalena Archipelago
A day by boat in the **La Maddalena Archipelago National Park**: **sixty-two islands and islets** between Sardinia and Corsica, almost all uninhabited.

You leave from Palau or La Maddalena. From the boat we saw the **Strait of Bonifacio**, with Corsica a few kilometers away, the coves of **Spargi** and **Santa Maria**, and the **Pink Beach of Budelli**, which owes its color to the fragments of a microorganism and has been **off limits since 1994**, after decades of sand being carried off: you look at it from the water, and that's it.

On land, **Caprera**: the island where **Garibaldi** bought half the land in 1855, where he lived his last twenty-seven years and where he is buried. The **house museum** has been left as it was, with the bed turned toward the window from which he watched the sea. It's a visit that surprises anyone who doesn't expect it.

Also on Caprera is **Cala Coticcio**, called "Tahiti", one of the most beautiful coves in the Mediterranean: **access by land is capped and allowed only with authorized guides**, in limited daily numbers. **Book well in advance, or look at it from the sea.**

### Day 3 — Capo Testa, the Valley of the Moon and Inland Gallura
In the morning we were in **Santa Teresa di Gallura** and on the headland of **Capo Testa**, where **wind and sea have eroded the granite into shapes that look sculpted**: round boulders, arches, channels. In antiquity it was a Roman quarry: there are still **columns roughed out and abandoned on the spot**, and granite from here ended up in the basilicas of Rome and in Pisa Cathedral.

Next to it is the **Valley of the Moon** (Cala Grande), a basin of granite boulders in the scrub where, since the 1970s, hippie communities have settled informally: some people still live there all year, among the rocks.

In the afternoon we entered **inland Gallura**, the opposite of the coast: granite hills, cork oak groves and the **stazzi**, the isolated stone farmhouses that were the traditional settlement pattern, scattered instead of gathered into villages. **Tempio Pausania**, **Aggius** with the boulder landscape of the Gallurese Valley of the Moon and the banditry museum, and **Luogosanto**.

This is where **Vermentino di Gallura** is made, the island's only **DOCG**, which on granite takes on a minerality it doesn't have elsewhere. **Winery by appointment.**

### Day 4 — Toward the Gulf of Orosei
We moved south along the east coast: it's the longest driving day of the itinerary and should be taken slowly.

Along the way you can stop at **San Teodoro**, with its lagoon and **flamingos**, or at **Cala Brandinchi** and **Lu Impostu**, beautiful beaches that are both **capped in high season**: check the year's rules first.

The destination is **Cala Gonone** or the **Dorgali** area, the base for the **Gulf of Orosei**: forty kilometers of **limestone cliffs plunging into the sea without a single road**, with coves reachable only by boat or on foot. It is the most spectacular stretch of coast in Italy and one of the few left inaccessible.

That evening we went to bed early: the next day we walk.

### Day 5 — Cala Goloritzé
The most beautiful day of the itinerary, and the one that takes the most organization.

We set out early from the **Golgo plateau**, above Baunei, reached by a steep road with hairpin bends. From there the path drops into the **codula**, a dry gully, for about an hour and a quarter, over rocky, uneven ground, among centuries-old holm oaks and junipers.

Then you arrive, and **Cala Goloritzé** is what it is: a crescent of white pebbles with water of a turquoise that looks retouched, closed in by cliffs, with the **Aguglia**, a limestone obelisk about 143 meters high, rising vertically from the rock on the south side, and a **natural arch** on the other. **The beach formed in 1962, after a landslide**: it is younger than the Costa Smeralda.

**There are no services, no shade, nothing to buy.** Water, food, a hat and sunscreen. **The way back is all uphill**, and it's the part that surprises anyone who hasn't factored it in: leave yourself time and avoid the middle of the day.

**Access is capped, with a reservation and a fee**, and **climbing the Aguglia is forbidden**, to protect the spire.

### Day 6 — The Gulf's Coves by Boat
The completion of the previous day, from the water.

We took the boat excursion from **Cala Gonone** or from **Santa Maria Navarrese** along the whole gulf, with swim stops: **Cala Mariolu**, with white pebbles and a seabed that drops away in steps, **Cala Biriola**, **Cala Sisine** and **Cala Luna**, with the caves behind the beach. These too are in large part **capped**, and boats have assigned landing quotas: **rules change, so ask the operator when you book.**

In the gulf there is also the **Bue Marino Cave**, a very long sea cave that you enter by boat and then continue on foot along walkways among stalactites and little lakes. The name comes from the **monk seal**, which lived here until the 1980s and which today is down to a few hundred individuals in the Mediterranean.

### Day 7 — The Supramonte: Gorropu or Tiscali
The mountain day, with two alternative options: doing both on the same day is not realistic.

**Option A — The Su Gorropu Gorge.** You descend from the Genna 'e Silana plateau on a path of about an hour and a half and enter a **canyon with walls over four hundred meters high**, which in places narrows to just a few meters wide. The floor is made of enormous boulders smoothed by water. **The first stretch is open to everyone; beyond it you need equipment, experience or a guide**, and whether it is passable depends on the water level. **The climb back out is demanding.**

**Option B — The Nuragic village of Tiscali.** You walk through the Lanaittu Valley and climb to a **doline**, a sinkhole in the limestone with its roof partly collapsed: **inside, hidden, is a Nuragic village**, with huts built under the natural vault. It was only found at the end of the nineteenth century and its function is debated: probably a refuge for local peoples during the Romanization. The route takes about four hours in all and **the last part needs sure feet**.

For anyone who doesn't feel up to either, there is the **Su Gologone** spring, a karst resurgence from which an entire river emerges, the water emerald green.

### Day 8 — The Barbagia: Orgosolo, Mamoiada, Cannonau
We went into the interior, the Sardinia that has nothing to do with the sea.

**Orgosolo** is famous for its **murals**: there are hundreds on the houses of the town, begun in the 1960s and 70s with political content (the shepherds' struggles, antimilitarism, international conflicts) and continued to this day. It's a town that started writing on its walls what it thought, and kept going: tourist decoration is something else.

**Mamoiada** is the town of the **masks**. The **mamuthones** are men covered in black sheepskins, with a dark wooden mask and **up to thirty kilos of cowbells on their backs**, advancing with a hopping, measured step; the **issohadores**, dressed in red, catch spectators with a rope. They come out on **January 17 for Sant'Antonio** and during Carnival. **The Museum of Mediterranean Masks** explains the context and shows the related masks of half of Europe: they are surviving pre-Christian rites, and seeing their common origin from Sardinia to the Balkans is the most interesting part.

This is also the area where the studies on **Sardinian longevity** are concentrated, one of the places in the world with the highest percentage of centenarians, and where **Cannonau**, the mountain red, is made. **Winery by appointment.**

In the evening, a proper meal: **porceddu**, **culurgiones**, **pecorino**, **pane carasau**, **seadas** and ice-cold **mirto**.

### Day 9 — The Sinis, Tharros and the Nuraghi
We crossed the island westward, on the day dedicated to archaeology.

**Su Nuraxi di Barumini**, a UNESCO site, is **the best-preserved and most understandable Nuragic complex**: a central tower from the 17th century BC with a four-tower bastion and a village of huts around it. **It can only be visited with a guide**, at fixed times. It's worth clarifying what nuraghi are: **there are about seven thousand in Sardinia**, built dry, without mortar, from enormous blocks, between 1800 and 700 BC, and **their function is not known for certain**. Fortresses, temples, storehouses, prestige homes: the debate is open. It is **a civilization whose writing has not survived**, and here is the point: what you see is all there is.

Not far away is the **Santa Cristina sacred well**: a trapezoidal staircase of perfectly squared blocks descending underground to a chamber with water, built with a precision that to the naked eye seems impossible for the Bronze Age. **On dates near the equinoxes the light enters through the opening and lights up the water's surface**, and every eighteen and a half years the Moon is reflected exactly at the center: it was an astronomical building.

In the afternoon, the **Sinis peninsula**: **Tharros**, a Phoenician and then Punic and Roman city, with columns on the point and the sea on three sides; **San Giovanni di Sinis**, with one of the oldest churches in Sardinia; and **Is Arutas**, the beach of white, pink and green **quartz grains**, as big as grains of rice, born from the erosion of rocks inland. **The sand is not to be touched: taking it is forbidden and fined.**

In **Cabras** is the museum with the **Giants of Mont'e Prama**: **stone statues over two meters tall** of archers, boxers and warriors, with eyes made of double concentric circles, about three thousand years old, found by chance by a farmer in 1974. They are **the oldest fully three-dimensional anthropomorphic statues of the western Mediterranean**, and their discovery rewrote what was thought about Nuragic civilization.

### Day 10 — The Sulcis-Iglesiente and Porto Flavia
We went down to the south-west, the least visited part of the island and one of the most extraordinary.

**Porto Flavia**, near **Masua**, is the essential stop: **a gallery dug horizontally into the cliff above the sea**, in 1924, to load ore directly onto ships. Before, the sacks were carried by hand to boats and then transferred; the engineer who designed it solved the problem with two superimposed tunnels, vertical silos in the rock and a retractable conveyor belt that reached the hold. **It can only be visited with a guide**, and from the opening onto the void you look at the **Pan di Zucchero**, a limestone sea stack **133 meters** high that rises from the sea two hundred meters away. It is **industrial archaeology set against a view that seems built for the purpose.**

Nearby are **Cala Domestica**, a bay enclosed between two walls with the Spanish tower and the remains of mining structures, and the **mines of Montevecchio and Ingurtosu**, abandoned factory-villages with the Liberty-style management buildings in the middle of nowhere. **The Geo-Mining Park** tells a story that lasted more than a century and ended badly: when the mines closed, in the 1970s and 80s, the area emptied out.

### Day 11 — The Piscinas Dunes and the Costa Verde
The day almost nobody puts in the plan.

The **Piscinas dunes** are **among the tallest in Europe**: a moving dune system reaching **tens of meters in height**, fed by the mistral and held in place by centuries-old bent junipers and lentisk bushes. We got there at the end of a dirt road in the middle of nowhere, and walked through a landscape that looks like nowhere else in Italy: golden sand as far as the eye can see, open sea ahead and, behind, the ruins of the mines.

The rest of the **Costa Verde** (**Scivu**, **Portixeddu**, **Capo Pecora**) is a string of very long beaches with no buildings, no beach clubs and often no one, even in August. **There are no services:** you have to bring everything.

**Watch out for the sea:** on this west-facing coast currents and waves are serious, and there are almost never lifeguards.

### Day 12 — Carloforte and the Island of San Pietro
We took the ferry to the **island of San Pietro** and **Carloforte**, Sardinia's linguistic and cultural anomaly.

In 1738 a group of **Ligurian** families, who for two centuries had lived in **Tabarka**, an island off the Tunisian coast where they worked coral for the Lomellini, were moved here by the Savoy after ending up in slavery. **Even today they speak Tabarchino, a Ligurian language**, have Genoese surnames and a cuisine that mixes Liguria, Sardinia and North Africa: **cascà** (a vegetable couscous), **pesto**, **farinata** and above all **tuna**.

For centuries Carloforte was a center of **tuna fishing with the fixed tonnara**, and bluefin tuna remains its culinary identity. Every part is eaten, **bottarga, ventresca, mosciame, lattume**, with a culture of cutting closer to the Japanese than the Italian.

The island can be toured in a day: the **basalt columns** of Punta delle Colonne, **Cala Fico**, the **salt pans** with **flamingos**, and the village with its colored houses and green shutters, which looks like a piece of Liguria set down by mistake in Sardinia.

### Day 13 — Cagliari
The last city, which surprises almost everyone.

**Cagliari** is built on white limestone hills. The **Castello** quarter, at the top, is closed in by Pisan walls with two towers, the **Elephant Tower** and the **San Pancrazio Tower**, which you can climb and from which you see the whole gulf. The **Bastione di Saint Remy** is the city's terrace.

The **National Archaeological Museum** is the stop that closes the itinerary. There are the **Nuragic bronzetti**, hundreds of little figures ten to thirty centimeters tall representing chiefs, archers, worshippers, boats and animals, cast by the lost-wax method three thousand years ago: they are the main visual source on a civilization that left no texts. Together with the **Giants of Mont'e Prama** kept here, they are the most complete portrait of the builders of those seven thousand nuraghi seen along the road.

The afternoon at the **Poetto**, the eight-kilometer urban beach, and at the **Molentargius lagoon** behind it, where **flamingos nest permanently** in the middle of the city: a colony that settled in the 1990s and today numbers thousands.

A last dinner in town, and a last mirto.

### Day 14 — Departure
The last morning can be spent in Cagliari or on the **Costa del Sud**, toward the airport: **Chia** with its dunes and Spanish tower, **Tuerredda** (capped in season) and the Phoenician-Roman site of **Nora** on the point, with the theater overlooking the sea and part of the city submerged a few meters deep.

Flight from Cagliari.

## The Two Short Versions
- **A chic week (north-east):** Costa Smeralda, the La Maddalena archipelago by boat, Capo Testa, inland Gallura and two days on the Gulf of Orosei with Cala Goloritzé. It's the most photogenic itinerary and the most expensive.
- **An unexplored week (south-west and interior):** fly into Cagliari, Barbagia with Orgosolo and Mamoiada, Barumini and Santa Cristina, Sinis and Tharros, Porto Flavia, the Piscinas dunes, Carloforte and Cagliari. It costs a fraction of the first, has almost no foreign tourism and is the Sardinia that stays in your mind longest.
- **Ten days:** the two weeks shortened, cutting inland Gallura, one of the two Supramonte days and the Costa del Sud.
- **What not to do:** fly into Olbia and stay within forty kilometers of the airport. It's the most expensive way to see the least Sardinian part of Sardinia.

## What to see / what to do
- **The La Maddalena archipelago by boat**, with the Pink Beach looked at from afar.
- **Garibaldi's house on Caprera**, with the bed turned toward the sea.
- **Cala Goloritzé on foot**, with the Aguglia and the climb back.
- **The coves of the Gulf of Orosei from the sea.**
- **The Su Gorropu Gorge** or the **village of Tiscali** in the doline.
- **The murals of Orgosolo** and the masks of Mamoiada.
- **Su Nuraxi di Barumini** and the **Santa Cristina sacred well**.
- **The Giants of Mont'e Prama**, which rewrote the prehistory of the western Mediterranean.
- **Porto Flavia**, the gallery dug into the cliff above the Pan di Zucchero.
- **The Piscinas dunes**, among the tallest in Europe.
- **Carloforte**, where they have spoken Ligurian since 1738.
- **The flamingos of Molentargius**, inside Cagliari.

## Possible extensions
- **Alghero and the north-west coast (+2-3 days):** the **Catalan** old town (Algherese, a variant of Catalan, is still spoken and signs are bilingual), **Capo Caccia** with the **Neptune's Grotto**, reached by boat or by going down the **Escala del Cabirol**, a staircase of **654 steps** cut into the cliff, and **Bosa** with its colored houses on the Temo river and its Malvasia.
- **Asinara (+1 day):** a former penal colony and now a national park, with **white albino donkeys**, reachable only by authorized means and in capped numbers.
- **Ogliastra and the Trenino Verde (+1-2 days):** a narrow-gauge railway crossing the interior on nineteenth-century routes, with **seasonal and limited** runs: check the schedule first.
- **Corsica (+3-5 days):** an hour by ferry from Santa Teresa to Bonifacio, and it's an entirely different island a few kilometers away.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **Costa Smeralda:** in high season it is among the most expensive areas in Europe. **Anyone who wants that coast without that price sleeps in Cannigione, Palau or San Pantaleo**, a few kilometers away, and drives to the beach.
- **Cala Gonone or Dorgali** for the Gulf of Orosei: Dorgali is cheaper and more of a real town.
- **Barbagia:** the agriturismi of the interior have very low prices compared with the coast and the best cooking on the island.
- **Sulcis and Costa Verde:** very few places to stay, very far from one another. **Book ahead even in low season**, because a few kilometers away there is no alternative.
- **Central Cagliari** (Castello, Marina, Villanova) for the last part.
- **General rule:** in Sardinia, for the same property, prices **triple in August** compared with June and September, and in the north-east the multiplier is higher still.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **In the interior**, where you eat better and spend less: **porceddu**, **culurgiones**, **malloreddus**, **pecorino** and **pane carasau**.
- **In Cabras** for **mullet bottarga**, the best in Italy.
- **In Carloforte** for **tuna** in all its parts and for **cascà**.
- **Seadas** with **bitter strawberry-tree honey** at the end of the meal, and ice-cold **mirto**.
- **To drink:** Cannonau in the Barbagia, Vermentino di Gallura in the north, Vernaccia around Oristano, Carignano in the Sulcis.
- **Note:** on the beaches of the Costa Smeralda and in the tourist marinas you pay for the location. Five kilometers inland, on the same days, you eat twice as well for half the price.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Check the capped-entry beaches and book**, months ahead in high season. It's the point that ruins the most plans in Sardinia.
- **Don't take away sand, pebbles or shells.** It's forbidden and fined, and the checks are real.
- **Hiking shoes for Cala Goloritzé**, and time for the climb back.
- **Don't do Gorropu and Tiscali on the same day.**
- **Open in Olbia and close in Cagliari** with a one-way rental.
- **Don't stay within the forty kilometers around Olbia.**
- **Don't go in August** and, if you absolutely must, book ferries and lodging months ahead.
- **Bring water, food and shade** to the Costa Verde and the isolated coves: there are no services at all.
- **Watch the currents** on west-facing beaches.
- **Book wineries and guided visits** (Barumini, Porto Flavia, Bue Marino Cave), which have fixed times.
- **Factor in the distances:** the island is big, and the interior state roads are slow.

## Chiusura personale
What stays with you from Sardinia is the contrast between two things seen in the same week. The first is Porto Cervo: a perfect little square, designed in the 1960s by brilliant architects, in which every detail (the plaster, the color of the shutters, the curve of the roof) was decided at a desk to evoke a Mediterranean village that never existed on that spot. It works beautifully, and it is fake in a completely honest way.

The second is any nuraghe at all, the kind you come across in the middle of a field along an interior state road, with no sign and no ticket office: a cone of boulders stacked dry, without mortar, standing for thirty-five hundred years, built by a civilization that left seven thousand of them on one island and whose language we don't know, nor the name it called itself, nor with certainty what those towers were for.

Between these two things there are sixty kilometers of road and thirty-five hundred years, and they sit on the same island. Anyone who goes to Sardinia and sees only the first hasn't chosen the wrong vacation: they've just looked at the two percent.
