# South Korea in Two Weeks: From the DMZ to the Haenyeo of Jeju

## Apertura personale
South Korea is the easiest country in Asia to travel in and one of the hardest to understand. Easy because everything works: trains leave on the minute, you pay for a two-dollar coffee by card, nobody touches a bag left on a café table. Hard because beneath the skyscrapers and the K-pop there are two things no guidebook puts on its cover: a war that was never closed with a peace treaty, and a community of women who have been free-diving off Jeju for centuries and who, at today's numbers, won't exist in twenty years. This itinerary is built around those two things (the DMZ at the start, the haenyeo at the end) and everything in between that helps you make sense of them.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** April for the cherry blossoms, which move up the country from south to north between late March and mid-April, or October to early November for the fall foliage, which in Korea is a national event followed bulletin by bulletin. Avoid the jangma, the monsoon season of late June and July, and above all the two big holidays, **Seollal** (Lunar New Year, January-February) and **Chuseok** (September-October), when the whole country travels to its hometowns, transport is sold out and many restaurants and shops stay closed for days.
- **Documents/visa:** passport valid for the whole stay. Italian citizens need no visa for up to 90 days, and the **K-ETA exemption has been extended until December 31, 2026**. You can still apply for it voluntarily (about 10,000 won, valid for three years) to avoid filling in the paper arrival card on every trip; since February 2025 there is also an **e-Arrival Card**, which you can fill in online before departure. The exemption has been extended year by year and is not permanent: check the rules again before you leave.
- **Currency and payments:** won (KRW). It is one of the simplest countries in the world: foreign cards work everywhere, even for tiny amounts, and you hardly need cash. The card to get right away is the **T-money**, sold in any convenience store for about 3,000-4,000 won: it works for the subway, buses, taxis and even for paying at the konbini.
- **Connectivity, the surprise that catches everyone out:** **Google Maps in Korea does not calculate routes**, neither on foot nor by car, because of the rules on exporting mapping data. It shows places but takes you nowhere. Before leaving, download **Naver Map** or **KakaoMap** for navigation (both have an English interface), **Kakao T** for taxis (it works with a foreign card) and **Papago** for translation, which is much better than the alternatives with Korean.
- **Trains:** the KTX links Seoul to Busan in just over 2h30 and is booked on the Korail website or app, which accept foreign cards. On the main routes it's best to book a few days ahead. Where the train doesn't go (Sokcho, for example) there are express buses, very convenient and frequent.
- **From the airport to the centre:** from **Incheon (ICN)** the **AREX Express** runs non-stop to Seoul Station in 43-51 minutes, about 13,000 won; the All-Stop, about an hour, costs about 4,750-5,350 won with T-money and also stops at Hongdae · **limousine buses** to the neighbourhoods and big hotels, about 18,000 won, tickets at the arrivals desks · taxi about 65,000-90,000 won with tolls, better through Kakao T than with the touts in the terminal. **Gimpo (GMP)** is on the subway. At **Busan Gimhae (PUS)** the BGL light rail goes to Sasang, about 1,600 won by card, and the limousine to Haeundae costs about 9,500 won. At **Jeju (CJU)** you pick up the rental car.
- **DMZ:** you can't enter independently. You need a tour with an authorized operator, booked days in advance, and your **original passport**, without which you won't get through the checkpoint. The **JSA at Panmunjom** is a different matter: it must be booked 2-4 weeks ahead, passport details must be sent 5-7 days before for military clearance, there is a dress code, and since 2023 civilian access has been suspended several times. Check its status shortly before you leave, with a plan B ready.
- **Electricity:** 220V, type C and F sockets. Italian plugs fit without an adapter, which is rare in Asia.
- **Language:** hangul, the Korean alphabet, was designed in the fifteenth century to be learned quickly, and it works: a couple of hours is enough to decipher signs and station names, and it makes a big difference. English is taught to everyone but spoken hesitantly, especially outside Seoul.
- **Jeju:** you get there by plane from Gimpo or Busan, with dozens of flights a day at very low prices. On the island you need a **rental car**, and to rent one you need an **international driving permit**, which you have to get in Italy before leaving: it's not negotiable.
- **Approximate budget:** KTX Seoul-Busan around €40, good hotels €60-90, meals €6-15 with banchan always included and refilled for free. Coffee, on the other hand, costs as much as in Europe: in Korea cafés are meeting places and you pay for the seat.
- **Useful numbers:** police 112, emergencies 119, and above all **1330**, the Korea Travel Hotline: open 24 hours a day, multilingual, it solves everything from transport problems to phone translation. It is one of the best tourist services in the world.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Arrival in Seoul
We landed at Incheon (ICN) and took the AREX into the center, about an hour. We stayed between Myeongdong and Jongno, near a subway station. The AREX has two services: the direct express train to Seoul Station, faster and with an assigned seat, and the all-stop train, slower but cheaper and connected to the subway. If you arrive with lots of luggage or late in the evening, the limousine buses stop in front of the big hotels.

The first day was light because of jet lag: the **T-money** card bought at the first convenience store, checking that Naver Map worked, and dinner at **Gwangjang Market**, which is the right way to start. You eat on plastic stools in front of the cooks: bindaetteok, mayak gimbap, tteokbokki. *In Korea Google Maps works poorly for walking and driving directions: download Naver Map or KakaoMap before leaving, with the map set to English.* The market is at its best towards evening, when all the food stalls are open.

**In practice:** AREX express about 45 minutes to Seoul Station, regular train about an hour · T-money can be topped up with cash at convenience stores and stations · Myeongdong and Jongno are handy bases for the palaces and the subway · Gwangjang open until evening.

### Day 2 — Palaces and the War Memorial
In the morning we visited **Changdeokgung**, with the guided tour of the **Secret Garden**, booked online a few days in advance because numbers are limited. It's the most beautiful of the five royal palaces and the best preserved. The garden can only be visited in a group, with English tours at fixed times, and some places are sold at the ticket office on the morning itself; you also need the palace ticket, bought separately. *Online bookings open a few days ahead, and in autumn, with the foliage, they sell out immediately.*

In the afternoon, **Gyeongbokgung** for the changing of the guard, ideally in **hanbok**, which gets you in free and which plenty of Koreans wear too. *Note: Gyeongbokgung closes on Tuesdays, the other palaces on Mondays.* Inside the walls are also the National Folk Museum, free, and the lake pavilion of Gyeonghoeru. At the end of the day we went to the **War Memorial of Korea**, free. It's the stop that makes the next day readable: without that context, the DMZ remains a collection of photographs.

**In practice:** Gyeongbokgung about 3,000 won, free in hanbok · Secret Garden with a separate ticket, booked on the official royal palaces website · hanbok rental in the shops around Gyeongbokgung, a few tens of thousands of won for a few hours · War Memorial closed on Mondays.

### Day 3 — The DMZ
We set off early in the morning with an authorized tour, **original passport in our pocket**: at the checkpoint no copy is accepted.

The standard route takes in **Imjingak**, with the Freedom Bridge and the bullet-riddled locomotive left where it stood; the **Third Infiltration Tunnel**, discovered in 1978, 1,635 meters dug from the North toward the south just over forty kilometers from Seoul, which you walk through hunched under a low ceiling; the **Dora Observatory**, from which you can see Kaesong and the show village of Kijong-dong with its 160-meter flagpole; and **Dorasan Station**, built in 2002 on the line to Pyongyang, which has been waiting ever since for a train that never came.

*If the **JSA at Panmunjom** is open and you managed to book it weeks ahead, it's a completely different day: the blue huts straddling the demarcation line, the soldiers of the two countries twenty meters apart, the Bridge of No Return. But since 2023 it has been closed more often than open: treat it as a bonus, never as the centerpiece of the trip.*

**In practice:** DMZ tours only with authorized operators, to be booked a few days ahead · original passport required · tours can be cancelled even the day before for military exercises or security reasons · back in Seoul by early afternoon.

### Day 4 — Seoul: neighborhoods, fortress, jjimjilbang
We spent the morning between **Bukchon**, the hanok village still lived in between the two palaces, and **Insadong**. In Bukchon the houses are inhabited, and the neighborhood has introduced visiting hours and areas closed to visitors in the evening: you walk quietly and don't photograph doors and windows up close. Insadong is the street of paper, ceramics and tea shops, with teahouses hidden in the courtyards.

In the afternoon we walked a stretch of the **Seoul city wall** along the ridge, with the city below: it's the best way to understand how the capital is wedged between the mountains. The easiest stretch is the one through **Naksan Park**, which climbs from the Dongdaemun area in half an hour and is lovely at sunset. In the evening, **jjimjilbang**, the 24-hour sauna: thermal pools separated by sex and without swimsuits, a mixed common room in pajamas where families sleep on mats on the floor. For a little over ten euros it's the most Korean institution there is, and you can sleep there if you want. *You enter the pools naked and after a thorough shower; in some places large tattoos are not welcome.*

**In practice:** Bukchon with limited visiting hours in the residential areas · Naksan wall section about half an hour of easy climbing · jjimjilbang with towels and pajamas included, locker key on your wrist.

### Day 5 — Sokcho and Seoraksan
**Express bus from Seoul to Sokcho, about 2.5 hours** (the train doesn't go this far). Buses leave several times an hour from Seoul's terminals, and it's worth booking online for the weekend. Sokcho is a seaside town on the east coast, with its fishing harbor and markets, and serves as a base for Seoraksan park, half an hour away by bus.

In the afternoon we visited the **Abai** neighborhood, founded by North Korean refugees who stopped here during the war thinking they'd move on after a few months. You get there by crossing the canal on the **galsseonbae**, the cable barge that passengers pull by hand all together. For dinner, **ojingeo sundae**, the stuffed squid that is the specialty brought by the refugees. Before dinner you can stop at the covered **Sokcho Central Market**, with stalls of fried seafood and sweet glazed chicken. *For Seoraksan tomorrow, start early: in high season the buses to the park fill up in the morning.*

**In practice:** Seoul-Sokcho about 2.5 hours by express bus · galsseonbae with a ticket of a few hundred won · from central Sokcho to Seoraksan park, direct buses in about half an hour · to sleep, near the terminal or the harbor.

### Day 6 — Seoraksan
We spent the day in **Seoraksan National Park**: the bronze Buddha of Sinheungsa at the entrance and then the climb to **Ulsanbawi**, about four hours round trip, with the last stretch on 808 metal steps over a sheer granite wall.

Since 2023 there's no longer a fee to enter the park; only parking is paid for those arriving by car. Start by eight, with water and something to eat, because there are only a couple of refreshment points along the trail. *In October the foliage makes the park magnificent and impassable at the same time: at weekends you climb in single file. Here a weekday is a requirement rather than a suggestion.* If you don't want to hike, you can take the **cable car to Gwongeumseong**, which climbs in a few minutes to a rocky viewpoint; tickets are sold by time slot and on busy days the morning ones go fast. Transfer to Andong in the evening or the next morning.

**In practice:** park entry free, paid parking · Ulsanbawi about 4 hours round trip, hiking shoes · cable car with timed tickets, buy them as soon as you arrive · few direct buses from Sokcho to Andong: check times the day before.

### Day 7 — Andong and Hahoe Village
We reached **Andong** and from there **Hahoe**, a UNESCO site, home to the same family for six hundred years. The layout reads as you walk: the nobles' houses with tiled roofs higher up and in the middle, the farmers' houses with thatched roofs all around. The village sits in a bend of the Nakdong River, and on the opposite bank the **Buyongdae** cliff gives the best view from above, reachable by boat in the warm season.

If you're there on the right days, there's the **byeolsingut mask** performance: once a year the farmers staged a satire in which the monk is corrupt and the nobleman an idiot, and the nobles watched. **Sleep inside the village**, in a hanok. At five the coaches all leave together and what remains is the river, the dogs and a few old people sitting outside: it's the only reason not to do it as a day trip. For dinner, **andong jjimdak**, the braised chicken named after the city.

**In practice:** from Andong to Hahoe by local bus in about 50 minutes, infrequent departures · village entry ticket of a few euros · hanok with heated floors and mattresses on the floor, book ahead · andong jjimdak at Andong market, portions for two.

### Day 8 — Gyeongju
We moved to **Gyeongju**, capital of the Silla kingdom for almost a thousand years. The city is small and flat, and easy to get around by bike: rentals are near the bus terminal and the tumuli. The best area to stay is **Hwangnidan-gil**, with hanoks turned into cafés and small guesthouses, a short walk from the sites.

In the afternoon we cycled among the **royal tumuli of Daereungwon**, which stand in the middle of the city rather than in a fenced archaeological park: grassy hills twenty meters high next to road junctions. One is open and you can go inside, with a replica of the gold crown displayed where it was found; the original is in the **Gyeongju National Museum**, free, which is worth an hour. Then **Cheomseongdae**, the seventh-century astronomical observatory. After dark, **Anapji**: the pond with its illuminated pavilions reflected in the water. It takes half an hour and is worth the whole day.

**In practice:** Daereungwon with an inexpensive ticket for the Cheonmachong tomb · Anapji (Donggung and Wolji) open into the evening, best after sunset · Gyeongju National Museum free · bike rental by the hour or day.

### Day 9 — Bulguksa, Seokguram and templestay
In the morning we went up Mount Toham. First **Seokguram**, as soon as it opens, before the coaches arrive: a granite dome from 751, built by fitting blocks together without mortar, with a three-and-a-half-meter Buddha facing the Eastern Sea. *You see it from behind a glass screen, from the antechamber: if you don't know beforehand, you'll be disappointed.* Then **Bulguksa**, with the two eighth-century stone pagodas in the courtyard. Since 2023 entry to both has been free. Between the two there's a shuttle, or a woodland path of about an hour downhill from Seokguram to Bulguksa.

In the afternoon, **templestay** in a mountain monastery: evening ceremony, the ritual baru gongyang meal, in which not a single grain of rice is left, and a wake-up around four for the liturgy in the dark. Book it on the official portal, choosing a temple with an English program, otherwise much of what happens stays incomprehensible. The temple provides the uniform, and you sleep on mats in simple rooms; bring little, and no alcohol.

**In practice:** Bulguksa and Seokguram free, open from early morning · city buses from central Gyeongju to Bulguksa in about half an hour · templestay booked on the official portal a few weeks ahead, costing a few tens of euros a night with meals.

### Day 10 — Busan
**KTX or bus to Busan**, barely half an hour from Gyeongju. The KTX leaves from Gyeongju station, outside town, and arrives at Busan Station; from there the subway reaches every neighborhood. To stay, those who want the sea choose Haeundae or Gwangalli, those who want the markets the Nampo area.

In the morning we went to **Jagalchi fish market**, the largest in Korea, where you buy at the stall downstairs and have it cooked upstairs. Next door are **Gukje** market and the street-food lane of BIFF Square. In the afternoon, **Gamcheon village**: take the bus to the top and walk down, leaving the signposted murals route for the side alleys, where old people still sit outside their homes. *Gamcheon is a lived-in neighborhood: no photos into houses and keep your voice down in the alleys.* In the evening, **Gwangalli**, with the bridge lit up over the sea.

**In practice:** Gyeongju-Busan by KTX about half an hour · at Jagalchi you pay for the fish at the stall and separately for the cooking upstairs · to Gamcheon by minibus from Toseong subway station · Busan subway uses the same T-money as Seoul.

### Day 11 — Busan: the UN cemetery and the coast
In the morning we went to the **UN Memorial Cemetery**, the only one in the world dedicated to UN war dead: rows of headstones of young men from twenty-two countries, many of them twenty years old. After the DMZ, which is all geopolitics, here the war goes back to being made of names. The cemetery is open every day and free, and next to it there's a small hall with the history of each country's troops. You get there by subway to Daeyeon and then a fifteen-minute walk.

In the afternoon there's the **Igidae coastal trail**, with a head-on view of the Haeundae skyline, or the **Haedong Yonggungsa** temple, built on the rocks, to be avoided at weekends, when the path becomes single file. The Igidae trail takes a couple of hours to the **Oryukdo Skywalk**, the glass walkway over the cliff. For dinner, **dwaeji gukbap**, the pork soup born right here in 1951 from the scraps refugees could afford.

**In practice:** UN cemetery free, open every day with longer hours in summer · Igidae trail about 2 hours, with ups and downs and stairs · Haedong Yonggungsa free, reachable by bus from Haeundae · dwaeji gukbap on the street dedicated to it in the Seomyeon district.

### Day 12 — Flight to Jeju and the east coast
**Busan-Jeju flight**, under an hour, and pickup of the rental car (**international driving permit required**). There are many flights and they're cheap if booked early; Busan's airport is Gimhae, linked to the city by light rail. On Jeju a car is really needed: the sights are spread along the coast and buses are infrequent. *Korean rental cars almost always have the navigation in Korean: use Naver Map or KakaoMap on your phone.*

We went straight to the east coast. The first stop is the **Haenyeo Museum** in Gujwa-eup, to be done first because without it everything else stays a curiosity. It explains the **bulteok**, the circular stone shelters where they change, warm up and for generations took community decisions by vote (about seventy remain); the internal hierarchy, from beginners to sanggun; and the 1932 protest, when the haenyeo led one of the largest uprisings against Japanese colonial rule. We stayed around Seongsan. For dinner, one of the **cooperative stalls**, where they sell the catch themselves and the money doesn't go through middlemen.

**In practice:** Busan-Jeju flight under an hour · international permit together with your national license at car pickup · from Jeju airport to Gujwa about an hour by road · Haenyeo Museum ticket about 1,100 won, closed on Mondays.

### Day 13 — The haenyeo and Seongsan Ilchulbong
At dawn we climbed **Seongsan Ilchulbong**, the volcanic cone rising from the sea: twenty minutes of steps and a wind that never stops.

In the morning, depending on the tide schedule, there's the **haenyeo diving demonstration** at the foot of the cone. It's the only reasonably guaranteed chance to see them enter the water. They go down about ten meters without tanks, stay under a couple of minutes and resurface with the **sumbisori**, the piercing whistle they use to expel carbon dioxide: from the pier you hear it before you see anything, and it's exactly how they recognize each other.

*If you have time and a car, you can try the untamed version: the villages of the east coast (Hado-ri, Gujwa, Jongdal) at the right tide hours, with nothing organized and no guarantee. Unwritten rules: don't approach, don't photograph from up close, don't enter the bulteok.*

The afternoon is free: the **Manjanggul** lava tube, a stage of the **Olle Trail** along the coast, or the **4·3 Peace Park**, dedicated to the events of 1948-1954, the harshest page in the island's history.

**In practice:** Seongsan Ilchulbong about 5,000 won, closed on the first Monday of the month · the haenyeo demonstration is free, with times that change with the season and the sea: ask at the ticket office · Manjanggul was closed for safety works: check that it's open before going.

### Day 14 — Return
Flight Jeju-Seoul Gimpo and on to Incheon, or a direct international flight, depending on connections. If you have an extra half day you can use it for **Hallasan**, but only with the online booking already made: the two trails that reach the summit have daily quotas and without a booking you don't get past the checkpoint. The other trails, which don't reach the top, need no booking and can be done in half a day.

If you'd rather stay in town, you can stop at **Dongmun Market**, in Jeju City, a few minutes from the airport, for last purchases: tangerines, the island's green tea and snacks. *From Gimpo to Incheon the AREX takes about half an hour, but between changing airports, luggage and checks, leave at least three hours between the two flights.* The car is returned at Jeju airport, where the rental companies share a parking lot with shuttles to the terminal.

**In practice:** frequent Jeju-Gimpo flights, about an hour · AREX Gimpo-Incheon about half an hour · car return with shuttle to the terminal: allow at least an extra half hour · Hallasan summit only with online booking.

## What to see / what to do
- **The War Memorial before the DMZ**, never after.
- **The Third Tunnel**, the point where the border stops being an abstraction.
- **Dorasan Station**, with the sign that says "first station toward the North."
- **The Secret Garden of Changdeokgung**, booked: it's the most beautiful of Seoul's palaces.
- **A night in a jjimjilbang.**
- **The Abai neighborhood in Sokcho**, which almost no itinerary includes.
- **Sleeping inside Hahoe**, to see the village after five o'clock.
- **Anapji after dark.**
- **The templestay with the ritual meal**, choosing a monastery with an English program.
- **The UN cemetery in Busan.**
- **The Haenyeo Museum before any dive.**
- **Buying from the haenyeo cooperative stalls**: that's how the money reaches them.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **Seoul:** Myeongdong or Jongno for the palaces and the subway; Hongdae and Yeonnam for lower prices and nightlife. The real rule is to choose near a subway station.
- **Sokcho:** in town, near the sea and the market, better than the bare guesthouses at the park entrance.
- **Andong:** inside Hahoe village, in a hanok: thin mattress on the heated floor, bathrooms often shared. It's the reason you stop there.
- **Gyeongju:** around Daereungwon, in hanok guesthouses. One night in a monastery with the templestay.
- **Busan:** Haeundae for the beach, Seomyeon for transport, Nampo-dong for the port and markets.
- **Jeju:** on the east coast around Seongsan, the right choice for anyone coming mainly for the haenyeo and the sunrise over the volcanic cone.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **Seoul:** Gwangjang Market in the evening (bindaetteok, mayak gimbap, tteokbokki), Korean barbecue with the grill built into the table, and the banchan system: five or ten free side dishes at every meal, refilled on request at no cost.
- **Sokcho:** fish at the central market and ojingeo sundae, the stuffed squid brought by the refugees from the North.
- **Andong:** andong jjimdak in the old market, where a whole arcade does nothing else, and heotjesatbap, the "ceremonial" bibimbap born from Confucian ritual offerings.
- **Gyeongju:** ssambap, with twenty small bowls of vegetables and meat to wrap in leaves.
- **Busan:** dwaeji gukbap and milmyeon, two dishes born from the war and the refugees; fish at Jagalchi bought downstairs and cooked upstairs; ssiat hotteok at Gukje Market.
- **Jeju:** the island's black pork on the grill, galchi, gogi guksu, and everything the haenyeo gather (abalone, sea urchin, octopus) bought at the cooperative stalls.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Download Naver Map or KakaoMap before leaving.** Google Maps in Korea shows places but calculates no routes: it's the surprise that catches almost everyone on the first day.
- **Original passport for the DMZ.** No copy, no photo on your phone. Without it, you stay at the checkpoint.
- **Don't build the trip around the JSA.** Since 2023 civilian access has been suspended several times: book the standard tour as the sure base and try for the JSA in parallel.
- **Check the palaces' closing days:** Gyeongbokgung on Tuesday, the others on Monday. It's the most common planning mistake in Seoul.
- **Book the Secret Garden of Changdeokgung**, which has limited numbers and fixed times.
- **Avoid Seoraksan on October weekends**, when the foliage makes it unwalkable.
- **Book Hallasan** if you want to climb it: without a reservation you don't get through.
- **International driving permit for Jeju**, to be done in Italy: without it, they won't give you the car.
- **Don't expect the haenyeo on cue.** They go out according to tides, season and the biological closure; the only scheduled outings are the demonstrations. And they're not a folkloric attraction: they are very old women at work.
- **Avoid Seollal and Chuseok.** On those days the country moves en masse and half of Korea is closed.

## Chiusura personale
What's left of this trip isn't the list of temples. It's two images at opposite ends of the country. The first is a new, gleaming railway station forty kilometers from Seoul, with the departure boards lit and the staff on duty, which since 2002 has been waiting for a train to Pyongyang: the war that hasn't ended isn't seen in a trench, it's seen there. The second is a pier on a cold, windy coast, and a long whistle that arrives from the water before anything visible: the sumbisori of a seventy-five-year-old woman resurfacing with her net full, as she has for sixty years, in a trade that counted four thousand five hundred people ten years ago and today counts about two thousand six hundred. Two things ending in different ways, and the reason it's worth coming now and not in twenty years.
