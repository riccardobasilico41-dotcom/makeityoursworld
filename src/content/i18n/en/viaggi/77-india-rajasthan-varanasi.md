# India: Varanasi, Ranthambore and Rajasthan in 19 Days, by Overnight Train and with a Driver

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 in progress (a trip Rick actually took in late February: the route, the overnight trains, the classes, the booking, Ranthambore, the Jaipur hotel and dinners, the street food and the driver come from him; itinerary and practical information verified with research in November 2026)
**Periodo del viaggio:** late February
**Durata:** 19 days, 18 nights
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independent, with two nights in a sleeper berth, Delhi-Varanasi and Varanasi-Agra, and then a car with driver for all of Rajasthan
**Categoria:** Asia · India · Varanasi · Gange · Rajasthan · Ranthambore · safari · deserto del Thar · treni notturni · spiritualità

---

## Apertura personale
This is how we did India: Varanasi and then Rajasthan, in late February. We landed in Delhi and took the overnight train to Varanasi. From Varanasi another train took us to Agra. From there on we got around with a driver, stop after stop, all the way to the far end of Rajasthan.

We traveled in sleeper berths: second class from Delhi to Varanasi, third from Varanasi to Agra. There isn't a huge difference between the two, and honestly they're not as bad as we expected, even if they're not exactly top of the line. Except for the bathrooms, which, well, you get the idea.

The driver, on the other hand, was a total loose cannon, and I wouldn't recommend him. That's why, further down, there's a section on how to pick one with a bit of judgment.

The rest of the loop is the classic North, taken slowly. Three days on the Ganges in Varanasi, the Taj Mahal at sunset and at dawn, the safaris among the tigers of Ranthambore, then Jaipur, the holy town of Pushkar, Jodhpur, a night among the dunes near Jaisalmer, the Ranakpur temple and the lakes of Udaipur. Varanasi comes first, while you're still fresh: it's the most powerful stop and the most tiring.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** October to March. Varanasi in December and January has cold mornings (5-8 °C) and thick fog on the river at dawn, which sometimes stops the boats and delays the trains; November and February are the most balanced months for the whole itinerary. From April to June the heat tops 40-45 °C both on the Ganges and in the Thar; with the monsoon (July to September) the Ganges rises, floods the lower ghats and boats may be suspended.
- **Documents:** passport valid for at least 6 months from the date of the visa application and two blank pages, e-Tourist Visa on the official portal indianvisaonline.gov.in, to be requested at least 4 days ahead ($25 for 30 days, $40 for one year, plus a 2.5-3% bank fee; fees unchanged in 2026), and the free online e-Arrival Card within the 72 hours before arrival, mandatory since April 1, 2026. Banned at entry: satellite phones, drones without a permit and e-cigarettes.
- **Currency and payments:** Indian rupee, roughly 95-105 to the euro. Cards in hotels and good restaurants; cash for boats, tuk-tuks, temple offerings, markets and tips.
- **Trains, how we booked them:** we booked them in November for late February and still had a hard time getting everything, but we made it. We got them on [12Go](https://12go.asia), because first class was sold out.
- **Trains, how it works:** that's two nights of travel, and they're the first thing to book: start thinking about them months ahead. On IRCTC (irctc.co.in), the Indian railways website, regular bookings open 60 days before departure, not counting the day of travel. 12Go is an online agency that also sells Indian trains, accepts foreign cards without registration and books on IRCTC on your behalf, with a markup; it only sells confirmed tickets.
- **Overnight train classes:** **1A**, air-conditioned first class, has compartments of two or four berths that lock from the inside, but it isn't on every train, has few seats and is expensive. **2A** has two-tier berths in open compartments with curtains (on some trains they never came back after Covid). **3A** has three tiers on each side, often without curtains, and is more crowded. All three have air conditioning, sheets, a blanket and a pillow. **SL** (sleeper) is the berth without air conditioning and without bedding: it's cheap, but it's the most crowded.
- **The Taj Mahal is closed on Fridays.** This is the thing to know before you lock in trains and driver: on Fridays the mosque inside the complex holds the midday prayer, reserved for Agra residents, and the monument is closed to visitors. Plan everything around it.
- **Ranthambore:** safaris need to be booked well ahead, like the trains, especially for the 6-seat gypsy. The park zones are assigned at random.
- **Driver:** you pick one up from Agra onward and keep him for all of Rajasthan. Choose carefully: see "How to choose a driver in Rajasthan."
- **Temples:** shoulders and legs covered, shoes off, often no leather. At Varanasi's Kashi Vishwanath you can't go in with phones, bags or pens: you leave them in the lockers outside.
- **Health:** bottled water only; bathing in the Ganges is not a good idea, the water is heavily polluted. Hepatitis A and B and typhoid vaccines recommended, plus an up-to-date tetanus shot; for a night in the desert you need warm clothes.
- **Clothing and women traveling:** loose clothes covering shoulders and legs, a scarf always in your bag. In Varanasi the alleys are narrow and crowded: better not to walk them alone after sunset.
- **Connectivity:** Airtel or Jio SIM at Delhi airport; good signal almost everywhere, weak among the dunes.
- **Time zone:** UTC+5:30, 3.5 hours ahead of Italy during daylight saving time, 4.5 in winter.
- **From the airport to the centre:** from **Delhi Indira Gandhi (DEL)** the Airport Express metro, orange line, leaves from Terminal 3 and reaches New Delhi Railway Station in about 20 minutes, about 60-65 rupees, token or QR at the ticket office, running from about 5:00 to 23:30; T1 is served by the Magenta line · prepaid taxi from the official police desk in arrivals, about 500-900 rupees, with a night surcharge · Uber and Ola about 400-800 rupees from the signed pick-up zone · on the way back, **Udaipur (UDR)** is 30-40 minutes from the centre by taxi or app · the typical mistake is listening to anyone who says your hotel is closed or full and offers somewhere else: go straight to the address you booked.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Arrival in Delhi and Old Delhi
You almost always land at Indira Gandhi airport at night or early in the morning. To get to the center there's the prepaid taxi from the official counter, Uber or the hotel transfer: *whoever tells you at the exit that your hotel is closed or full is just after a commission on another one*. If you land at night, sleep a few hours; if you arrive in the morning you still need a break, because the next evening you leave on the overnight train and it's better not to board it already tired. That's also why, for the first night, a hotel near a metro station is handy.

After a few hours' rest, the afternoon is for **Old Delhi**, Shah Jahan's Mughal city, by metro to Chandni Chowk. The **Jama Masjid**, the largest mosque in India, is visited outside prayer times, covered up and barefoot; from the courtyard you can see the Red Fort. Then the alleys of **Chandni Chowk** by cycle rickshaw, the spice market of **Khari Baoli** and the fried parathas of Paranthe Wali Gali. It's the most chaotic neighborhood of the whole trip, best taken as a first taste. Head back before dark.

**In practice:** Airport Express metro to New Delhi in about 20 minutes · the metro has a women-only carriage at the front of the train · Jama Masjid free entry, camera fee, closed to tourists during prayers · cycle rickshaw 300-500 rupees a ride, price agreed beforehand.

### Day 2 — Delhi: Humayun, Qutub Minar and the overnight train to Varanasi
Early in the morning, **Humayun's Tomb**, the Mughal mausoleum that inspired the Taj Mahal, with the gardens still almost empty. Then the **Qutub Minar**, south of the city. If you have time, stop at **Gurudwara Bangla Sahib**, the Sikh temple where you can eat for free at the community kitchen.

In the evening you leave. We went from Delhi to Varanasi on the **overnight train**, in a second-class berth: you sleep on the train and save a night in a hotel. There are several overnight trains on this route. The **Shiv Ganga Express** leaves New Delhi around 8:05 p.m. and gets to Banaras around 6:10 a.m., every day; the Swatantrata Senani Express leaves New Delhi around 9:15 p.m. and gets to Varanasi Junction around 8:05 a.m. *Get to the station with an hour to spare: New Delhi is huge and crowded, and you find your platform on the departures board.*

**In practice:** Humayun and Qutub Minar about 600 rupees each · leave the big bag at the hotel until evening or in the station's left-luggage office · dinner before boarding or a meal ordered on the train · double-check the times on NTES or the IRCTC app the day before.

### Day 3 — Varanasi: arriving at dawn, the ghats and the aarti at Dashashwamedh
You get off the train in the morning light. From the station to the river it's a good half hour by e-rickshaw or taxi. The car stops at the edge of the old city, and the last stretch to the hotel on the ghats is on foot through the alleys, with your luggage. *Ask the hotel to send someone to meet you where you get dropped off: the first time, the alleys are a maze.*

After a shower and a few hours of rest, the first walk is along the 84 ghats, one after another, among washermen, wrestlers, cows and chai sellers. At sunset, **Dashashwamedh Ghat** for the **Ganga Aarti**, the evening ceremony: seven priests swing big fire lamps to the rhythm of bells and chanting, in front of thousands of people on the steps and on the boats. To get a spot on the steps you need to arrive at least an hour early; otherwise you watch from the water, from a boat.

**In practice:** ask the hotel for early check-in, since the train arrives before the usual time · Ganga Aarti every evening, starting around 6 p.m. in winter and 7 p.m. in summer · pickpocketing is common in the aarti crowd: bag in front.

### Day 4 — Varanasi: the boat at dawn, Manikarnika and the Kashi Vishwanath
The day starts before sunrise, on a boat. A rowboat from **Assi Ghat** or Dashashwamedh heads up the Ganges while pilgrims come down for their ritual bath, women set flower lamps floating and priests recite the morning prayers. At Assi Ghat, at dawn, there's also Subah-e-Banaras, a ceremony with chanting, yoga and a small aarti.

**Manikarnika Ghat** is the main cremation ghat, where the pyres burn day and night. For Hindus, dying and being cremated in Varanasi frees you from the cycle of rebirth. You can watch in silence, from a distance, but without taking photos and without going along with anyone who offers to explain: *the self-appointed "guide" who takes you up to a terrace to watch and then asks for a donation for wood for the poor is the best-known scam in the city*.

Behind the ghats opens the old city, a tangle of alleys a meter and a half wide, full of little shrines, silk shops and lassi stalls. The **Kashi Vishwanath**, the golden temple dedicated to Shiva, now has a big corridor linking it to the river; the lines can be long. The afternoon is for resting, because Varanasi wears you out.

**In practice:** private rowboat for an hour at dawn roughly 800-1,500 rupees, agree on the price first · Manikarnika no photos, respect for grieving families · Kashi Vishwanath with lockers outside for your things, covered clothing · "handmade" silks aren't always handmade: buy in recommended shops.

### Day 5 — Sarnath and the overnight train to Agra
**Sarnath**, half an hour from Varanasi, is where the Buddha gave his first sermon after enlightenment. The Dhamek stupa, the monastery ruins, the temples built by Sri Lanka, Tibet, Thailand and Japan and the archaeological museum, with Ashoka's lion capital that became the symbol of India, make for a quiet morning after the intensity of the ghats. *The museum is closed on Fridays: if the day falls on one, swap it with the day before.*

In the late afternoon you're off again, by train once more: we went from Varanasi to Agra in a berth, third class this time. The Marudhar Express takes about fourteen hours, arriving the next morning. Lock your bag to the berth with a padlock; keep your documents and phone on you.

**In practice:** Sarnath about 300 rupees for foreigners (UNESCO site since July 2026), museum separate, closed on Fridays · the Marudhar Express leaves from Varanasi City (not the Junction) around 4:30-4:40 p.m. and arrives at Agra Fort around 6:35 a.m., in 2A or 3A · the three Marudhar trains don't run on the same days: check NTES with your date.

### Day 6 — Agra: the Fort, the "baby Taj" and the Taj Mahal at sunset
You reach Agra in the morning: shower and breakfast at the hotel. Late in the morning, **Agra Fort**, the red sandstone Mughal citadel, then **Itimad-ud-Daulah**, the inlaid mausoleum that came before the Taj and is almost always quiet.

In the late afternoon you go into the **Taj Mahal**. We saw it twice: at sunset the first day and at dawn the next. The complex closes half an hour before sunset, so go in with a couple of hours to spare and stay in the garden while the light fades on the marble. There are lines at security. Our advice: bring the bare minimum and, if you can, no backpacks, to speed everything up.

If you'd rather see it from outside, go to **Mehtab Bagh**, the Mughal garden on the opposite bank of the Yamuna: it's outside the complex, has its own ticket, and from there you see the Taj head-on, across the river, right up to actual sunset.

It's also the day you meet the driver who'll take you around Rajasthan. Meet him in the evening, without rushing, and go over the plan, the stops and what's included in the price together.

**In practice:** Agra Fort about 650 rupees · Taj Mahal open from half an hour before sunrise to half an hour before sunset, about 1,100 rupees plus 200 for the mausoleum, tickets online on the ASI portal · each entry has its own ticket: for two visits you buy two · Mehtab Bagh 300 rupees for foreigners · stay in Taj Ganj, near the east gate · **the Taj Mahal is closed on Fridays**: if Day 6 is a Friday, watch the sunset from Mehtab Bagh and go in only at dawn on Day 7; if Day 7 is the Friday, you're left with the sunset visit on Day 6, so take it slowly and make it longer.

### Day 7 — The Taj Mahal at dawn and the road to Ranthambore
Be at the gates of the **Taj Mahal** half an hour before dawn, with your ticket on your phone and as little as possible on you. Food, drones and tripods without a permit are not allowed in; big backpacks also slow down the security checks. The first hour is the right one: the marble turns from gray to pink to white, the pool reflects the dome and you can walk up onto the platform without crowds. By the time the tour groups arrive the best light has gone, and you go back to the hotel for breakfast and check-out.

Then you leave with the driver heading southwest, stopping at **Fatehpur Sikri**, Akbar's abandoned capital, less than an hour from Agra: red sandstone palaces, courtyards and the great mosque with the monumental gate of the **Buland Darwaza**. A couple of hours is enough. *Around the mosque you'll be approached by pushy vendors and "guides": a firm no is enough, and if you want a guide, hire one at the official entrance.* The rest is road, through fields and villages. In the late afternoon you reach **Sawai Madhopur**, the town at the gates of **Ranthambore** park, in time for a quiet dinner and to confirm the pick-up time for the next day's safari.

**In practice:** Taj Mahal closed on Fridays · Fatehpur Sikri about 600 rupees · Agra-Ranthambore about 5-6 hours by car with the stop · sleep in the lodges and hotels along the park road, in Sawai Madhopur.

### Day 8 — Ranthambore: safaris among the tigers
Ranthambore is one of the most famous tiger parks in India: dry forest, lakes, ruins and a tenth-century fort on top of a cliff, inside the park. You can only go in on official safaris, in two shifts a day, early morning and afternoon, in two kinds of vehicle: the **gypsy**, a 6-seat jeep, and the **canter**, an open-top truck with about twenty seats, noisier and less nimble.

Here too you have to book well ahead if you want the little jeep (the gypsy) and not the canter. We moved too late and ended up on the canter, but we got lucky anyway: we saw 3 tigers on a single morning safari, plus two little bears running. Always do at least two safaris, partly because the park is split into zones and you can't choose which zone to visit or when: they're assigned at random on the day of the tour.

**In practice:** two safaris, morning and afternoon, or one today and one tomorrow morning · bookings on the Rajasthan Forest Department portal or through an agency, with an ID document: bring the same one to the safari · shift times change with the season: check them when you book · the core zones, 1 to 5, close for the monsoon, from July 1 to September 30 · neutral-colored clothes and a jacket for the cold winter mornings.

### Day 9 — Ranthambore and the road to Jaipur
If you booked a second safari at dawn, this is when you do it: the jeep or canter picks you up at the hotel while it's still dark, and breakfast is waiting when you get back. If you did two safaris the day before, you can go up to **Ranthambore Fort**, a UNESCO site among the hill forts of Rajasthan, with its **Ganesh temple**, before leaving. The fort sits on top of a cliff inside the park and the climb is all steps: do it early, before the heat. *There are lots of monkeys on the stairs and they snatch food from your hands: keep bags of food out of sight.*

It's about three and a half to four hours on the road to **Jaipur**, where you arrive in the afternoon. Leave by late morning, so you don't hit the city at rush hour and still have daylight to settle in. The rest of the day is light: check-in, a walk around the hotel and an early dinner, because the next day you get to Amber Fort at opening time. It's also the right moment to agree the morning departure time with the driver.

**In practice:** Ranthambore-Jaipur about 3.5-4 hours by car · sleep in the old city or in Bani Park · for dinner in Jaipur, our two maharaja dinners are in "Where to eat."

### Day 10 — Jaipur, the Pink City and Amber Fort
A full day for the capital of Rajasthan. Get to **Amber Fort** at opening time, walking up the ramp, for the Hall of Mirrors and the terraces over the lake. Inside the fort there's also the 1135 AD restaurant, where we came back for dinner (see "Where to eat"); then a stop at **Jal Mahal** and the **Panna Meena** stepwell. In the afternoon, the old city: the honeycomb facade of the **Hawa Mahal**, the **City Palace** and the **Jantar Mantar** observatory. Watch the sunset from **Nahargarh Fort**, above the city.

**In practice:** Amber 1,000 rupees for foreigners since 2026, Hawa Mahal, Jantar Mantar and Nahargarh 600 each, City Palace about 700 · the composite ticket is now a 10-day pass for all of Rajasthan's state monuments at 5,500 rupees: only worth it if you visit a lot of them · *nobody sells gems you can resell in Italy at a profit: it's a scam*.

### Day 11 — Jaipur: the bazaars and the road to Pushkar
The morning is for the bazaars, when the shops open and the city is still cool: **Johari Bazar** for jewelry and fabrics, **Bapu Bazar** for embroidered shoes, a block-printing workshop. The old city bazaars run one after another under the pink arcades and can be covered on foot in a couple of hours. You always bargain, starting well below the first price and without hurrying. *Whoever offers to take you to "a cooperative" or a textile factory usually gets a commission on what you buy.*

After lunch you leave for **Pushkar**, about three hours on the road, in time for sunset on the **ghats of the sacred lake**, when the evening chants fill the water with sounds and bells. Pushkar is vegetarian by tradition: no meat, eggs or alcohol in town. It's a small town gathered around the lake and you get around on foot; the car stays on the edge of the center. You go barefoot on the ghats. In the evening, dinner is a vegetarian thali or malpua, the local fried sweets.

**In practice:** Jaipur-Pushkar about 3 hours by car · *the "free" flower for the puja on the ghats is followed by a request for a donation: decline politely* · photograph bathers only if you ask first · sleep in a guesthouse with a terrace on the lake; during the camel fair prices multiply and you book months ahead.

### Day 12 — Pushkar at dawn and the road to Jodhpur
You walk up (or take the cable car) to the **Savitri temple**, on the hill above town, to watch sunrise over the lake and the desert. On foot it's about three quarters of an hour of steps: set off with your phone torch and a bottle of water, and come down before the sun starts to heat up. From the top you see the white town gathered around the lake and, to the west, the first dunes. Then the **Brahma temple**, one of the very few in India dedicated to the creator god, where shoes and bags are left at the entrance, and a last walk through the bazaar.

In the early afternoon you leave for **Jodhpur**, about four and a half hours, arriving for sunset, with the blue houses of the old city stretching toward the desert. The old city lanes are narrow: the car stops on the edge and you finish on foot or by tuk-tuk, with your luggage. On the first evening it's enough to go up to the haveli terrace, when the wall of **Mehrangarh** lights up orange, and have dinner there or nearby. The fort can wait until tomorrow.

**In practice:** climb to the Savitri temple about 45 minutes on foot, paid cable car if you're short of time · Pushkar-Jodhpur about 4-5 hours by car · sleep around Ghanta Ghar or Navchokiya.

### Day 13 — Jodhpur: Mehrangarh and the blue city
**Mehrangarh Fort** is visited in the morning, with the audio guide included in the ticket: palaces of carved sandstone and the museum of weapons, palanquins and miniatures. The fort stands on a spur of rock, with walls up to 36 meters high and seven gates, some with the handprints of the queens. Go up at opening time, before the heat and the groups, and allow at least three hours. Then walk down the path to the **Jaswant Thada** cenotaph, in white marble, with its view of the fort.

The afternoon is for the blue lanes of **Navchokiya** and **Brahmpuri**, among temples, courtyards and stairways, down to the **Toorji ka Jhalra** stepwell, restored and surrounded by cafés: a good place for a break in the shade. *The blue lanes are people's homes: take photos discreetly and ask before photographing anyone.* In the evening the **Ghanta Ghar**, the clock tower with the Sardar market around it, and a makhaniya lassi, the city's thick, spiced lassi.

**In practice:** Mehrangarh about 600 rupees with audio guide · Jaswant Thada separate ticket · the zipline over the walls is paid separately · stepped lanes: no wheeled suitcases.

### Day 14 — Toward Jaisalmer and the night in the Thar Desert
The transfer to **Jaisalmer** is the longest with the driver: about five hours through an ever drier landscape. Leave the big bag at the hotel in Jaisalmer, and in the afternoon head out to the dunes of **Sam** or **Khuri**, 40-50 kilometers away, to sleep in a tented camp. A camel ride at sunset, dinner around the fire with the music of the Manganiyar, the desert musicians, and a night under a sky with very little artificial light.

*The Sam dunes are the most spectacular but also the most crowded, with dozens of camps and jeeps; Khuri is smaller and quieter.* The quality of the camps varies hugely: pick places with recent reviews and a stated policy on animal welfare, and book them yourself instead of letting the driver choose.

**In practice:** Jodhpur-Jaisalmer about 5 hours by car · tented camp with dinner, breakfast and camel roughly 3,000-8,000 rupees per person depending on the category · in the desert at night in December-January it drops below 5 °C.

### Day 15 — Jaisalmer, the golden city
In the morning you come back from the dunes and spend the day in town. **Jaisalmer Fort**, in yellow sandstone, is one of the few in the world that are still inhabited: a few thousand people live inside the walls, among houses, shops, carved Jain temples and hotels. Come back early from the camp and head straight up to the fort, because the **Jain temples** are open to non-Jains only in the morning. You enter through the main gate and walk up the paved lanes to the **royal palace** and the ramparts.

Below the fort are the **Patwon** and Nathmal **havelis**, merchants' mansions decorated like stone lace. See them in the early afternoon, after a break during the hottest hours. At sunset, the fort's ramparts or **Gadisar** lake with its pavilions on the water: the artificial lake that for centuries was the city's water reserve. In the evening, dinner on a terrace with the lit-up fort in front of you.

**In practice:** entry to the fort is free; the royal palace and Jain temples are paid (the temples open to non-Jains only in the morning) · Patwon ki Haveli 100-200 rupees for foreigners depending on the house visited · staying inside the fort contributes to its decay through water use: better a hotel below the walls.

### Day 16 — From Jaisalmer to Jodhpur
A day heading back east: five hours by car from Jaisalmer to **Jodhpur** and a free afternoon in the blue city. Leave after breakfast so you arrive around lunchtime with the whole afternoon ahead. The road crosses the desert with few towns and few stops: water and something to eat in the car are useful. You sleep below the fort again, so the luggage only travels a few meters.

The afternoon is for a stroll through the **Sardar** spice market, **Ranisar** lake at the foot of the fort and dinner on a terrace with Mehrangarh lit up. It's a buffer day, useful even just to wash clothes before the last part of the trip: ask for laundry when you arrive so you get it back the next day. If you skipped something on Day 13, Jaswant Thada or the Toorji ka Jhalra stepwell, now's the time.

**In practice:** if you have fewer days you can fly from Jaisalmer: from late October to late March Air India has daily flights to Delhi and, from 25 October 2026, to Mumbai; in summer the airport closes · desert sand gets everywhere: a bag for your electronics helps · sleep below the fort, around Ghanta Ghar or Navchokiya.

### Day 17 — Ranakpur and arrival in Udaipur
Driving south through the Aravalli hills, you stop at the **Ranakpur Jain temple**, from the fifteenth century, with 1,444 carved marble columns, all different. Non-Jains are allowed in only between midday and the afternoon: leave Jodhpur in the morning to arrive at opening time. Inside, light comes in from the inner courtyards and shifts across the columns as the hours pass. You go in barefoot, with shoulders and legs covered, and take your time: an hour is enough, but it deserves more.

You continue through the hills to **Udaipur**, about two hours away. If you like, you can take the detour to **Kumbhalgarh** fort, with the second longest wall in the world after China's, but it adds a good two hours to the day. *The Aravalli roads are narrow and winding: better to arrive before dark.* You reach Udaipur in the late afternoon, in time for dinner on a terrace over **Lake Pichola**, with the City Palace lit up on the opposite shore.

**In practice:** Jodhpur-Ranakpur about 3 hours, Ranakpur-Udaipur about 2 hours · Ranakpur open to non-Jains from about 12 to 5 pm, no leather items and no food inside · if you like, add Kumbhalgarh fort, with two extra hours.

### Day 18 — Udaipur, the city of lakes
The **City Palace**, built over four centuries on the shore of the Pichola, with its peacock courtyards and balconies over the lake, is best visited at opening time, when the rooms are still quiet: allow a couple of hours. Then the **Jagdish temple**, a few steps away at the top of a stairway, and the **Gangaur** and **Ambrai** ghats in the morning, when women wash clothes and the city wakes up on the water. During the hottest hours, take a break at the hotel or on a shaded terrace.

In the afternoon, the boat trip on **Lake Pichola**, stopping at **Jag Mandir**, the palace on the island, and sunset from the Monsoon Palace at **Sajjangarh** or from a terrace. Sajjangarh is on a hill outside town: you go up by car or tuk-tuk, leaving an hour's margin before sunset. In the evening, if you like, there's the traditional dance show at **Bagore ki Haveli**, at Gangaur Ghat. It's also the last day with the driver: you settle the bill and the tip. *Before paying, double-check kilometers, tolls and advances already paid, in writing.*

**In practice:** City Palace from 400 rupees (museum only) to about 600-700 for foreigners · boats from the City Palace jetty or Lal Ghat, pricier at sunset than during the day · Bagore ki Haveli evening show, paid.

### Day 19 — From Udaipur to Delhi and departure
A last quiet morning in Udaipur, perhaps with breakfast by the lake, then back to **Delhi** for the international connection, often overnight. If your flight is in the afternoon, there's time for a last walk along the ghats and some light shopping for the suitcase. Udaipur airport is about half an hour from the center: leave with a margin, because traffic stretches the times.

The most convenient option is the domestic flight, about an hour and a half. *In Delhi the domestic flight may arrive at a different terminal from your long-haul flight: check your ticket and allow time to transfer and go through security again.* If your flight to Europe leaves in the early hours of the next day, take a room near the airport, in **Aerocity**, for a few hours' rest. Spend any leftover rupees before leaving, because outside India they're hard to change.

**In practice:** Udaipur airport about 30 minutes from the center · Udaipur-Delhi flight 4,000-8,000 rupees if booked early · leave at least 4-5 hours between the domestic flight's arrival and the long-haul flight, because of frequent winter delays.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **Delhi:** Karol Bagh or Connaught Place in the mid-range, South Delhi for quiet, Aerocity near the airport for the last night or for late-night arrivals. For the first night it's worth staying near a metro station, since the next evening you leave by train from New Delhi.
- **Varanasi:** on the **ghats** or in the alleys just behind, between Assi and Dashashwamedh: budget guesthouses from 1,500 rupees, heritage hotels in havelis on the river in the mid and high range. Chain hotels in the new city (Cantonment) are comfortable but far from the river, and the river is the reason to be here.
- **Agra:** Taj Ganj and Fatehabad Road, walking distance from the east gate.
- **Ranthambore:** lodges and hotels along the park road in Sawai Madhopur, many with safari pickup included.
- **Jaipur:** we can't recommend the **Jaipur Heritage Palace** enough, it's amazing: it's cheap and the service is super attentive, with an over-the-top breakfast at its sister property, and it's really, really good. In general, havelis and heritage hotels in the old city or Bani Park.
- **Pushkar:** guesthouses with a terrace on the lake; during the camel fair prices multiply and you need to book months ahead.
- **Jodhpur:** havelis below the fort, Ghanta Ghar and Navchokiya.
- **Jaisalmer:** hotels in the havelis below the fort; a tented camp at Sam or Khuri for the night in the desert.
- **Udaipur:** Lal Ghat and Gangaur Ghat in the budget and mid-range, Ambrai and Hanuman Ghat for the view; palace hotels on the lake at the luxury end.
- **With a driver:** better to book the hotels yourself and ask whether they have a room for drivers. If the driver "knows a better place," he often just knows a better commission.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **Varanasi:** thick lassi with fruit, kachori sabzi in the morning, tamatar chaat (tomato chaat, a specialty of the city), malaiyo (saffron milk foam) in winter, Banaras paan after dinner. Many restaurants on the ghats are vegetarian; near the Kashi Vishwanath you won't find meat.
- **Delhi and Agra:** parathas, kebabs, chaat, Punjabi and Mughlai cooking, petha in Agra.
- **On the overnight trains:** you can order a meal on board or have one delivered at some stations through IRCTC's e-catering services; otherwise bring fruit, cookies and water.
- **Jaipur, the maharaja dinners:** for dinners, we went full maharaja. At **[Jamavar](https://www.theleela.com/the-leela-palace-jaipur/restaurants/jamavar)**, the Indian restaurant at The Leela Palace Jaipur: expensive, and booking takes a deposit, but it's worth every bit. And at **1135 AD**, the restaurant inside Amber Fort: very good, and the setting is incredibly atmospheric. Afterward we were invited to see the maharajas' private dining room, on the top floor, where they still have dinner and receive important guests.
- **Jaipur and Jodhpur, the food:** dal baati churma, laal maas, ker sangri, mirchi bada, pyaaz kachori and makhaniya lassi.
- **On the street:** during the day we always ate street food. If you're careful you can go for it, always with an eye on hygiene. Strange but true, our stomachs were fine the whole time (lucky us).
- **Pushkar:** vegetarian thalis and malpua, with no meat or alcohol.
- **Jaisalmer and the desert:** Thar cooking with dried legumes, desert berries, millet bread (bajra roti); in the tented camps, buffet dinner.
- **Udaipur:** lakeside terraces for the view, the thali houses of the old city for the food.
- **On the road:** drivers often stop at "tourist" restaurants where they eat for free. You can ask for a regular dhaba.
- **Golden rules for street food:** only bottled water with an intact seal, and no ice unless you know where it comes from; food cooked in front of you and served piping hot, not lukewarm or left sitting out; fruit only if you peel it yourself; stalls crowded with locals, where the food turns over fast. Wash your hands or use hand gel before eating. And no bathing in the Ganges and no river water, not even "blessed."

## Getting around
- **Delhi-Varanasi overnight train:** about 10-11 hours. The Shiv Ganga Express leaves New Delhi around 8:05 p.m. and gets to Banaras around 6:10 a.m., every day; the Swatantrata Senani Express leaves around 9:15 p.m. and gets to Varanasi Junction around 8:05 a.m. If you'd rather travel by day there's the Vande Bharat, about 8 hours seated; the flight is about an hour and a half, plus an hour from the airport to the ghats.
- **Varanasi-Agra overnight train:** the Marudhar Express (numbers 14853, 14863 or 14865 depending on the day) leaves Varanasi City around 4:30-4:40 p.m. and arrives at Agra Fort around 6:35 a.m., about 14 hours; it then continues to Jaipur and Jodhpur. Alternatively, fly Varanasi-Delhi and take a fast train to Agra.
- **The classes:** they're explained in the practical info. For two nights, 2A and 3A are the most common choices; 1A sells out fast.
- **Booking:** on irctc.co.in with a foreigner account, activated with an SMS code on your Italian number; you pay with an international card (the gateway that accepts foreign cards sometimes declines the payment: try again). We went through [12Go](https://12go.asia), which accepts foreign cards without registration and only sells confirmed tickets, with a markup. Regular bookings on IRCTC open 60 days ahead. Tatkal, the last-day quota, has required an account verified with Aadhaar, the Indian ID, since July 2025: for a foreigner it's pretty much out of reach.
- **Foreign tourist quota:** on a small number of trains there's a small block of seats reserved for foreigners (Foreign Tourist Quota), often just two or a few more. You need your passport and it costs a bit more. It's useful when the general quota is sold out, not as a first choice.
- **Wait list:** a **WL** (waiting list) ticket doesn't get you a berth: if it's still waitlisted when the charts are prepared, a few hours before departure, the e-ticket cancels itself and is refunded, minus a small fee. An **RAC** ticket lets you board, but you share a berth with another passenger until one frees up. Check the status with your PNR number right up to the end.
- **Luggage safety:** theft is rare but it happens. A chain or padlock to lock your pack under the berth, documents, money and phone on you even while you sleep. The upper berth is more private and out of the way. At the station, watch out for pickpockets.
- **Car with driver:** from Agra to Udaipur, meaning Agra-Ranthambore, Ranthambore-Jaipur, Jaipur-Pushkar-Jodhpur, Jodhpur-Jaisalmer and back, Jodhpur-Ranakpur-Udaipur. The Thar dunes are reached by the camp's jeep or with the driver. Roughly 2,800-4,000 rupees a day for all of Rajasthan, tolls and parking to be clarified, tip at the end of the trip.
- **In Varanasi:** you walk along the ghats and in the alleys; boats connect the ghats by river; tuk-tuks and e-rickshaws for the longer distances to the station, the new city and Sarnath.
- **Getting home:** Udaipur-Delhi flight, about 1 hour 30 minutes.

## How to choose a driver in Rajasthan
Our driver was a total loose cannon, and I wouldn't recommend him. I'm not naming him. Here, instead, is how to pick a good one.
- **Book through a registered agency.** India's Ministry of Tourism publishes lists of approved tour operators and tourist transport operators, state by state. A registered agency has an address, answers if something goes wrong and can swap the driver. The hotel for your first night in Agra can also recommend a service it's worked with for years.
- **Read recent reviews**, from the last year, and look for the ones that mention the driver by name, not just the agency. Big agencies pay for advertising; reviews tell you how the person driving you actually drives.
- **Ask for two or three quotes** for the same itinerary, and compare them line by line.
- **Clear things up beforehand, in writing:** whether the price is per day or per kilometer, how many kilometers a day are included and how much each extra kilometer costs; how many hours a day the driver drives; whether the allowance for the driver's nights (the "bata," which covers his food and lodging) is included; who pays for fuel, tolls, parking and interstate entry taxes; whether the car has air conditioning and whether the driver stays the same for the whole trip.
- **A written contract**, even just an email, with the itinerary, the stops and everything that's included. Pay a modest deposit, not everything up front.
- **No shop stops.** Say it beforehand, and put it in writing: no mandatory stops at carpet, gem, textile or marble shops. Many drivers take a commission on whatever you buy. If he stops anyway, stay in the car or get out without buying.
- **Note down his name, phone number and license plate** and send them to someone before the long legs.
- **If something's wrong,** call the agency right away. Dangerous driving, speeding, phone in hand at the wheel, alcohol: ask him to stop and insist on another driver. No night transfers and seat belt always, in the back too.
- **The tip** goes at the end of the trip, if he's earned it.

## When to go, month by month
- **October:** end of the monsoon, the Ganges is still high and some low ghats are flooded; hot by day. A good month for Rajasthan.
- **November:** the best month. Diwali (November 8 in 2026), Dev Deepawali in Varanasi with all the ghats lit up (November 24 in 2026), the Pushkar camel fair around the Kartik full moon. Cities and trains are full: book early.
- **December-January:** high season, mild days and cold mornings, thick fog in Varanasi and between Delhi and Agra, with overnight train delays of many hours. Nights at or near freezing in the desert.
- **February:** ideal weather everywhere, less fog.
- **March:** the heat begins; Holi (March 22 in 2027) is spectacular in Varanasi and Pushkar, but go into it with caution, especially as a woman.
- **April-June:** extreme heat, 40-45 °C and more in the Thar. Not recommended.
- **July-September:** monsoon. The Ganges rises, floods the ghats and boats may be suspended; the aarti moves up to the terraces. Udaipur is green and the lakes are full.

## Common scams and safety
- **Varanasi, wood for the poor:** at Manikarnika a man offers to explain the cremations, takes you up to a terrace and then asks for a donation for wood for the sick in the hospice. Watch on your own, from a distance, and say no.
- **Varanasi, boats:** fix the price before getting on, per boat and not per person, and with the duration. The rower may stop halfway and ask for more.
- **Varanasi, "blessings":** the sadhu who poses for photos or the kid who puts a red mark on your forehead will ask for money afterward. A small offering is normal if you choose to photograph a sadhu, but ask first.
- **Varanasi, silk:** the "weavers'" shops where tuk-tuk drivers and guides take you often sell synthetic silk at high prices.
- **New Delhi station:** someone stops tourists before the stairs saying the train is canceled or the foreigners' ticket office is closed. It's not true: your e-ticket is enough, and you can check the status on the app.
- **"Your hotel is closed" and fake tourist offices in Delhi:** as everywhere in India, you answer by calling the hotel, and you don't walk into agencies that claim to be official.
- **Jaipur gems and Pushkar flowers:** two classics, both to turn down.
- **Drivers and shops:** the "courtesy" stops at carpet and textile shops are there for the driver's commission. You can refuse them.
- **On the overnight train:** luggage locked with a padlock or chain, valuables on you, be wary of food or drinks offered by strangers.
- **Women traveling:** covered clothing, booked transfers for night arrivals, the upper berth on the train (more private), women's lines at security checks. In the alleys of Varanasi and in the aarti crowd, harassment and groping can happen: better to have company after sunset.
- **The road:** don't drive yourself, no night transfers by car, seat belt always.
- **Terrorism alert:** Viaggiare Sicuri reports high vigilance in Delhi and Rajasthan in crowded places, such as markets, stations and temples; it's a good idea to register on Dovesiamonelmondo before leaving.

## Variations
- **12-13 day version:** drop Jaisalmer and the desert (days 14-16) and go straight from Jodhpur to Udaipur. You still get Varanasi, Agra, Ranthambore, Jaipur, Pushkar, Jodhpur and Udaipur.
- **Without Ranthambore, 17 days:** from Agra you go straight to Jaipur in 5-6 hours, with Fatehpur Sikri and the Chand Baori stepwell in Abhaneri along the way.
- **23 day version:** add Bundi and Chittorgarh between Pushkar and Udaipur, or a night at Kumbhalgarh, and one more night in Varanasi for Ramnagar and a music or cooking lesson.
- **With Khajuraho:** between Varanasi and Agra, the temples of Khajuraho (UNESCO heritage, famous for their sculptures) can be reached from Varanasi, then on by train toward Agra or Jhansi; it takes one more night.
- **Without overnight trains:** if you don't want to sleep on a train, fly from Delhi to Varanasi and from Varanasi to Delhi, then take the fast train to Agra. You lose the nights in a berth and add two hotel nights.
- **Reverse order:** you can do Rajasthan first and finish in Varanasi, with an overnight train or a flight from Varanasi to Delhi on the last day.
- **Rajasthan only:** a short ten-day version, built on this trip, is in the guide *India: Rajasthan in 10 Days, from Delhi and the Taj Mahal to the Lakes of Udaipur*.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Choose the driver with judgment**, through a registered agency, with recent reviews and a written contract. Ours was a total loose cannon: this isn't something to leave to chance.
- **Put it in the contract: no shop stops.** And clear up kilometers, hours, the driver's nights, tolls and meals beforehand.
- **The Taj Mahal is closed on Fridays:** plan everything around it, trains and driver included, to avoid trouble. If Day 6 or Day 7 falls on a Friday, you lose one of the two Taj visits: see Day 6.
- **Book Ranthambore well ahead** if you want the gypsy, the 6-seat jeep, and not the canter. And always do at least two safaris: the zones are assigned at random, and you don't get to choose which one you see.
- **The Taj Mahal twice:** we did it at sunset and again at dawn the next day. There are lines at security: bring the bare minimum and, if you can, no backpacks, to speed everything up.
- **Think about the trains months ahead:** we booked them in November for late February and still had a hard time getting everything. On IRCTC booking opens 60 days ahead: mark the date and book that day, with a plan B (a flight) if the ticket stays on the wait list.
- **Check your ticket status (PNR)** the day before and the morning of departure.
- **Bring a chain or padlock** for your luggage on the train.
- **Check the other closing days too:** the Sarnath museum is also closed on Fridays.
- **In Varanasi sleep on the river**, not in the new city: the river at dawn is the reason for the trip.
- **Get to the aarti an hour early** and keep your bag in front.
- **Never photograph cremations** or grieving families.
- **Don't bathe in the Ganges** and don't drink the river water.
- **Leave margin for the trip home:** in December-January fog causes delays of hours. No intercontinental flight on the same day as a domestic one without at least 4-5 hours of margin.
- **Choose the desert camp yourself:** the cheapest is rarely the best, for hygiene and for the animals.

## Chiusura personale
We did India in late February, Varanasi and then Rajasthan. I remember the two nights in a sleeper berth, which in the end weren't as bad as we'd thought (toilets aside, you get the idea). The Taj Mahal seen twice, at sunset and again at dawn the next day. And the morning safari at Ranthambore: we'd booked late and ended up on the canter, but we saw three tigers and two little bears running.

Then Jaipur, with the maharaja dinners at Jamavar and at 1135 AD, where at the end they invited us up to see the maharajas' private dining room on the top floor. During the day we always ate street food and, strange but true, our stomachs were fine the whole time. The driver, on the other hand, was a loose cannon: I wouldn't recommend him.

One thing to factor in: by Western standards people can come across as pushy. But it's an incredible country and an incredible trip, one that gives you a lot and makes you appreciate so many things we often take for granted.
