# Malaysia and Singapore in Two Weeks: From Kuala Lumpur to the Strait, Eating All the Way

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 in progress (itinerary and practical information complete and verified)
**Periodo del viaggio:**
**Durata:** 14 days, 13 nights
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independent, using intercity buses, one domestic flight and the border bus to Singapore
**Categoria:** Asia · Malesia · Singapore · gastronomia · città coloniali · giungla

---

## Apertura personale
Malaysia is the most underrated country in Southeast Asia and probably the easiest one to travel in: good roads, buses that run on time, English spoken everywhere, low prices, and none of the complications that cost you your first few days elsewhere. It's also, without much competition, the place where you eat best in the whole region, because three communities, Malay, Chinese and Indian, have lived side by side in the same cities for generations without any of them watering down its own cooking. This itinerary heads down the west coast, from the capital to the strait, and ends in Singapore: fourteen days that take you from a vertical city to a rainforest 130 million years old, from a highland where you need a sweatshirt to two UNESCO colonial ports, and you eat five times a day without ever spending more than ten euros a meal.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** this itinerary follows the **west coast**, so the window is **December to March**. The rule that confuses everyone is that Malaysia's two coasts have opposite monsoons: the east coast and the Perhentian Islands run from May to September and **almost completely shut down from November to February**. Choosing the coast comes before choosing the dates.
- **Documents, Malaysia:** passport with 6 months of remaining validity, no visa for Italian citizens for up to 90 days, but the **MDAC (Malaysia Digital Arrival Card)** has been mandatory since 2024: a free online declaration to fill in within the three days before arrival, for any point of entry. You do it on the official Immigration portal: the sites that charge for it are middlemen.
- **Documents, Singapore:** same logic. No visa for up to 90 days, but the **SG Arrival Card** is mandatory and must be submitted within the three days before arrival on the ICA portal or through the MyICA app. **You need it even when entering by land from Malaysia**, and that's exactly what people arriving by bus from Malacca forget.
- **Money:** ringgit in Malaysia, Singapore dollars in Singapore. In Malaysia cards work in hotels and restaurants but **cash is essential at food courts and street stalls**, which means the best part of the trip. In Singapore it's the opposite: you pay by contactless card even at the subway turnstiles, with no need to buy a local one.
- **Getting around:** Malaysian intercity buses are comfortable, punctual and cheap: they're the normal way to travel. The ETS train covers the west spine well. Domestic flights cost next to nothing. In the cities, **Grab** solves everything and works with a foreign card.
- **Electricity:** **type G** sockets, the British three-pin rectangular kind, in both Malaysia and Singapore. Italian plugs don't fit: you need the right adapter, not the European one.
- **Health:** no mandatory vaccinations, but **dengue is present all year round, even in cities**: repellent is the real prophylaxis. Tap water isn't safe to drink in Malaysia; in Singapore it is.
- **Context:** Malaysia is a Muslim country with large Chinese and Indian communities. In the conservative areas of the east coast, covered clothing is the expected norm and alcohol is hard to find; in the Chinese and Indian neighborhoods everything changes. During Ramadan many Malay places close in the daytime, but the evening bazaars are one of the best food moments of the year.
- **Laws:** in both countries **drug penalties are among the harshest in the world**, with the death penalty for trafficking and no exceptions for foreigners. In Singapore the fines should be taken literally too: eating or drinking on the subway, smoking outside permitted areas, littering.
- **Rough budget:** in Malaysia you can comfortably stay under €50 a day all-in. Singapore only upends the math on lodging (€100-180 a night) and alcohol: transport and hawker centers stay very cheap.
- **From the airport to the centre:** from **Kuala Lumpur (KUL, terminals 1 and 2)** the KLIA Ekspres reaches KL Sentral in about 30 minutes (55 ringgit, 65 from 1 January 2027; 10% off on the official app or website, or a contactless card at the gate; trains from about 5am until past midnight) · bus to KL Sentral about 15 ringgit, an hour or more · Grab about 70-100 ringgit, up to an hour in traffic · taxis only with the prepaid coupon from the counter: whoever approaches you in arrivals offering a ride is not an official taxi · at **Singapore Changi (SIN)**, for the departure, the MRT (green line, change at Tanah Merah) costs about 2-3 SGD with a contactless card and runs from about 5.30am to 11.30pm; taxi and Grab about 25-45 SGD, plus the airport surcharge (up to 8 SGD) and 50% after midnight.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Arrival in Kuala Lumpur
We landed at KUL and took the **KLIA Ekspres**, which gets you downtown in 28 minutes. We settled in between Bukit Bintang and Chinatown, close to a monorail or subway stop. The train arrives at **KL Sentral**, where you continue by subway, monorail or Grab for the last stretch; if you land at KLIA2 with a low-cost airline you take the same train, with a few extra minutes. Keep the afternoon free: the humid heat of the first day weighs more than the jet lag. *Immigration may ask for the MDAC you filled in online: have the confirmation ready on your phone.*

The first evening we went to **Jalan Alor**, the night food market: you eat outdoors on plastic stools, with Chinese cooking on your right, Malay on your left and an Indian stall in front of you. It's the right way to start this trip, because it explains the country in a single evening. The street comes alive after sunset and stays open late. The restaurants with tables on the street cost a bit more than the stalls, which remain the better choice: order from several places and share, tasting a little of everything, from satay to grilled fish to cut fruit. *Smell a durian before you buy it: many hotels forbid bringing it into the room.*

**In practice:** KLIA Ekspres about 55 ringgit one way (65 from January 2027), a train every 15-20 minutes · Grab from the airport about an hour with traffic · Jalan Alor from evening until late, cash at the stalls · sleep between Bukit Bintang and Chinatown, near the monorail or subway.

### Day 2 — Batu Caves and the neighborhoods
We went to the **Batu Caves** **at dawn**, taking the KTM Komuter train from the central station. At seven the 272 colorful steps are still in shade and you climb alongside the faithful; three hours later it's a scorching staircase with monkeys snatching bottles out of people's hands. *Shoulders and knees covered, and nothing in your hands.* The caves are a working Hindu shrine, with the huge golden statue of **Murugan** at the foot of the stairs and the Temple Cave at the top, where light falls through an opening in the rock. The train stops right at the entrance, so no taxi is needed, and by around ten you're back in the city, ahead of the heat and the tour buses.

We spent the afternoon in the neighborhoods: **Chinatown** around Petaling Street, **Little India** in Brickfields and the **Islamic Arts Museum**, one of the best of its kind in the world and almost always empty. The Petronas Towers are best seen from below at blue hour: going up to the skybridge costs money and gives back less than it promises. The museum is near the National Mosque and the Perdana park gardens, and the hottest hours are the right ones to spend indoors, among ceramics, manuscripts and models of mosques. Toward evening, head to **KLCC Park** at the foot of the towers to watch them light up.

**In practice:** Batu Caves free entry, open from early morning to evening · KTM Komuter from KL Sentral about 35-40 minutes, a few ringgit · Islamic Arts Museum open daily about 9:30am-6pm, ticket about 20 ringgit · Petronas skybridge about 80 ringgit for foreigners, closed on Mondays, best booked online.

### Day 3 — To Taman Negara
Transfer toward the park: a minibus to Kuala Tembeling and then a **longboat up the Tembeling River**, two to three hours against the current between two walls of green. It's the best part of the arrival and worth the trip on its own. You leave Kuala Lumpur early in the morning, by bus to **Jerantut** in about three and a half hours, then a short minivan ride to the jetty. Many agencies in the capital sell a single bus-plus-boat package, handy because it lines up the timetables. *Boat departures depend on the river level and have been cut back in recent years: check them beforehand. There's also a direct minivan by road to Kuala Tahan, faster and far less beautiful.*

We checked in at Kuala Tahan, on the bank across from the entrance, where you eat dinner in the **floating restaurants** moored on the river. Kuala Tahan is a village of a few streets, with guesthouses, small shops and the offices where you book the next days' activities. To enter the park you cross the river on the little boats that shuttle back and forth for small change, and you buy the permit at the headquarters. It's worth doing this the evening you arrive, together with booking the night walk, so the next morning you're ready when the walkway opens. *An ATM in the village isn't a given: arrive with the cash you need.*

**In practice:** bus Kuala Lumpur-Jerantut about 3.5 hours, then minivan or taxi to Kuala Tembeling jetty · boat about 2-3 hours, variable times · bus-plus-boat packages from Kuala Lumpur about 80-150 ringgit · park permit and camera fee a few ringgit at the headquarters · bring cash.

### Day 4 — The 130-million-year-old forest
**Canopy walkway at opening time**: it's one of the longest suspended walkways in the world, forty meters up, and you cross it one at a time: later in the day you stand in line on a span that sways.
From the headquarters you reach it on foot along a forest trail of twenty minutes or a little more. Being first also pays off for the light, which comes in sideways between the trunks in the morning, and for the birds, more active in the early hours. Afternoon on **Bukit Teresek**, or a boat trip upriver to the Lata Berkoh rapids: the first is a short but steep climb to a viewpoint over the forest, the second a place to swim in the river.

In the evening there's the **guided night walk**, which almost everyone skips out of tiredness and which is the best part: by day the forest is quiet and you can't see anything, at night it's deafeningly loud and with a flashlight you pick out spiders, snakes and eyes shining among the leaves. You head out after dinner with the guide for a couple of hours; you need a headlamp, long sleeves and closed shoes, and you walk slowly, stopping often. *In the wet season you need anti-leech socks: they climb up from your feet and it's no small detail.* Wear repellent during the day too, and always carry more water than you think.

**In practice:** canopy walkway usually open from morning to early afternoon, ticket a few ringgit, closed one day a week and in bad weather (check at the headquarters) · guided night walk bookable in Kuala Tahan, a few tens of ringgit · get anti-leech socks before going in · at least two liters of water each.

### Day 5 — To the Cameron Highlands
Transfer up to the highland, with the last stretch on a winding mountain road that anyone prone to carsickness will feel a lot. Direct tourist minivans leave Kuala Tahan for the Cameron Highlands, to be booked in the village the day before: the trip takes most of the day, with a couple of stops along the way. Take the first minivan and bring water and something to eat. *If you get carsick, take the pill before the hairpin bends, not after, and ask for the front seat.*

Arrival at **Tanah Rata** at 1,500 meters, where the temperature drops below 68°F: it's the only point of the trip where you need a sweatshirt, and after days of equatorial heat the relief is physical. Dinner is **steamboat**, the pot of boiling broth in the middle of the table, which you eat here because it's cold. Tanah Rata is the most convenient base in the highlands: the main street has the bus station, the tour agencies and almost all the restaurants. The evening is the time to choose the next day's tour, asking precisely what it includes.

**In practice:** minivan Kuala Tahan-Tanah Rata in a day, book in Kuala Tahan · in the evening the temperature drops to around 59°F: sweatshirt and light jacket · sleep in Tanah Rata · book the plantations and mossy forest tour the evening before.

### Day 6 — Tea plantations and the mossy forest
Morning among the **plantations**, with green terraces rolling down the hills and the tea room overlooking the valley, a colonial legacy that doesn't feel out of place here. The most visited is **Sungei Palas**, run by BOH, with its terrace hanging over the rows: arrive early, before the tour buses come up, and walk down among the bushes to see the picking up close. Tea comes with scones, a habit left by the British. Morning is also the best time for the light, because from early afternoon clouds and rain often roll over the highlands.

Then the **mossy forest** on Gunung Brinchang, a dwarf forest at 2,000 meters completely covered in moss. *Ask ahead whether the tour goes to the real forest or to the boardwalk next to the tower's parking lot: the cheapest tours do the second.* Since 2022 you can only enter with a licensed guide, and since autumn 2025 the main boardwalk has been closed after a landslide: before booking, ask whether it has reopened. The trail is muddy and slippery even when it isn't raining, so you need shoes with good grip. You're back in Tanah Rata in the afternoon, and if you still have energy you can walk one of the numbered trails that start from town.

**In practice:** BOH Sungei Palas open about 8:30am-4:30pm, closed on Mondays, free entry · half-day jeep tour with a guide from Tanah Rata, a few tens of ringgit · mossy forest only with a licensed guide · frequent afternoon rain: rain jacket in your daypack.

### Day 7 — To Penang
Buses and minivans run direct from Tanah Rata to Penang, about four to five hours: you come down from the highlands, cross the bridge over the strait and reach the island in the early afternoon. We moved on to **George Town**, a UNESCO World Heritage site. It's best to sleep inside the historic perimeter, ideally in a restored shophouse: you get around everything on foot and it's the only way to see the city in the evening. There are many shophouses turned into guesthouses but they have few rooms: book ahead, especially for weekends and Chinese New Year.

We had the first dinner at the stalls: **assam laksa**, the sour broth of mackerel and tamarind that throws you off at the first taste and that you go looking for again by the third. In George Town you eat at hawker centres and along the streets where the stalls set up at sunset, and from the historic center there's always one a few steps away. You order the dish by name, pay at the stall and sit wherever there's room, often at shared tables. An evening walk among the lit-up shophouses also helps you get your bearings for tomorrow's day on foot.

**In practice:** bus or minivan Tanah Rata-George Town about 4-5 hours, with morning and early-afternoon departures · sleep inside the UNESCO perimeter, booking early · cash for the stalls · Grab for longer trips.

### Day 8 — George Town on foot
We walked the historic center: the **Peranakan shophouses**, the **Clan Jetties** (the stilt villages of Chinese families, each belonging to a different clan, still lived in), the **Street of Harmony** with a mosque, a Chinese temple, a Hindu temple and an Anglican church on the same street, and the **Blue Mansion**. Start with the jetties in the morning, when the wooden walkways are quiet; Chew Jetty is the largest. *These are real homes: walk quietly and don't photograph through open doors.* The Blue Mansion can only be visited on the guided tour at set times, and along the way you pass the Armenian Street murals. *Walk the center in the morning or after four: between eleven and three the heat is serious and shade is scarce.*

For the afternoon there's **Kek Lok Si**, the largest Buddhist complex in Malaysia, or the ride up **Penang Hill** on the funicular, for the cool air. Kek Lok Si is in Air Itam, at the foot of the hill, with its tall multi-tiered pagoda and the statue of Guanyin towering over the complex; the funicular station is close by, and the two combine well. At the top of Penang Hill the temperature drops a few degrees and the view over the city and the strait is at its best toward sunset. On weekends the line for the funicular can be long.

**In practice:** Blue Mansion only with a guided tour at set times, check the official website and book · Penang Hill funicular with a return ticket, more expensive for foreigners · Kek Lok Si and Penang Hill reachable by Rapid Penang buses or Grab · walk in the early morning or after 4pm.

### Day 9 — Penang: the food day
It was the day without monuments, which here is the most sensible one. We ate five times in small portions, following the stalls' hours: **char kway teow** tossed over a live flame under the wok, **roti canai** for breakfast with dahl, **nasi kandar** for lunch, iced **cendol** in the afternoon, **rojak** in the evening. Roti canai is eaten early, in the Indian Muslim eateries open to the street; nasi kandar is white rice with different curries poured over it as you choose, and cendol, shaved ice with coconut milk and palm sugar, is what gets you through the afternoon heat.

The mechanism to understand is this: every stall makes one thing only, has done so for thirty years, keeps its own hours and closes when it runs out. Showing up at two at a famous stall and finding it closed is the norm. Alternatively, half a day at **Penang National Park**, the smallest national park in the world, out to Monkey Beach. You take the public bus to Teluk Bahang, register at the entrance and follow the coastal trail through the forest, about an hour and a half; for the way back, fishermen offer boat rides. *Start early, with water and repellent, and no food in sight because of the monkeys.*

**In practice:** many stalls close when they run out: arrive early · dishes from a few ringgit up to about ten · Penang National Park free entry with registration, bus 101 from George Town to Teluk Bahang about an hour · agree on the price of the return boat from Monkey Beach first.

### Day 10 — To Malacca
Then we headed south to **Malacca**, the port that for centuries controlled the most strategic strait in Asia: conquered by the Portuguese in 1511, then Dutch, then British. Three European dominations layered on top of each other within a few blocks. The transfer from Penang takes most of the day: by bus you travel down the whole west coast, direct or changing in Kuala Lumpur, while the ETS train only goes as far as the capital and from there you continue by bus for a couple of hours. Buses arrive at **Melaka Sentral**, a few minutes by Grab from the historic center.

We arrived in the afternoon and took our first walk in the red **Dutch Square** and up **St. Paul's Hill**, where the roofless Portuguese church has Dutch gravestones propped against its walls. The **Stadthuys** and Christ Church face the square; on the other side of the hill stands the **Porta de Santiago**, the only surviving piece of the Portuguese fortress A Famosa. At sunset you walk along the river, when the houses facing the water light up. *Agree on the price of the trishaws decorated with flowers and lights before getting in.*

**In practice:** Penang-Malacca is a full travel day, by direct bus or by ETS to Kuala Lumpur and then a bus from TBS terminal (about 2 hours) · Melaka Sentral a few minutes by Grab from the center · historic center entirely on foot · sleep on the Chinatown side.

### Day 11 — Malacca and Nyonya cooking
In the morning we visited the **Baba & Nyonya Heritage Museum**, a Peranakan house preserved with its original furnishings, then the alleys of Chinatown around **Jonker Street**.
The museum visit is guided, in small groups, and it's worth showing up early. In the nearby lanes is **Cheng Hoon Teng**, the oldest Chinese temple in Malaysia, a few steps from a mosque and a Hindu temple. For lunch, **Nyonya** cooking (ayam pongteh, laksa with coconut milk, chicken rice balls), the cuisine born from the meeting of Chinese merchants and Malay women, which exists almost only here and in Penang.

The hot afternoon suits a break in your room or a boat ride on the river. In the evening, once the day-trippers' tour buses have left, the city changes completely: that's the reason to sleep here rather than pass through. For dinner there's satay celup, skewers you cook yourself in the pot of peanut sauce in the middle of the table, best eaten in company. *If you're there from Friday to Sunday, there's the Jonker Street night market, the best moment and also the most crowded.*

**In practice:** Baba & Nyonya Heritage Museum with guided tours, closed one day a week (check the day and hours on the official website), ticket about 20-25 ringgit · Jonker Street night market Friday, Saturday and Sunday from the evening · satay celup and Nyonya restaurants without booking, but expect lines on weekends.

### Day 12 — To Singapore
**We took the direct Malacca-Singapore bus, 4-5 hours including the border.** You get off twice, when leaving Malaysia and when entering Singapore, and the **SG Arrival Card must already be filled in**: it's the most common mistake for people arriving overland. Buses leave from Melaka Sentral or from stops in town, depending on the company, and arrive at different stops in Singapore: choose one that drops you near your accommodation. The time depends almost entirely on the line at the Woodlands border. *Friday evenings, long weekends and the eves of public holidays are the times to avoid.*

To sleep, Chinatown or Bugis, which are central and much cheaper than Marina Bay. The first evening we went to a **hawker centre**: you look for the stall with a line and join it. Hainanese chicken to start. In Chinatown, **Maxwell Food Centre** is the most convenient, but every neighborhood has its own. To get around, a contactless card at the subway gates is all you need. *A packet of tissues left on a table means the seat is taken, and it should be respected.*

**In practice:** Malacca-Singapore bus about 4-5 hours with the border, book at least the day before · SG Arrival Card online on the ICA portal or the MyICA app within the three days before · subway with a contactless card · hawker centre dishes about 4-8 Singapore dollars.

### Day 13 — Singapore: the neighborhoods and the bay
We spent the morning between **Chinatown**, **Little India** and **Kampong Glam**: they're two subway stops apart and nothing alike. The smells change, the music, the language on the street. In Chinatown there's the **Buddha Tooth Relic Temple** and **Sri Mariamman**, the city's oldest Hindu temple; in Little India the covered Tekka market and the flower garland stalls; in Kampong Glam the **Sultan Mosque** and Haji Lane. Morning is the right time, before the heat builds. *Places of worship require covered shoulders and knees, and at the mosque you respect prayer times.*

In the late afternoon we went to **Marina Bay**, walking around the bay at blue hour, and in the evening to **Gardens by the Bay** for the light show at the Supertrees: it lasts a quarter of an hour, it's free, and you watch it lying on the lawn with hundreds of other people. *Don't pay for the skyscraper's observation deck: order a cocktail at the bar in the same building.* The two climate-controlled conservatories of the Gardens, the Flower Dome and the Cloud Forest, charge admission and are best visited in the late afternoon, before the show.

**In practice:** Garden Rhapsody free at the Supertrees, two evening shows at 7:45pm and 8:45pm · Flower Dome and Cloud Forest paid, tickets online · Sultan Mosque with covered clothing, closed to visitors during Friday prayers · a few minutes by subway between neighborhoods.

### Day 14 — Green Singapore and departure
The morning almost nobody puts on the plan: the **Botanic Gardens** at opening time, a UNESCO site, full of locals jogging and doing tai chi: a park that's used rather than visited. The gardens open at five in the morning and are free; only the **National Orchid Garden**, which opens later, charges admission. Or the **MacRitchie TreeTop Walk**, a suspension bridge over the rainforest twenty minutes by subway from the financial district, with macaques on the trails and monitor lizards crossing. From the station you walk to the start of the trails and then a couple of hours through the reserve, so set off early. *The TreeTop Walk is closed on Mondays.*

We flew out of Changi, one of the few airports in the world where it's worth arriving early. The reason is **Jewel**, the complex linked to the terminals with its huge indoor waterfall, indoor gardens and restaurants, open even to those who haven't checked in yet. You can leave your bags at the left-luggage desk, eat one last time and go up to the terminals calmly: Changi's checks are fast, but at Jewel time flies. From the center you get there by subway in under an hour.

**In practice:** Botanic Gardens open 5am to midnight, free entry, National Orchid Garden paid · TreeTop Walk Tuesday-Friday 9am-5pm, weekends and public holidays 8:30am-5pm, closed on Mondays, last entry 4:45pm · Changi by subway from the center in under an hour · Jewel open to non-passengers too.

## What to see / what to do
- **Batu Caves at seven in the morning**, never at noon.
- **The night walk at Taman Negara**, more than the canopy walkway.
- **Ask which mossy forest** before booking the tour in the Cameron Highlands.
- **George Town on stall hours**, not museum hours.
- **The Clan Jetties**, which are lived in and not an attraction.
- **Sleep in Malacca**, for the evening.
- **The hawker center with the longest line**, always.
- **The Supertree show**, free, stretched out on the lawn.
- **Pulau Ubin or MacRitchie**, for the Singapore nobody expects.

## Possible extensions
- **East coast islands (Perhentian, +3-4 days):** Malaysia's real sea, with turtles you run into snorkeling from the shore. **It only works from May to September**: from November to February the monsoon closes the islands and stops the ferries. If your trip falls in that window, the itinerary needs flipping: east coast instead of west coast.
- **Langkawi (+3 days):** the west coast alternative, open in the same season as this itinerary. The sea is mediocre and murky, but the mangrove tour at Kilim and the Sky Bridge are worth the stop, and since it's a duty-free port, alcohol costs a third as much.
- **Borneo (+10-12 days):** two and a half hours by air from Kuala Lumpur, and it's a trip of its own. The site has a dedicated itinerary.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **Kuala Lumpur:** Bukit Bintang for convenience, Chinatown for prices and character. The rule is to stay near a monorail stop.
- **Taman Negara:** Kuala Tahan, on the bank across from the entrance, with the floating restaurants on the river. Inside the park you sleep better and you're already on the right side in the morning.
- **Cameron Highlands:** Tanah Rata. The rooms have no heating: at night it gets seriously cold and blankets matter more than stars.
- **Penang:** inside the UNESCO perimeter of George Town, in a restored shophouse. Batu Ferringhi has the beaches but cuts you off from the part that counts.
- **Malacca:** in the historic center, on the Chinatown side.
- **Singapore:** Chinatown or Bugis. Marina Bay costs double for being ten minutes by subway from where you can sleep perfectly well anyway.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **Kuala Lumpur:** nasi lemak for breakfast, roti canai with dhal, jianbing from the stalls, charcoal-grilled satay, and Jalan Alor in the evening.
- **Penang:** assam laksa, char kway teow, nasi kandar, cendol, rojak. Five times a day in small portions, following the stalls' hours.
- **Cameron Highlands:** steamboat in the evening because it's cold, tea and scones in the plantation tea rooms.
- **Malacca:** Nyonya cooking (ayam pongteh, Nyonya laksa, chicken rice balls) and satay celup, cooked by the diners in a pot of peanut sauce in the middle of the table.
- **Singapore:** hawker centers, always. Hainanese chicken, Katong laksa, char kway teow, roti prata for breakfast, carrot cake that isn't a cake.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Choose the coast before the dates.** The monsoons on Malaysia's two coasts are opposite, and that shapes everything else.
- **Fill in the MDAC and SG Arrival Card** within the three days before arrival, on the official sites. The second one is needed even when arriving by bus.
- **Bring a type G adapter**, not the European one.
- **Cash in Malaysia** for food courts and street stalls; in Singapore a card is enough.
- **Batu Caves at dawn**, and nothing in your hands because of the monkeys.
- **Never eat around Marina Bay**: you pay double to eat worse than at any hawker center.
- **Don't pay for observation decks** when there's a bar on the same floor.
- **Repellent everywhere**: dengue in Malaysia is present all year, cities included.
- **Remember where you are**: very harsh drug penalties in both countries, real fines in Singapore for things that are trivial elsewhere.

## Chiusura personale
What's left of this trip isn't a monument, which is unusual to say after fourteen days. It's the fact that in a single evening, in Kuala Lumpur or George Town, you can eat Chinese, Malay and Indian at three stalls ten meters apart, and that none of the three cuisines has watered anything down to fit in with the others. That coexistence is neither simple nor idyllic, and it's openly debated in Malaysia, but it's the thing that makes this country different from all its neighbors. Then there's the finish in Singapore, which looks like the opposite and is really the same story taken to the extreme: four official languages, four calendars of holidays, and a system of public food centers that keeps the best food in the city at three euros a plate. You cross Malaysia by eating, and that's not a figure of speech: it's literally the itinerary.
