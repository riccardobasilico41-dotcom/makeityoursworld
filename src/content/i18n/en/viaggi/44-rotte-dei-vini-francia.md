# The Wine Routes of France: Alsace and Champagne in 8 Days

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 work in progress (itinerary and practical information verified)
**Periodo del viaggio:**
**Durata:** 8 days, 7 nights
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independent, by car and by bike in Alsace
**Categoria:** Europa · Francia · vino · Alsazia · Champagne

---

## Apertura personale
Among all the French wine routes, this itinerary combines the two best suited to a first time: Alsace, the most beautiful and the most accessible, and Champagne, the easiest to reach from Paris. Burgundy, harder and more expensive, is left for a later trip.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** **May-June or September-October**
- **Alsace**: wineries often welcome visitors without an appointment, with free or almost free tastings.
- **Champagne**: an appointment is recommended for the big houses, free or paid depending on the house.
- **Designated driver, or spit when tasting**, always.
- **From the airport to the centre:** for Colmar the handiest airport is **EuroAirport Basel-Mulhouse (BSL/MLH)**: from the French sector bus 11 goes to Saint-Louis station in about 10 minutes (about €2.50, 5am to midnight), then a direct TER train to Colmar every half hour, about 40 minutes · from **Strasbourg (SXB)** the shuttle train reaches the central station in 9 minutes, then a TER to Colmar in about 30 · if you rent a car, pick it up at the airport and you're in Colmar in about an hour · on the way back, from Champagne-Ardenne TGV station near Reims, direct TGVs reach **Paris Charles de Gaulle (CDG)** in about 40 minutes · at EuroAirport, exit on the French side, not the Swiss one.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno
### Day 1 — Arrival in Colmar
We started in Alsace, the most accessible of the wine routes. We spent the first day in the old town of **Colmar**, with the Unterlinden Museum and the Isenheim Altarpiece. In the evening, tarte flambée. The **Unterlinden Museum** is best in the morning, in a former Dominican convent: the main hall holds Grünewald's **Isenheim Altarpiece**, one of the masterpieces of sixteenth-century German painting, which deserves at least half an hour on its own.

Then you walk out into the center: **Petite Venise**, with colorful half-timbered houses along the Lauch canal, the tanners' quarter, the Maison Pfister and the old customs house, the Koïfhus. Colmar is also the town of Bartholdi, the sculptor of the Statue of Liberty, whose house museum is here. Late afternoon is the best time for Petite Venise, when the light falls on the façades and the groups have already left.

**In practice:** Unterlinden open 9 a.m. to 6 p.m., closed on Tuesdays, ticket about 14 euros · the old town can only be explored on foot, so leave the car in the parking lots outside · to sleep, Colmar or a village on the Route des Vins, handy for the following days.

### Day 2 — Riquewihr and Eguisheim by bike
We rented bikes and rode between the villages of the **Route des Vins**: Riquewihr, Eguisheim and Kaysersberg. Here the wineries often welcome you without an appointment and tastings are free or almost free: you walk in and chat. The villages are a few kilometers apart, linked by cycle paths and back roads through the vines, but the climbs toward the hills are noticeable: an e-bike makes the day much easier.

Start with **Eguisheim**, the village built in concentric rings around its castle, then head north to **Kaysersberg**, with castle ruins above the houses and its fortified bridge, and finish in **Riquewihr**, the most famous, with the Dolder tower at the top of the main street. *Riquewihr is packed in the middle of the day: better to arrive after five, when the coaches have gone.*

**In practice:** in Colmar you can rent regular bikes and e-bikes for the day, best booked at weekends · the loop of the three villages is around forty kilometers: for less effort, choose two · at tastings you sip and spit, especially if you are cycling.

### Day 3 — Route des Vins to the south
We followed the wine road south, past Colmar, with a tasting at a producer who receives visitors without formality. The designated driver rule always applies, or you spit when tasting. South of Colmar the **Route des Vins** continues for about sixty kilometers to Thann, through villages less known and less touristy than those in the north: **Rouffach**, Pfaffenheim, Soultzmatt, **Guebwiller**.

The road is marked by brown signs and crosses vineyards where many of Alsace's 51 **grands crus** have their name on a sign at the entrance. It is the right day to understand the grapes: riesling, gewürztraminer, pinot gris and muscat, the four noble varieties of Alsace, tasted one after another at the same producer. *Many small wineries close at lunchtime and on Sundays: plan visits for the morning and the afternoon.*

**In practice:** the Route des Vins is about 170 kilometers long from north to south, and the southern stretch takes half a day by car · wineries that welcome visitors usually have a «dégustation» sign at the entrance · if you buy bottles, the tasting is often covered by the purchase.

### Day 4 — Strasbourg
We spent a day in **Strasbourg**, between the cathedral, the Petite France quarter and Alsatian cooking, choucroute included. From Colmar to Strasbourg is about thirty minutes by regional train, so leave the car at the hotel. The morning is for **Notre-Dame Cathedral**, with its pink sandstone façade and the **astronomical clock**: every day at 12:30 the apostles parade, preceded by a short film.

If you have the legs, climb the roughly 330 steps to the platform, from where you see the whole city. In the afternoon **Petite France**, the old quarter of millers and fishermen, with the canals of the Ill, the Ponts Couverts and the Vauban dam, from which you look down on the quarter. A boat tour of the canals goes as far as the European quarter, with the European Parliament and the Council of Europe. In the evening take the train back to Colmar, or stay the night in Strasbourg.

**In practice:** the apostles' parade needs a separate ticket, a few euros, while the cathedral itself is free · the platform has a separate paid entrance, about €10 · choucroute is eaten in the winstubs, the Alsatian taverns, best booked for the evening.

### Day 5 — Transfer to Reims
We moved on to Champagne, and here everything changes: the big houses have to be booked in advance, and sometimes the visit is free, sometimes paid. We spent the afternoon in **Reims**, in the cathedral of the coronations, with Chagall's stained glass. The transfer takes most of the morning: from Alsace to Reims is about four hours by car, or the train from Strasbourg with a change at the Champagne-Ardenne TGV station, in about two hours.

**Reims Cathedral**, where the kings of France were crowned for centuries, is visited in the afternoon: the façade with the **Smiling Angel**, the soaring naves and, in the axial chapel, **Chagall's windows**, all played in blue. Next door is the **Palais du Tau**, the former archbishops' residence, with the coronation treasury. In the evening you eat in the center, around Place Drouet-d'Erlon.

**In practice:** entry to the cathedral is free, the Palais du Tau has a separate ticket · if you travel by car, leave it at the hotel: central Reims is walkable · to sleep, Reims or Épernay, both handy for the houses.

### Day 6 — A Champagne crayère
At one house we went down into the **crayères**, the chalk galleries dug by the Romans, and the tasting came together with an explanation of riddling. In Alsace wine is told through conversation; here you watch a technical process. For a snack, jambon de Reims and pink biscuits. The best-known **crayères** are on the Saint-Nicaise hill, home to houses such as **Pommery**, **Ruinart**, **Taittinger** and **Veuve Clicquot**: the Champagne hillsides, houses and cellars have been a UNESCO World Heritage site since 2015.

The visit lasts about an hour and a half and goes down to twenty or thirty meters underground, where the temperature is constant all year. You see the bottles in the pupitres for **riddling** and learn how the bubbles are born from the second fermentation in the bottle. Plan one visit in the morning and one in the afternoon, with the **Basilica of Saint-Remi** in between, a short distance from the cellars.

**In practice:** visits to the big houses usually cost a few tens of euros and must be booked online days or weeks ahead · it is cool in the galleries even in summer: bring a sweater · Reims pink biscuits are bought at the pastry shop and dipped in Champagne.

### Day 7 — Épernay and the Avenue de Champagne
We went to **Épernay**, where the galleries run under the street itself. Here you can add another house of your choice. From Reims to Épernay is about thirty minutes by car or train. The **Avenue de Champagne** is the avenue of the great houses, with nineteenth-century mansions and kilometers of cellars under the pavement: those of **Moët & Chandon** alone run to about 28 kilometers.

Visits are booked on the houses' websites, while the smaller ones often welcome visitors more informally. In the afternoon you drive up through the vines to **Hautvillers**, the village of Dom Pérignon's abbey, with a view over the whole Marne valley. On the way back you can stop at a small grower, who often receives visitors even without a booking. *In the vineyard hills the roads are narrow and poorly signposted: a navigation app helps more than road maps.*

**In practice:** at Moët & Chandon the basic visit costs about 62 euros and must be booked · the Avenue de Champagne takes an unhurried hour on foot · whoever drives between houses spits at tastings, the others take the train between Reims and Épernay.

### Day 8 — Return
From Reims to Paris it's less than an hour by train. Burgundy is left for the next trip: it's harder and more expensive, with a welcome opposite to the Alsatian one. The last morning is free for whatever was left out: a smaller house, a stroll through the **Boulingrin** covered market in Reims, or a stop at the **Verzenay Lighthouse**, among the vines of the Montagne de Reims, with its wine museum and a wide view over the rows.

The bottles bought over the previous days need packing well, and many houses sell boxes made for travel. If you go home by train there is time for lunch in Reims before leaving. *If you fly, remember the liquid rules: Champagne goes in checked luggage, well protected, or is shipped by the house.*

**In practice:** TGVs between Reims and Paris Est take about forty to fifty minutes · trains to Charles de Gaulle airport also leave from Champagne-Ardenne TGV station · a rental car can be returned in Reims or Paris, often with a fee for dropping it in a different city.

## What to see / what to do
- The Route des Vins d'Alsace by bike.
- A Champagne crayère dug by the Romans.
- The cathedral of Reims.

## Possible extensions
- **Burgundy (+3-4 days):** with appointments made weeks ahead.
- **Paris (+2-3 days):** a natural connection from Reims.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
Colmar or a village on the Route des Vins for Alsace; Reims or Épernay for Champagne.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
Tarte flambée and choucroute in Alsace, jambon de Reims and pink biscuits in Champagne, Alsatian whites and Champagne itself.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Don't drive after tastings.**
- **Don't treat Burgundy like Alsace**: the welcome works the opposite way.
- **Book the big houses** in Champagne in advance.

## Chiusura personale
The difference between Alsace and Champagne is all in the welcome: in Alsace you walk in and chat, in Champagne you go down into a Roman gallery and watch a technical process. They are two opposite ways of telling the story of French wine, and put in sequence they complete each other.
