# California Road Trip in 19 Days: San Francisco, Yosemite, Sequoia, Death Valley, Joshua Tree, Los Angeles and Big Sur

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 in progress (itinerary, prices, park rules and road status checked in October 2026)
**Periodo del viaggio:**
**Durata:** 19 days, 18 nights
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independent, with a rental car picked up and returned in San Francisco and about 2,800-3,000 km of driving
**Categoria:** Nord America · Stati Uniti · California · road trip · parchi nazionali · deserto · Highway 1

---

## Apertura personale
It's the most complete American road trip you can do in three weeks without leaving a single state. A loop that starts and ends in **San Francisco**, climbs into the Sierra Nevada for **Yosemite** and the giant sequoias of **Sequoia**, drops into the hottest desert on the planet in **Death Valley**, crosses the Mojave to **Joshua Tree**, reaches **Los Angeles** and goes back up the coast on **Highway 1** between Santa Barbara, Big Sur and Monterey. In a few days everything changes: altitude, temperatures, landscape, pace.

But California has to be organized. Since 2026 the most famous parks charge foreign tourists a surcharge, lodging inside the parks is booked months ahead, Yosemite's Tioga Road is open only in summer, Death Valley in summer is dangerous and Highway 1 at Big Sur closes in places because of landslides. This guide starts from there: first the rules and the seasons, then the days.

It suits people who gladly drive for many hours, couples, friends and families with teenagers. If you have two weeks, you'll find shorter variants at the end.

## Scheda pratica

### When to go, month by month, and closed roads
- **November-March:** the best time for Death Valley and Joshua Tree, but Yosemite and Sequoia are snowed in: the **Tioga Road** (CA-120 east), the Glacier Point road beyond Badger Pass and the Kings Canyon road close, and on mountain roads chains may be mandatory even for rental cars. Rain on the coast and landslides at Big Sur.
- **April-May:** Yosemite's waterfalls at their peak, desert still livable (30-38 °C in May in Death Valley), wildflowers. The Tioga Road usually opens between late May and June, depending on the snow.
- **June:** everything is open; the desert starts to get extreme. On the coast there are gray morning skies (June Gloom).
- **July-August:** high season in the mountain parks, with lines at the entrances and full parking lots from 9 a.m.; Death Valley over 45-50 °C, to be done only at dawn or skipped; fog and cold in San Francisco and Big Sur. The **wildfire** season begins, which can close roads and parks.
- **September-mid-October:** probably the best period for the full loop: fewer crowds, San Francisco at its best, desert temperatures dropping, Tioga Road still open until the first snowfalls (usually November). Fire risk still high.
- **Always check:** road status on **Caltrans QuickMap** (especially Highway 1 at Big Sur, fully reopened on January 14, 2026 after almost two years of closure at Regent's Slide, but subject to temporary closures after every bout of bad weather) and the "Current conditions" pages of each park on nps.gov.

### Documents, ESTA and insurance
- **Passport:** electronic (with a microchip), valid at least until the date of return; a 6-month margin avoids problems with airlines.
- **ESTA:** only on the official site esta.cbp.dhs.gov, at least 72 hours before departure; from October 16, 2026 it costs **$40.62** (about €35) and is valid for 2 years, or until the passport expires. Sites offering it for $80-100 are intermediaries. Anyone who has been to Cuba since January 12, 2021, or to Iran, Iraq, Libya, Syria, Somalia, Sudan, Yemen or North Korea since March 1, 2011, must apply for a visa.
- **Driver's license:** the Italian one is valid for driving as a tourist; the **international permit** is recommended and some rental companies ask for it.
- **Health insurance** with a high coverage limit, effectively mandatory: a hospital stay in the United States costs tens of thousands of dollars.

### National parks: tickets, the 2026 surcharge and reservations
- **Entrance per car, 7 days:** Yosemite $35, Sequoia and Kings Canyon $35, Death Valley $30, Joshua Tree $30 (about €26-30). At Yosemite you pay only by card at the entrances, no cash.
- **Surcharge for non-residents from January 1, 2026:** in eleven of the most visited national parks, including **Yosemite** and **Sequoia and Kings Canyon**, foreign visitors aged 16 and over pay **$100 per person** (about €86) on top of the regular ticket. The others are Acadia, Bryce Canyon, Everglades, Glacier, Grand Canyon, Grand Teton, Rocky Mountain, Yellowstone and Zion. Death Valley and Joshua Tree are not on the list.
- **America the Beautiful annual pass for non-residents:** **$250** (about €215; it was $80 until 2025). For one year it covers entrance fees and surcharges at federal parks for the holder and all passengers in the same private car; at sites that charge per person, like Muir Woods, it covers the holder and three other adults. For two people visiting Yosemite and Sequoia it's always worth it: without the pass you would spend $470 on entrances and surcharges, with the pass $250.
- **Entry reservations:** Yosemite **does not require entry reservations for all of 2026**, summer and Firefall included: you enter without one, but with long lines on peak days, and the park diverts traffic when parking lots are full. The park recommends entering before 9 a.m. or after 5 p.m. Nothing has been decided yet for 2027: it's worth checking nps.gov/yose before leaving. **Muir Woods**, near San Francisco, does require a parking or shuttle reservation.
- **Lodging in the parks:** the lodges in Yosemite Valley and the campgrounds on recreation.gov sell out the very day reservations open (up to 13 months ahead for lodges, 5 months for campgrounds). Without one, you sleep outside the park, 30-60 minutes away.

### Car rental
- **Pick up and return in San Francisco** to avoid the one-way surcharge, which between San Francisco and Los Angeles can exceed $300-500.
- **Book from Italy** with full insurance (damage coverage with no deductible and additional liability, often called SLI or LIS): counter rates are much more expensive.
- **Credit card** in the driver's name for the deposit; surcharges for drivers under 25 and for a second driver.
- **Tolls:** the Golden Gate Bridge is paid only electronically on exiting Marin toward the city, and the rental company charges the toll with a fee. Few other toll roads along this itinerary.
- **Snow chains:** in the Sierra Nevada, from October to April, they may be mandatory; many contracts forbid their use, so on snow days it's better not to go up.
- **Fuel:** roughly $4.80-5.80 a gallon (3.8 liters), more expensive inside the parks and along Big Sur. Fill up before Death Valley and before Big Sur.
- **Driving:** obey speed limits, and there are checks; a right turn on red is allowed after stopping unless a sign says otherwise, and you don't pass stopped school buses.
- **From the airport to the centre:** **San Francisco (SFO)**. · **BART** from the International Terminal station to Powell Street or Embarcadero: about $11, about 30 minutes, with a Clipper card (also on your phone), last train around midnight · **Uber/Lyft**: about $40-60 to Union Square, from the pickup point shown in the app · metered **taxi**: about $55-70 · rental cars are collected at the Rental Car Center, reached on the free AirTrain · the mistake to avoid is leaving luggage in sight in a car parked in the city: break-ins through car windows are common in San Francisco.

### Budget: the items that surprise
- **Taxes and tips:** about 7-10% sales tax is added to prices and, at restaurants, an 18-20% tip. In hotels, often a lodging tax or a "resort fee" per night.
- **Parking:** $50-80 a night at hotels in San Francisco and Los Angeles.
- **Lodging in the parks and along the coast:** the most expensive of the trip, with prices that double in summer and on weekends.

### Safety
- **Thefts from cars:** in San Francisco and Los Angeles, windows broken to steal luggage are a daily occurrence, even in broad daylight at the viewpoints. Nothing in view, ever, not even for five minutes.
- **Bears:** at Yosemite and Sequoia, food, cosmetics and trash must not be left in the car overnight but in the designated bear-proof containers; fines are high.
- **Heat:** in Death Valley you hike only in the early morning hours from May to September, with 4 liters of water per person in the car.
- **Fires and air quality:** from July to October check closures and the air quality index (AirNow).
- **Urban areas:** on some streets in downtown San Francisco (Tenderloin) and Los Angeles (Skid Row) it's better not to stroll in the evening.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Arrival in San Francisco
We landed at **SFO** and went into the centre on the **BART** metro, in half an hour. For the first three days you don't need the car: it only costs you parking and the risk of break-ins, and you pick it up on the day you leave for Yosemite. As a base in the city, choose a central, well-connected area such as Union Square, North Beach or the Financial District, avoiding the Tenderloin, a few blocks from Union Square, where it's best not to stroll in the evening. Take the first afternoon slowly: after the long flight it's enough to settle in and go for a short walk.

In the evening, for a first taste of the city, we had dinner in **North Beach**, the Italian neighbourhood, or in **Chinatown**, the oldest in North America. The two areas are side by side: you enter Chinatown through the **Dragon Gate** on Grant Avenue and in a few minutes on foot you reach the cafés and pizzerias of North Beach, with City Lights bookstore, linked to the Beat Generation writers. *On the first day, stay awake until evening and have an early dinner: it's the quickest way to get over the jet lag.*

**In practice:** BART airport-downtown about $10-12, 30 minutes · Clipper card or app for streetcars, cable cars and buses · time difference: 9 hours behind Italy, best to stay awake until evening.

### Day 2 — San Francisco: the Golden Gate, the Presidio and Alcatraz
In the morning we crossed the **Golden Gate Bridge** on foot or on a rented bike, starting from the **Presidio**, to **Sausalito** on the other side, returning by ferry. The bridge is almost three kilometres long and takes less than an hour to cross on foot; by bike the ride to Sausalito, all on bike paths and quiet roads, takes a couple of hours with stops. The best views of the bridge are from **Battery Spencer**, on the Marin side, and from **Baker Beach**. In summer, morning fog can cover it completely: the middle of the day is best.

In the afternoon we took the ferry to **Alcatraz**, the island prison, with the audio guide narrated by former inmates and guards. Ferries leave from **Pier 33**, near Fisherman's Wharf, and the visit, with the crossing, the walk up to the prison and the audio guide, takes two and a half to three hours. Tickets sell out weeks ahead and are sold only on the official operator's website. *On the bridge and on the island there's almost always a cold wind: you need a jacket even in summer.*

**In practice:** bike rental roughly $30-50 a day · Sausalito-San Francisco ferry roughly $15 · Alcatraz about $48 (Day Tour, City Experiences), book weeks ahead · sites reselling Alcatraz at higher prices are not the official one.

### Day 3 — San Francisco: the neighborhoods and Muir Woods
Morning at **Muir Woods**, the coast redwood forest half an hour north of the bridge, with parking or shuttle to be booked in advance; or in the city, depending on where you prefer to spend the day. The main trail is a flat boardwalk along the creek, among trees over seventy metres tall; **Cathedral Grove** is a quiet zone, where you walk without talking. At opening time there are fewer people and the light filters better between the trunks.

Then the neighbourhoods: the murals of the **Mission**, in Balmy Alley and Clarion Alley, and a burrito for lunch, the Victorian houses of **Alamo Square**, the **Castro**, the **Powell-Hyde cable car** to Lombard Street and Fisherman's Wharf, sunset from **Lands End**, the clifftop trail with the ruins of the Sutro Baths and a view of the Golden Gate. *At the Powell Street cable car turnaround the queue can be long: boarding at a later stop means less waiting.*

**In practice:** Muir Woods entry $15 per person (covered by the annual pass) plus a parking or shuttle reservation on gomuirwoods.com · cable car $9 per ride · pick up the car in the evening or early next morning.

### Day 4 — From San Francisco to Yosemite
We picked up the car and headed east: three and a half to four hours to Yosemite's **Big Oak Flat** entrance, crossing the Central Valley. Leave San Francisco early, before the Bay Area traffic, and stop for groceries and fuel in the towns before the mountains: after **Groveland** services become scarce. The annual pass can be bought at the entrance or online before leaving, and it also covers the $100 per person surcharge that foreign visitors pay at Yosemite from 2026.

The arrival in the valley goes through **Tunnel View**, the viewpoint with El Capitan on the left, **Bridalveil Fall** on the right and **Half Dome** at the back: we got there in the afternoon, with the best light. *In 2026 no entry reservation is needed, but on peak days the park diverts traffic when the car parks are full: arriving after 5 pm helps.* If you didn't get a room in the valley lodges, you sleep outside the park, 30-60 minutes away.

**In practice:** San Francisco-Yosemite Valley 3.5-4 hours, heavy traffic leaving the Bay Area on Fridays · non-resident annual pass $250, covering entries and surcharges · shop at the supermarket before entering: inside the park everything costs much more · Yosemite entrances accept cards only.

### Day 5 — Yosemite Valley: the Mist Trail and the waterfalls
We set off early, before 8 am, to find parking. The **Mist Trail** climbs along the Merced River to **Vernal Fall** and, for those with the legs, to **Nevada Fall**: between May and June the trail passes right under the spray, and you need a waterproof jacket and shoes that grip on wet rock. It's about 5 km round trip to Vernal, 11 to Nevada, with 600 metres of elevation gain.

In the afternoon we explored the valley meadows on foot or by bike, with **Yosemite Falls** and El Capitan, where with binoculars you can spot climbers on the walls. At sunset we went back to Tunnel View or to **Valley View**.

**In practice:** Mist Trail 3-6 hours depending on the destination, water and layers · free valley shuttles so you don't have to move the car · food in bear-proof lockers, never in the car overnight.

### Day 6 — Yosemite: Glacier Point and Mariposa Grove
The road to **Glacier Point**, open roughly from late May to November, climbs to 2,200 metres above the valley with a head-on view of Half Dome and the waterfalls. We then drove south to **Mariposa Grove**, the giant sequoia grove at the southern entrance, with the **Grizzly Giant** and a walk of a couple of hours. You leave the park to the south and sleep in **Oakhurst** or **Fish Camp**, already on the way to Sequoia.

If you travel in summer with the **Tioga Road** open, you can consider the alternative route east, described in the variants, which leads to Mono Lake and Death Valley, leaving out Sequoia.

**In practice:** Glacier Point about 1 hour's drive from the valley · Mariposa Grove with a shuttle from the entrance car park, trails of 3-11 km · Oakhurst has cheaper supermarkets and petrol stations.

### Day 7 — From Yosemite to Sequoia
Three and a half to four hours south, via **Fresno**, to the **Kings Canyon** entrance. The stretch from Oakhurst to Fresno is quick, then Highway 180 climbs from the farmland to the Sierra in just over an hour. We started at **General Grant Grove**, with the second largest tree in the world, which Americans call the Nation's Christmas Tree: a paved loop of less than a kilometre goes around the trunk and the nearby sequoias.

If the season allows, you drive down the **Kings Canyon Scenic Byway** into a glacial gorge as deep as Yosemite and far less visited, stopping at Grizzly Falls and at the end of the road, Road's End, where the flat Zumwalt Meadow trail runs along the river beneath the granite walls. *There and back is two hours of driving: leave by midday to get back in daylight.* The night at **Wuksachi Lodge** or a lodge in the area.

**In practice:** Oakhurst-Grant Grove about 2.5-3 hours · Sequoia and Kings Canyon $35 per car plus the $100 per person surcharge for foreigners, both covered by the annual pass · Kings Canyon Scenic Byway open roughly from late April-May to November, about 1 hour each way to Road's End · fill up in Fresno: there's almost no fuel in the parks.

### Day 8 — Sequoia: the General Sherman and Moro Rock
We spent the day in the **Giant Forest**. The **General Sherman**, the largest tree on Earth by volume, over 2,000 years old, is reached by a short trail from the car park; from there the **Congress Trail** winds among dozens of giant sequoias in an hour and a half. Be at the General Sherman before 9 am, while the car parks are still empty; in summer park shuttles link the main points of the Giant Forest so you can leave the car. The Giant Forest Museum, along the road, explains how these trees live and resist fire.

Then **Moro Rock**, a granite dome with 350 steps carved to the top and views over the Sierra and the valley. A little further on the same road is the **Tunnel Log**, a fallen sequoia with a passage cut through it for cars, and the meadow of Crescent Meadow. In the afternoon we drove down the very winding **Generals Highway** to the southern exit at **Three Rivers**, where you sleep. *The descent drops almost two thousand metres in a few dozen kilometres: gentle braking and low gears.*

**In practice:** Congress Trail about 5 km loop, easy · Moro Rock 30-45 minutes round trip, not advised in thunderstorms · Generals Highway about 1.5 hours from Giant Forest to Three Rivers, tight hairpins · Three Rivers has motels and restaurants, cheaper than inside the park.

### Day 9 — To Death Valley
A driving day, five to six hours, from the Sierra to the desert: we went around the range to the south via **Lake Isabella** and **Ridgecrest**, to the western entrance of Death Valley at **Panamint Springs**. The first part climbs the Kern River canyon, narrow and full of bends; after Ridgecrest the road opens onto empty plateaus. The landscape changes completely in the last hour, with the descent into Panamint Valley and then the climb over Towne Pass before dropping towards Stovepipe Wells.

We reached **Stovepipe Wells** for sunset over the **Mesquite Flat dunes**, with the low light drawing their crests. The car park is on the highway, and you walk on the sand as far as you like, with no trail: just keep an eye on the direction of the car. *Even at sunset the sand stays hot for hours: closed shoes, no sandals.* Stovepipe Wells has a hotel, a general store and a petrol station, and is handy for the next day's sunrise.

**In practice:** Three Rivers-Stovepipe Wells about 5-6 hours · fill up in Ridgecrest and carry 4 litres of water per person in the car · Death Valley entry $30 per car, covered by the annual pass · in much of the park there's no phone signal: download maps beforehand.

### Day 10 — Death Valley: Badwater, Artists Drive and Zabriskie Point
We watched sunrise at **Zabriskie Point**, when the yellow and brown clay hills light up. Then south: **Golden Canyon** on foot in the cool hours, **Artists Drive** with walls coloured by minerals, and **Badwater Basin**, the salt flat 86 metres below sea level, the lowest point in North America. From the Badwater car park you walk on the white crust to where the salt forms hexagonal cells, about twenty minutes away; along the road there's also **Devil's Golf Course**, a field of sharp salt crystals. Artists Drive is one-way and best driven in the afternoon, when the sun lights up Artists Palette.

In the hot hours we stopped at **Furnace Creek**, at the visitor centre, where a small exhibition explains the valley's climate, which holds the record for the highest air temperature ever measured. Sunset is seen from **Dante's View**, 1,700 metres above Badwater. *In summer, if the car breaks down, stay in it and wait: walking off in search of help can be fatal.*

**In practice:** from May to September no hiking after 10 am · Dante's View 40 minutes from Furnace Creek, steep final stretch · at night the park has one of the darkest skies in the United States · fuel at Furnace Creek or Stovepipe Wells, expensive but the only options around.

### Day 11 — From Death Valley to Joshua Tree
We crossed the **Mojave Desert** to the south-east, four to five hours. If you have time, leave Death Valley towards **Shoshone** and **Baker**, the town with the giant thermometer by the highway, then cross the Mojave preserve and take a stretch of historic **Route 66** between **Amboy**, with its old motel and gas station, and **Twentynine Palms**, at the gates of Joshua Tree. Near Amboy there's also an extinct volcanic crater, visible from the road.

It's a day of straight roads and almost empty towns, the part of California the films made famous. Leave early, to drive the hottest stretch in the morning hours, and stop only in pull-outs: by the roadside the sand is soft and a rental car gets stuck easily. *On this route petrol stations are few and far between: leave every stop with an almost full tank.* In the evening you reach Twentynine Palms, the base for the park.

**In practice:** Furnace Creek-Twentynine Palms via Amboy about 4.5-5 hours · few petrol stations on Route 66: fill up in Baker · base in Twentynine Palms, Joshua Tree or Yucca Valley · water and food in the car for the whole day.

### Day 12 — Joshua Tree
We drove through the park from north to south, with many short stops. Joshua Tree brings together two deserts: to the north the Mojave, higher and cooler, with the Joshua trees that give the park its name; to the south the Colorado Desert, lower and hotter, with cacti and ocotillo. **Hidden Valley**, a loop of about 1.5 km among granite boulders where people climb and boulder; **Skull Rock**, the skull-shaped boulder by the road; **Keys View**, over the Coachella Valley and the San Andreas Fault.

The **Cholla Cactus Garden**, a field of cacti that seems lit up at sunset, is further south, half an hour from the centre of the park: get there in the last hour of light. The night is for the stars: Joshua Tree is a dark-sky park, and you just need to stop in a car park far from the town lights. *There's no drinking water along the park roads, and no shops: bring everything you need from your base.*

**In practice:** entry $30 per car, covered by the annual pass · Ryan Mountain 5 km round trip for a proper hike · keep away from the cholla: the spines detach at the slightest touch · visitor centres in Joshua Tree and Twentynine Palms for maps and conditions.

### Day 13 — Palm Springs and arrival in Los Angeles
In half an hour we were down in **Palm Springs**, the city of mid-century modernist houses, with the **aerial tramway** that climbs in ten minutes from the desert to 2,600 metres on Mount San Jacinto, among the pines. The cars rotate on the way up, so from every spot you see the whole valley; at the top the temperature is several degrees lower than in the desert, and there are forest trails and a viewpoint. Go up in the morning, before the heat and the weekend queues.

Back down, a stroll along **Palm Canyon Drive**, the main street, among shops and 1950s architecture. In the afternoon, two and a half hours on the freeway to **Los Angeles**, where you sleep in **Santa Monica** or **West Hollywood**. *In Los Angeles you drive a lot even for short distances: choosing the hotel area according to the next days' outings saves hours of traffic.*

**In practice:** Palm Springs Aerial Tramway roughly $35-40, closed for a few weeks a year for maintenance · Palm Springs-Los Angeles 2-3 hours, avoid the afternoon rush hour · hotel parking $35-60 a night · at the top of the tramway you need a sweatshirt even in summer.

### Day 14 — Los Angeles: the Getty, Hollywood and the Griffith Observatory
In the morning we went to the **Getty Center**, the museum on the Brentwood hill with its collection of European art, gardens and views over the city: free entry, paid parking. From the car park an automated tram climbs to the museum; inside, the pavilions are linked by terraces, and the central garden is worth the visit as much as the galleries. The museum is closed on Mondays, and entry must be booked for a set time.

Then **Hollywood**, with the Walk of Fame, the Dolby Theatre and the stars' handprints in front of the TCL Chinese Theatre, a **Warner Bros.** studio tour for film lovers, and at sunset the **Griffith Observatory**, with the Hollywood sign on the hill and the city lighting up. *The observatory is also usually closed on Mondays: move this day if it falls on a Monday.* The car park near the observatory fills up early: arrive at least an hour before sunset or leave the car lower down and walk up.

**In practice:** Getty Center free with reservation, closed on Mondays, parking $25 ($15 after 3 pm) · Warner Bros. tour roughly $70-80 · Griffith Observatory free, paid parking that is often full at sunset.

### Day 15 — Los Angeles: Santa Monica, Venice and Malibu
A day by the sea. We started at the **Santa Monica Pier**, where a sign marks the end of Route 66, and continued by bike along the beach path to **Venice**, with its boardwalk, the outdoor gym of Muscle Beach and the canals. The path is flat, separated from pedestrians, and Santa Monica to Venice takes half an hour. At the Venice canals, a few streets south of the boardwalk, you walk among houses with little bridges, and Abbot Kinney Boulevard is the street of shops and cafés.

In the afternoon we drove north on the **Pacific Coast Highway** to **Malibu**, stopping at **El Matador Beach**, among the rocks, and at sunset a seafood dinner by the sea. At El Matador you go down a steep staircase, and at high tide the beach almost disappears. *Along the Pacific Coast Highway, work after the 2025 fires may still slow traffic: check road conditions on Caltrans QuickMap.*

**In practice:** bike rental in Santa Monica roughly $15-25 for a few hours · Getty Villa in Malibu free with reservation, paid parking, check the closing day · nothing visible in the car in beach car parks.

### Day 16 — Santa Barbara and the coast to Cambria
We left Los Angeles heading north. **Santa Barbara**, an hour and a half to two hours away, is a white town with red roofs, the eighteenth-century Spanish **mission** and the walk along Stearns Wharf. The mission is visited on a self-guided route through the church, cloister, cemetery and museum, in about an hour; in town the main street, State Street, runs down to the sea, and the Funk Zone near the wharf is full of tasting rooms for local wines.

We continued on **Highway 101** and **Highway 1** to **San Simeon** and **Cambria**, with visits to **Hearst Castle**, the residence of publisher William Randolph Hearst, and the **elephant seal** colony at Piedras Blancas, visible for free from a boardwalk on the beach. You go up to the castle only on the visitor centre bus, at the time of your booked tour. *Between Santa Barbara and the castle there are two and a half hours of driving: to do both, leave Los Angeles early and book an afternoon tour.*

**In practice:** Los Angeles-Santa Barbara 1.5-2 hours, Santa Barbara-Cambria about 2.5 hours · Santa Barbara mission, self-guided visit about $17 · Hearst Castle Grand Rooms Tour $35, to be booked · elephant seals present all year, more numerous in winter and spring.

### Day 17 — Big Sur on Highway 1
This is the most famous day of the trip: about 150 km of road above the ocean from Cambria to Carmel, to be taken slowly, at least six to seven hours with stops. The highlights: **Ragged Point**, **McWay Falls** dropping onto the beach in **Julia Pfeiffer Burns State Park**, **Pfeiffer Beach** with its purple sand, the **Bixby Creek** bridge and **Point Lobos**, a reserve with seals, otters and cypresses. Before setting off, check road conditions on Caltrans QuickMap.

Heading north, the sea is on the left and pull-outs are easier to reach. McWay Falls is seen from a short trail starting at the park car park, in ten minutes; the road to Pfeiffer Beach is narrow and unsigned, and the car park at the end fills early. *Along almost all of Big Sur there's no phone signal: download maps and directions beforehand.* You reach Carmel or Monterey in the late afternoon, where you sleep.

**In practice:** fill up in Cambria: along Big Sur petrol stations are few and expensive · stop only in marked pull-outs, never on bends · Point Lobos $10 per car, arrive early at weekends · Julia Pfeiffer Burns and Pfeiffer Beach have paid parking.

### Day 18 — Monterey, Carmel and the return to San Francisco
In the morning we were in **Monterey**, at the **Monterey Bay Aquarium**, one of the best aquariums in the world, with its kelp forest and sea otters. The aquarium is at the end of **Cannery Row**, the street of former sardine canneries described by Steinbeck, and the otters' and other animals' feeding times are fixed and shown at the entrance: plan the visit around them. You need at least two to three hours.

Then **Carmel-by-the-Sea**, the village of fairy-tale houses, and the **17-Mile Drive** among golf courses and cypresses above the ocean, with a stop at the **Lone Cypress** on its rock. In the afternoon, a good two hours to **San Francisco**: we returned the car at the airport and slept near SFO. *Traffic towards the Bay Area on weekday afternoons is heavy: leaving Carmel by 2-3 pm avoids the worst queues.*

**In practice:** Monterey Bay Aquarium around $65, tickets online · 17-Mile Drive $12.50 per car · fill up before returning the car and check the tolls charged.

### Day 19 — Departure from San Francisco
Flight home from **SFO**. With flights to Europe usually leaving in the afternoon or evening, the morning is enough for a last visit to the **Ferry Building**, if you slept in the city, or to get to the airport without rushing. The Ferry Building, on the Embarcadero waterfront, has food shops and cafés inside, and on Tuesdays, Thursdays and Saturdays it hosts the Bay Area farmers' market. From the hotels near the airport, instead, free shuttles reach the terminal in a few minutes.

At SFO the terminals are linked by the AirTrain, the automated train that also goes to the BART station and the rental car centre. *Before leaving, check the hotel's final bill and any rental charges, such as tolls and fuel, while there's still time to dispute them.*

**In practice:** arrive at the airport 3 hours before international flights · check tips and hotel taxes on the final bill · BART from downtown to the airport about 30 minutes.

## Where to sleep
- **San Francisco:** budget: hostels and motels on Lombard Street, $120-180 · mid-range: boutique hotels in Union Square, Nob Hill and North Beach, $200-320 · luxury: the Fairmont, big hotels on Nob Hill.
- **Yosemite:** inside the park: Curry Village (tents and cabins) and Yosemite Valley Lodge, $200-400, The Ahwahnee over $600 · outside: motels and lodges in Mariposa, El Portal, Groveland and Oakhurst, $150-280 · campgrounds on recreation.gov.
- **Sequoia and Kings Canyon:** Wuksachi Lodge and John Muir Lodge, $220-350 · outside: motels in Three Rivers, $140-220.
- **Death Valley:** budget: Panamint Springs and Stovepipe Wells, $150-250 · luxury: The Inn at Death Valley in Furnace Creek.
- **Joshua Tree:** motels and desert houses in Twentynine Palms and Joshua Tree, $120-250.
- **Los Angeles:** mid-range: motels and hotels in West Hollywood and Culver City, $180-300 · luxury: seafront hotels in Santa Monica.
- **Coast:** Santa Barbara $200-350, Cambria and San Simeon $150-250, Monterey and Carmel $200-350; in Big Sur few lodges, over $300-400.

## Where to eat
- **San Francisco:** the Mission **burrito**, dim sum in Chinatown, **clam chowder** in a sourdough bread bowl, the producers at the Ferry Building, Hog Island oysters.
- **In the parks:** grocery shopping outside the park and packed lunches; in the lodges dinners are expensive and often need booking.
- **Desert:** the town diners on Route 66, American breakfasts with pancakes and eggs.
- **Los Angeles:** **tacos** from food trucks, the Korean food of Koreatown, the Grand Central Market in Downtown, the In-N-Out burger for those who want the Californian ritual.
- **Coast:** **fish and chips** and seafood at the Santa Barbara pier, Santa Maria barbecue (grilled tri-tip), the wines of the Santa Ynez Valley and Monterey's clam chowder.

## Variants by length
- **14 days, without the desert:** San Francisco (3), Yosemite (2), Sequoia (2), Los Angeles (2), coast to Monterey (3), return to San Francisco. From Sequoia to Los Angeles about 4 hours.
- **Summer, with the Tioga Road:** from Yosemite you leave east on the **Tioga Road** (open roughly from June to November) and go down to **Mono Lake**, **Mammoth Lakes** and along Highway 395 to **Lone Pine** and the **Alabama Hills**, then Death Valley at dawn. You skip Sequoia or make it up at the end, coming up from Los Angeles. It's the most scenic variant but does the desert in the hottest month.
- **Winter (November-March):** the Tioga Road is closed and Yosemite is reached only from the west; more time is devoted to the desert (Death Valley, Joshua Tree, Anza-Borrego) and the coast, checking the status of Highway 1 after the rains.
- **With Las Vegas and the Utah parks:** from Death Valley to Las Vegas in 2 hours, then Zion and the Grand Canyon before returning to California for Joshua Tree. It takes an extra week, and Zion and the Grand Canyon fall under the non-resident surcharge, already covered by the annual pass.
- **Coast only:** San Francisco-Los Angeles in 7-8 days along Highway 1 with a one-way rental car.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Apply for the ESTA only on the official site**, at $40.62.
- **Buy the annual pass for non-residents** if you visit Yosemite and Sequoia: it pays for itself with just two parks.
- **Book park lodging months ahead**, or accept sleeping an hour away.
- **Check nps.gov and Caltrans QuickMap** before every stop: closures for snow, landslides, fires and entry rules change every year.
- **Leave nothing in view in the car**, in cities and in viewpoint parking lots.
- **Store food and cosmetics in bear-proof containers** at Yosemite and Sequoia.
- **Fill up before** Death Valley, Route 66 and Big Sur.
- **Hike at dawn in the desert** and never in the hot hours in summer.
- **Budget for taxes and tips**: 25-30% more on almost every bill.
- **Pick up the car only when needed:** in San Francisco and Los Angeles it costs in parking and risk of theft.

## Chiusura personale
Nineteen days in California are a trip of contrasts measured in hours of driving: from sequoias to salt flats below sea level, from the granite of Yosemite to the cacti of Joshua Tree, from the hills of San Francisco to the beaches of Malibu. It's an expensive trip that asks you to plan reservations and seasons, but once on the road it moves easily: wide roads, landscapes that change every day, and the Big Sur coast as the last stretch before returning to where you started.
