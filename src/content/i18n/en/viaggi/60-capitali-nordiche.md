# Nordic Capitals: Copenhagen, Stockholm and Oslo in 9 Days

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 in progress (trip done by Rick; itinerary and practical information verified)
**Periodo del viaggio:**
**Durata:** 9 days, 8 nights (3 days per capital)
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independent, with a train and a domestic flight between the three capitals, everything else on foot, by bike or on public transport
**Categoria:** Europa · Danimarca · Svezia · Norvegia · città · design · cultura nordica

---

## Apertura personale
Three capitals, three countries, three languages that understand each other better than they understand Italian, and three different currencies, none of them the euro. That's the detail that surprises people most when they show up thinking "European Union, Scandinavia, it'll all be the same": Denmark, Sweden and Norway share an intertwined history and a recognizable aesthetic, but they remain three separate economies, with three different banknotes in your pocket in the same week.

Copenhagen, Stockholm and Oslo are also, according to almost every cost-of-living ranking in Europe, among the most expensive cities on the continent: Copenhagen and Oslo regularly fight for a spot in the top three or four, along with Zurich and Geneva. This isn't a detail to play down: a beer at a bar costs two or three times what it does in Italy, a simple dinner for two can top €80-100, and transport between the three capitals (train or plane, never free) has to be budgeted from the start. That said, the trip is worth every krone spent: Copenhagen is perhaps the most bike-friendly city in the world, Stockholm is built on fourteen islands with an archipelago of thirty thousand islets at its doorstep, and Oslo sits right on a fjord, with a sculpture park that has no equal anywhere else.

Nine days, three days each, a train and a domestic flight as the hinge: not a continuous overland run like other itineraries on this site, but three capitals connected in the most sensible way, by train when the scenery along the way justifies it, by plane when time matters more.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** **May to September** for milder weather and long days (in June the sun sets well after 10 pm in all three cities). **June to August** is high season: pricier and more crowded, but it's also when the Stockholm archipelago and the Oslofjord cruise are at their best. **April and October** remain a good compromise, with fewer crowds and lower prices on flights and lodging. Winter is cold and dark early in all three cities, but it isn't out of the question: in December Copenhagen has the Tivoli Christmas markets, although several museums and seasonal activities (like the archipelago or the fjord) run less often or not at all.
- **Three currencies, none of them the euro:** Denmark (Danish krone, DKK, closely pegged to the euro through the ERM II agreement, roughly €1 = 7.46 DKK), Sweden (Swedish krona, SEK, free-floating, roughly €1 = 11.3 SEK) and Norway (Norwegian krone, NOK, also free-floating, roughly €1 = 10.8 NOK) each have their own currency, and Norway isn't even part of the European Union (only of the European Economic Area). All three are countries where you pay almost entirely by card: it works everywhere, but foreign cards sometimes pay a small currency conversion fee. It's best to check your card's terms beforehand or bring one designed for travel.
- **The highest costs in Europe:** a beer at a bar costs €7-11 in Copenhagen, €6-9 in Stockholm (75-105 SEK) and as much as €9-14 in Oslo (100-130 NOK, among the highest prices in the world because of alcohol taxes). A simple meal at a casual place runs around €12-20 in all three cities; a dinner at a mid-range restaurant starts at €35-45 per person. Supermarkets (especially for beer) are much cheaper than bars everywhere.
- **Copenhagen-Stockholm:** direct SJ train, roughly **5 to 5.5 hours** (the fastest under 5 hours), with regular departures several times a day; or a direct flight, about **1 hour**, with several airlines on the route. There's no real direct alternative by sea between the two cities.
- **Stockholm-Oslo:** same choice, direct SJ train in about **5 hours 45 minutes** or a direct flight of about **1 hour**, with several departures a day for both. There's no direct Stockholm-Oslo ferry: the best-known sea link in the region, the DFDS overnight ferry of about 17 hours 45 minutes, connects Copenhagen with Oslo. Anyone with the time and the desire to try it can consider it a variation on the itinerary, outside this linear route.
- **Returning from Oslo:** Oslo Gardermoen airport is well connected to Italy; for those who still prefer to go back through Copenhagen, SAS flights between Oslo and Copenhagen are very frequent (more than 70 a week) and last just over an hour.
- **Christiania (Copenhagen):** the "free town" founded in 1971 has its own rules, posted and enforced: no photos on the former Pusher Street (closed by the Danish authorities in 2024 after an episode of violence) or of residents without permission. Selling drugs, hashish included, remains illegal throughout Denmark: whatever its reputation says, it isn't an outlaw enclave.
- **The Viking Ship Museum in Oslo is closed:** the historic site on the Bygdøy peninsula is under renovation and the Viking ships are being moved to the new Museum of the Viking Age; reopening isn't expected before 2027. On this itinerary you still visit Bygdøy, for the Fram Museum and the Kon-Tiki Museum, but before leaving it's worth rechecking the Viking museum's reopening date.
- **Tivoli Gardens (Copenhagen) is seasonal:** it opens in spring and summer, for Halloween and for the Christmas markets, but **closes for some winter months** outside those periods. It isn't open year-round: check the official calendar before planning your evening.
- **Documents:** Denmark and Sweden are in the European Union and the Schengen area, Norway is in the Schengen area but outside the EU. For Italian citizens, a valid ID card for travel abroad is enough in all three, with no visa for tourist stays under three months.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Arrival in Copenhagen and Nyhavn
We landed at Copenhagen-Kastrup (CPH), about 20 minutes from the center by metro. After checking in, we took a first walk toward **Nyhavn**, the 17th-century canal lined with the colorful facades of merchants' houses, now a continuous row of cafés along the quay: it's the most photographed spot in the city and the most natural way to get your bearings. From there we strolled along **Strøget**, one of the longest pedestrian shopping streets in Europe, as far as Kongens Nytorv. In the late afternoon, a boat tour on the canals from Nyhavn (about an hour) to see the old town from the water: there's a dedicated experience page. The first evening was a quiet one, with a stop at Torvehallerne for a first taste of smørrebrød.

### Day 2 — Tivoli Gardens, the Little Mermaid and the waterfront
We gave the day to the center and the waterfront. The morning at **Tivoli Gardens** (if it's in season: check the calendar before planning), open since 1843 and among the oldest amusement parks in the world still in operation. It has manicured gardens, historic wooden roller coasters and an atmosphere you can feel even just walking through, without going on any rides. In the afternoon we walked along the Langelinie waterfront to the **Little Mermaid** (Den Lille Havfrue), the 1913 bronze sculpture inspired by the Hans Christian Andersen fairy tale. It's small, often surrounded by crowds and tour groups, one of the most cited cases of an attraction that's more famous than it is impressive, but the walk around it is still worth the stop. To round off the day nicely, a ride on a rented bike along the waterfront, to try, even just for an afternoon, how most Copenhageners get around every day.

### Day 3 — Christiania, Christianshavn and the train to Stockholm
In the morning we visited **Christianshavn**, the neighborhood of smaller canals and houseboats, with the church of **Vor Frelsers Kirke** and its famous external spiral staircase around the golden spire. From there we went on to **Christiania**, the self-governed community founded in 1971: entry is free, but the community's rules must be respected without exception, so no photos on the former Pusher Street or of residents without permission. In the early afternoon, we transferred to **Stockholm** by train (direct SJ, roughly 5 to 5.5 hours) or, for those who prefer to save time, on a direct flight of about an hour. We arrived in Stockholm in the evening and had dinner around Gamla Stan or Södermalm.

### Day 4 — Gamla Stan and Stockholm's old town
The day was for **Gamla Stan**, Stockholm's medieval old town, on an island all its own: narrow cobbled alleys, pastel-colored houses, the square of **Stortorget** (the oldest in the city) and the **Royal Palace**, with the changing of the guard. In the afternoon we visited the **Stadshuset**, the red-brick City Hall on the water. It hosts the Nobel Prize banquet in the Blue Hall and has the Golden Hall, covered in over 18 million gold tiles. In the warmer months you can climb the tower for the view of the city (access is seasonal: check beforehand). Free evening among the restaurants of Södermalm.

### Day 5 — Vasamuseet, Stadshuset and ABBA The Museum
In the morning we went to the **Vasamuseet**, on the island of Djurgården. The museum houses the *Vasa*, a Swedish royal warship that sank in Stockholm harbor on its maiden voyage in 1628, after less than 1,300 meters of sailing, and was raised almost intact in 1961: it's the best-preserved 17th-century ship in the world. In the afternoon, still on Djurgården, **ABBA The Museum**, dedicated to the most famous Swedish pop group in the world, with original memorabilia and interactive rooms. Between one museum and the other there's time for a walk in Djurgården park, the city's green lung.

### Day 6 — The Stockholm archipelago and the train to Oslo
We spent the morning on a boat trip in the **Stockholm archipelago** (Stockholms skärgård), the more than thirty thousand islands and islets that open up east of the city toward the Baltic Sea. You can take a guided outing of 2 to 2.5 hours or, for those who prefer to organize things themselves, take the public Waxholmsbolaget ferries. In the early afternoon, transfer to **Oslo**: direct SJ train (about 5 hours 45 minutes) or domestic flight (about an hour). We arrived in Oslo in the evening and took a first walk toward the center.

### Day 7 — Central Oslo and the Opera House
We spent the day downtown: the **Royal Palace Gardens**, the **Aker Brygge** waterfront, the **Astrup Fearnley Museum** (in the building designed by Renzo Piano) and, to finish, the **Oslo Opera House**, with the sloping facade you can walk up to the roof for the view over the city and the fjord. A quiet evening, maybe with a hot tea in the Opera House's indoor bar before dinner.

### Day 8 — Vigeland Park and the museums of Bygdøy
In the morning we visited **Vigeland Park** (Frognerparken), the largest sculpture park in the world made by a single artist. There are over 200 works in bronze, granite and iron by Gustav Vigeland, including the famous Monolith, a column over 14 meters tall carved with 121 intertwined human figures. Entry is free, it's always open, and it's the most visited attraction in all of Norway. In the afternoon we moved to the museum peninsula of **Bygdøy** for the **Fram Museum**, dedicated to the polar ship used by Nansen and Amundsen, and the **Kon-Tiki Museum**, with Thor Heyerdahl's raft that crossed the Pacific in 1947. The Viking Ship Museum, on the same peninsula, is left out of the plan: it's closed for renovation and reopening isn't expected before 2027.

### Day 9 — Oslofjord cruise and departure
We gave the last morning to a **cruise on the Oslofjord**, an outing of 1.5 to 2 hours among the islands and bays south of the city, departing from the pier in front of City Hall. It's the best way to say goodbye to the last of the three capitals: seeing it from the water, like Copenhagen on the first day. The rest of the day is free for last-minute shopping or one more walk, before the transfer to Oslo Gardermoen airport for the flight home (direct to Italy, or via Copenhagen for those who want to close the circle of the three capitals on the way back too).

## What to see / what to do
- **Nyhavn**, the colorful 17th-century canal, by day and by night.
- **Tivoli Gardens**, if it's in season: one of the oldest amusement parks in the world.
- **Christiania**, respecting the community's rules without exception.
- **Gamla Stan**, Stockholm's medieval old town, on an island all its own.
- **The Vasamuseet**, with the 1628 warship raised almost intact.
- **The Stockholm archipelago**, thirty thousand islands east of the city.
- **Vigeland Park** in Oslo, the largest sculpture park in the world by a single artist.
- **The Oslofjord cruise**, to say goodbye to the trip from the water.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **Copenhagen:** the old town (Indre By) is the most convenient choice for doing everything on foot, but also the priciest. Vesterbro, a former red-light district and now one of the liveliest for bars and restaurants, is a good compromise between price and location.
- **Stockholm:** Gamla Stan and Norrmalm remain the most central areas, convenient on foot; Södermalm, younger, is an alternative that's often cheaper, a few minutes by metro from the center.
- **Oslo:** the area around Oslo S (the central station) and Karl Johans gate has almost all the tourist offerings within walking distance.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **Copenhagen:** Torvehallerne, the covered market near Nørreport, for smørrebrød and local products; Reffen, the street food area on the waterfront (seasonal), for something more casual.
- **Stockholm:** Södermalm and Östermalm hold much of the city's dining scene, from fast-casual to high-end restaurants.
- **Oslo:** the Aker Brygge area, on the waterfront, for traditional Norwegian cuisine. Keep in mind that, of the three capitals, it's the most expensive of all for eating out.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Don't assume the euro in any of the three cities**: Denmark, Sweden and Norway have three different currencies, none of them the euro.
- **Check the Tivoli Gardens calendar before planning your evening in Copenhagen**: it's open only in certain seasons, not year-round.
- **Don't take photos in Christiania in the posted areas** (the former Pusher Street in particular) or of residents without permission.
- **Don't plan on the Viking Ship Museum in Oslo**: it's closed for renovation and reopening isn't expected before 2027. You still visit Bygdøy for the Fram Museum and the Kon-Tiki Museum.
- **Book Vasamuseet and ABBA The Museum in advance** on weekends and in high season: the most popular slots sell out.
- **Budget for the highest costs in Europe**, in all three cities: meals and transport weigh on the daily budget more than anywhere else.
- **Don't walk or stand in Copenhagen's bike lanes without looking**: they're as busy as the roads, and cyclists don't slow down for distracted tourists.
- **Check the seasonal schedules of boat outings** (Stockholm archipelago, Oslofjord cruise): in winter the offering shrinks considerably compared to May to September.

## Chiusura personale
What stays with me is the contrast between three cities that look alike enough to seem like variations on one another (the same clean design, the same language of road signs, the same clean northern air) and yet remain three separate countries all the way down: three currencies, three capitals, three slightly different ways of being Scandinavian. Copenhagen moving by bicycle, Stockholm spread over fourteen islands with a boundless archipelago at its doorstep, Oslo sitting right on the fjord with a sculpture park that looks like nothing else in Europe. Nine days aren't enough to really know them, but they're enough to understand why, despite the final bill, it's worth lining up all three.
