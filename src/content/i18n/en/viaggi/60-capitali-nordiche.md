# Nordic Capitals: Copenhagen, Stockholm and Oslo in 9 Days

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 in progress (trip done by Rick; itinerary and practical information verified)
**Periodo del viaggio:**
**Durata:** 9 days, 8 nights (3 days per capital)
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independent, with a train and a domestic flight between the three capitals, everything else on foot, by bike or on public transport
**Categoria:** Europa · Danimarca · Svezia · Norvegia · città · design · cultura nordica

---

## Apertura personale
Three capitals, three countries, three languages that understand each other better than they understand Italian, and three different currencies, none of them the euro. That's the detail that surprises people most when they show up thinking "European Union, Scandinavia, it'll all be the same": Denmark, Sweden and Norway share an intertwined history and a recognizable aesthetic, but they remain three separate economies, with three different banknotes in your pocket in the same week.

Copenhagen, Stockholm and Oslo are also, according to almost every cost-of-living ranking in Europe, among the most expensive cities on the continent: Copenhagen and Oslo regularly fight for a spot in the top three or four, along with Zurich and Geneva. This isn't a detail to play down: a beer at a bar costs two or three times what it does in Italy, a simple dinner for two can top €80-100, and transport between the three capitals (train or plane, never free) has to be budgeted from the start. That said, the trip is worth every krone spent: Copenhagen is perhaps the most bike-friendly city in the world, Stockholm is built on fourteen islands with an archipelago of thirty thousand islets at its doorstep, and Oslo sits right on a fjord, with a sculpture park that has no equal anywhere else.

Nine days, three days each, a train and a domestic flight as the hinge: not a continuous overland run like other itineraries on this site, but three capitals connected in the most sensible way, by train when the scenery along the way justifies it, by plane when time matters more.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** **May to September** for milder weather and long days (in June the sun sets well after 10 pm in all three cities). **June to August** is high season: pricier and more crowded, but it's also when the Stockholm archipelago and the Oslofjord cruise are at their best. **April and October** remain a good compromise, with fewer crowds and lower prices on flights and lodging. Winter is cold and dark early in all three cities, but it isn't out of the question: in December Copenhagen has the Tivoli Christmas markets, although several museums and seasonal activities (like the archipelago or the fjord) run less often or not at all.
- **Three currencies, none of them the euro:** Denmark (Danish krone, DKK, closely pegged to the euro through the ERM II agreement, roughly €1 = 7.46 DKK), Sweden (Swedish krona, SEK, free-floating, roughly €1 = 11.3 SEK) and Norway (Norwegian krone, NOK, also free-floating, roughly €1 = 10.8 NOK) each have their own currency, and Norway isn't even part of the European Union (only of the European Economic Area). All three are countries where you pay almost entirely by card: it works everywhere, but foreign cards sometimes pay a small currency conversion fee. It's best to check your card's terms beforehand or bring one designed for travel.
- **The highest costs in Europe:** a beer at a bar costs €7-11 in Copenhagen, €6-9 in Stockholm (75-105 SEK) and as much as €9-14 in Oslo (100-130 NOK, among the highest prices in the world because of alcohol taxes). A simple meal at a casual place runs around €12-20 in all three cities; a dinner at a mid-range restaurant starts at €35-45 per person. Supermarkets (especially for beer) are much cheaper than bars everywhere.
- **Copenhagen-Stockholm:** direct SJ train, roughly **5 to 5.5 hours** (the fastest under 5 hours), with regular departures several times a day; or a direct flight, about **1 hour**, with several airlines on the route. There's no real direct alternative by sea between the two cities.
- **Stockholm-Oslo:** same choice, direct SJ train in about **5 hours 45 minutes** or a direct flight of about **1 hour**, with several departures a day for both. There's no direct Stockholm-Oslo ferry: the best-known sea link in the region, the DFDS overnight ferry of about 17 hours 45 minutes, connects Copenhagen with Oslo. Anyone with the time and the desire to try it can consider it a variation on the itinerary, outside this linear route.
- **Returning from Oslo:** Oslo Gardermoen airport is well connected to Italy; for those who still prefer to go back through Copenhagen, SAS flights between Oslo and Copenhagen are very frequent (more than 70 a week) and last just over an hour.
- **From the airport to the centre:** at **Copenhagen Kastrup (CPH)** the **M2** metro, running 24 hours a day, reaches Kongens Nytorv in about 15 minutes, the train gets to Central Station in 13: 3-zone ticket, about 30 DKK, from the machines or the DOT Billetter app. At **Stockholm Arlanda (ARN)** the **Arlanda Express** gets to Central Station in about 20 minutes, about 349 SEK, more if bought on board; slower and cheaper are the SL commuter train, with an airport surcharge, and Flygbussarna; taxis only from the big companies, at a fixed fare. At **Oslo Gardermoen (OSL)** **Flytoget** reaches Oslo S in about 20 minutes, about 276 NOK; the Vy regional train takes 23-30 minutes, about 125 NOK.
- **Christiania (Copenhagen):** the "free town" founded in 1971 has its own rules, posted and enforced: no photos on the former Pusher Street (closed by the Danish authorities in 2024 after an episode of violence) or of residents without permission. Selling drugs, hashish included, remains illegal throughout Denmark: whatever its reputation says, it isn't an outlaw enclave.
- **The Viking Ship Museum in Oslo is closed:** the historic site on the Bygdøy peninsula is under renovation and the Viking ships are being moved to the new Museum of the Viking Age; reopening isn't expected before 2027. On this itinerary you still visit Bygdøy, for the Fram Museum and the Kon-Tiki Museum, but before leaving it's worth rechecking the Viking museum's reopening date.
- **Tivoli Gardens (Copenhagen) is seasonal:** it opens in spring and summer, for Halloween and for the Christmas markets, but **closes for some winter months** outside those periods. It isn't open year-round: check the official calendar before planning your evening.
- **Documents:** Denmark and Sweden are in the European Union and the Schengen area, Norway is in the Schengen area but outside the EU. For Italian citizens, a valid ID card for travel abroad is enough in all three, with no visa for tourist stays under three months.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Arrival in Copenhagen and Nyhavn
We landed at Copenhagen-Kastrup (CPH), about 20 minutes from the centre by metro. After checking in we took a first walk towards **Nyhavn**, the seventeenth-century canal with the colourful façades of the merchants' houses, now a continuous row of cafés along the quay: it is the most photographed spot in the city and the most natural way to get your bearings. The sunny side, with the coloured houses, is the busiest; the places on the quay are expensive, and a coffee or a beer outdoors costs much more than a few blocks away.

From there we strolled along **Strøget**, one of the longest pedestrian shopping streets in Europe, to Kongens Nytorv. In the late afternoon, a canal boat tour from Nyhavn (about an hour) to see the old town from the water: there's a dedicated experience page. *The boats are open and it's windy on the water even in summer: bring a jacket.* The first evening was a quiet one, with a stop at **Torvehallerne**, the covered market near Nørreport station, for a first taste of smørrebrød, rye bread with the topping on top.

**In practice:** M2 metro from the airport to the centre in about 20 minutes, tickets from the machines or the public transport app · canal tour of about an hour, frequent departures from Nyhavn in high season, fewer in winter · in Denmark you pay by card almost everywhere.

### Day 2 — Tivoli Gardens, the Little Mermaid and the waterfront
We gave the day to the center and the waterfront. The morning at **Tivoli Gardens** (if it's in season: check the calendar before planning), open since 1843 and among the oldest amusement parks in the world still in operation. It has manicured gardens, historic wooden roller coasters and an atmosphere you can feel even just walking through, without going on any rides. In the afternoon we walked along the Langelinie waterfront to the **Little Mermaid** (Den Lille Havfrue), the 1913 bronze sculpture inspired by the Hans Christian Andersen fairy tale. It's small, often surrounded by crowds and tour groups, one of the most cited cases of an attraction that's more famous than it is impressive, but the walk around it is still worth the stop. To round off the day nicely, a ride on a rented bike along the waterfront, to try, even just for an afternoon, how most Copenhageners get around every day.

**In practice:** Tivoli is open only in certain seasons, entry around DKK 180-200 with rides extra: calendar and prices on tivoli.dk · the Little Mermaid is free, about half an hour's walk from Nyhavn · bikes for hire through the city's bike-sharing apps, to be parked only in permitted areas.

### Day 3 — Christiania, Christianshavn and the train to Stockholm
In the morning we went to **Christianshavn**, the district of smaller canals and houseboats, with the church of **Vor Frelsers Kirke** and its famous external spiral staircase around the golden spire. The climb to the spire is paid, the last stretch is outdoors on steps that narrow towards the top, and in strong wind or rain it may be closed: do it first, while the weather holds. From there we moved on to **Christiania**, the self-governing community founded in 1971: entry is free, but the community's rules must be respected without exception, so no photos on the former Pusher Street or of residents without permission.

In the early afternoon, transfer to **Stockholm** by train (direct SJ, roughly 5-5h30) or, for those who prefer to save time, on a direct flight of about an hour. The train leaves from the central station, crosses the Øresund bridge and southern Sweden, and is convenient if you have luggage. *SJ tickets cost much less booked weeks ahead; on the day they can cost twice as much or more.* We arrived in Stockholm in the evening and had dinner around Gamla Stan or Södermalm.

**In practice:** Christiania is free to enter, photos forbidden in the marked areas · Copenhagen-Stockholm SJ train to be booked on sj.se · if you fly you land at Arlanda, about 20 minutes from the centre on the Arlanda Express · in Sweden you pay by card, cash is almost never needed.

### Day 4 — Gamla Stan and Stockholm's old town
The day was for **Gamla Stan**, Stockholm's medieval old town, on an island of its own: narrow cobbled lanes, pastel houses, the square of **Stortorget** (the oldest in the city, home to the Nobel Prize Museum) and the **Royal Palace**, with the changing of the guard. It is best explored in the morning, before the main street, Västerlånggatan, fills with groups and souvenir shops: the side lanes stay quiet even at peak times. The changing of the guard usually takes place around midday, with different times on Sundays and in winter.

In the afternoon we visited the **Stadshuset**, the red-brick City Hall on the water. It hosts the Nobel Prize banquet in the Blue Hall and has the Golden Hall, covered in over 18 million gilded tiles. The interior can only be seen on a guided tour, at set times several times a day. In the warmer months you can climb the tower for the view over the city (access is seasonal: check beforehand). Free evening among the restaurants of Södermalm, where the Monteliusvägen walk, above the water, gives the best view of Gamla Stan at sunset.

**In practice:** Stadshuset only on guided tours of about 45 minutes, ticket of just over €10, tower open roughly May to September with a separate ticket · changing of the guard times on the royal court website · Gamla Stan is reached by metro, Gamla Stan stop.

### Day 5 — Vasamuseet, Stadshuset and ABBA The Museum
In the morning we went to the **Vasamuseet**, on the island of Djurgården. The museum holds the *Vasa*, a Swedish royal warship that sank in Stockholm harbour on its maiden voyage in 1628, after less than 1,300 metres of sailing, and was recovered almost intact in 1961: it is the best-preserved seventeenth-century ship in the world. Arrive at opening time: the ship hall is dark and on several levels, and it makes most sense from the upper floors, where you can see the deck. Djurgården is reached on tram 7 from the centre or by ferry from Slussen, which is already a short trip on the water.

In the afternoon, still on Djurgården, **ABBA The Museum**, devoted to the world's most famous Swedish pop group, with original memorabilia and interactive rooms where you sing and dance. Between the two museums there's time for a walk in Djurgården park, the city's green lung. If you still have energy, **Skansen**, the open-air museum of traditional Swedish houses and farms, is a few steps away. *At weekends and in summer the timed tickets for both museums sell out: buy them online.*

**In practice:** Vasamuseet open daily, in summer 8.30am to 6pm and the rest of the year 10am to 5pm, Wednesdays until 8pm; ticket around €20 · ABBA The Museum with timed entry, price varies with the period · tram 7 or ferry from Slussen to Djurgården.

### Day 6 — The Stockholm archipelago and the train to Oslo
We spent the morning on a boat trip into the **Stockholm archipelago** (Stockholms skärgård), the more than thirty thousand islands and islets that open up east of the city towards the Baltic Sea. You can take a guided 2-2.5-hour trip or, if you prefer to organise it yourself, the public Waxholmsbolaget ferries, which leave from the centre and go to the nearer islands. With only one morning available, stay in the inner archipelago rather than heading for the distant islands, which take a full day.

In the early afternoon, transfer to **Oslo**: direct SJ train (about 5h45) or domestic flight (about an hour). The train runs past the lakes and forests of western Sweden and arrives at Oslo Sentralstasjon, right in the centre; flying means landing at Gardermoen and adding the transfer into town. *Leave a margin between the morning boat and the train: bags already packed and a quick lunch near the station.* We arrived in Oslo in the evening and took a first walk towards the centre.

**In practice:** archipelago trips and Waxholmsbolaget ferries run reduced timetables out of season · Stockholm-Oslo SJ train to be booked in advance on sj.se · in Norway you pay by card, and prices are even higher than in Sweden.

### Day 7 — Central Oslo and the Opera House
We spent the day in the centre. From the station the pedestrian street of **Karl Johans gate** climbs up to the **Royal Palace Gardens**, the open park around the king's residence. From there you walk down towards the harbour and the **Aker Brygge** waterfront, the former shipyard area now full of restaurants on the water, with Akershus Fortress on the other side of the bay. At the far end, on the Tjuvholmen peninsula, is the **Astrup Fearnley Museum** (in the building designed by Renzo Piano), with its collection of contemporary art.

To finish, the **Oslo Opera House**, with its sloping façade that you walk up to the roof for views over the city and the fjord. It is the best place for sunset, and in summer the light lasts a long time at that hour. Next door is the **MUNCH** museum, for those who want to see Edvard Munch's paintings. *The marble of the roof becomes slippery in rain or ice: wear shoes with some grip.* A quiet evening, perhaps with a hot tea in the Opera House's indoor bar before dinner.

**In practice:** the Opera roof is freely accessible · Astrup Fearnley and MUNCH charge admission, Astrup Fearnley closed on Mondays, MUNCH open every day · the centre is walkable, otherwise trams, buses and metro with Oslo's public transport app.

### Day 8 — Vigeland Park and the museums of Bygdøy
In the morning we went to **Vigeland Park** (Frognerparken), the largest sculpture park in the world created by a single artist. It has more than 200 works in bronze, granite and iron by Gustav Vigeland, including the famous Monolith, a column more than 14 metres high carved with 121 intertwined human figures. Entry is free, it's always open and it's the most visited attraction in all of Norway. Early in the morning the park is almost empty, and the central route, from the bridge of statues to the Monolith, takes less than an hour.

In the afternoon we moved to the museum peninsula of **Bygdøy** for the **Fram Museum**, devoted to the polar ship used by Nansen and Amundsen, and the **Kon-Tiki Museum**, with the raft on which Thor Heyerdahl crossed the Pacific in 1947. The two museums face each other, and on the Fram you can go on board. The Viking Ship Museum, on the same peninsula, is left out of the programme: it's closed for renovation and is not expected to reopen before 2027.

**In practice:** Vigeland Park free and always open · Kon-Tiki around NOK 180, combined Fram and Kon-Tiki ticket around NOK 325 (museum websites, 2026) · Bygdøy by bus 30 from the centre all year or, in the warmer months, by ferry from the City Hall pier.

### Day 9 — Oslofjord cruise and departure
We spent the last morning on an **Oslofjord cruise**, a 1.5-2-hour trip among the islands and bays south of the city, departing from the pier in front of City Hall. It's the nicest way to say goodbye to the last of the three capitals: seeing it from the water, as with Copenhagen on the first day. On the fjord it is colder than in town even in summer: keep a jacket handy, and take an outdoor seat for photos of the Opera and Akershus Fortress on the way out.

The rest of the day is free for last-minute shopping or a final stroll, before the transfer to Oslo Gardermoen airport for the flight home (direct to Italy, or via Copenhagen for those who want to close the circle of the three capitals on the way back too). *Gardermoen is about 50 km from the centre: with the train and security, leave town at least three hours before the flight.*

**In practice:** fjord cruises with several departures a day in summer, far fewer in winter · to Gardermoen, the Flytoget express train from Oslo Sentralstasjon in about 20 minutes, or the slower and cheaper regional trains · leave bags in the central station lockers for the last morning.

## What to see / what to do
- **Nyhavn**, the colorful 17th-century canal, by day and by night.
- **Tivoli Gardens**, if it's in season: one of the oldest amusement parks in the world.
- **Christiania**, respecting the community's rules without exception.
- **Gamla Stan**, Stockholm's medieval old town, on an island all its own.
- **The Vasamuseet**, with the 1628 warship raised almost intact.
- **The Stockholm archipelago**, thirty thousand islands east of the city.
- **Vigeland Park** in Oslo, the largest sculpture park in the world by a single artist.
- **The Oslofjord cruise**, to say goodbye to the trip from the water.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **Copenhagen:** the old town (Indre By) is the most convenient choice for doing everything on foot, but also the priciest. Vesterbro, a former red-light district and now one of the liveliest for bars and restaurants, is a good compromise between price and location.
- **Stockholm:** Gamla Stan and Norrmalm remain the most central areas, convenient on foot; Södermalm, younger, is an alternative that's often cheaper, a few minutes by metro from the center.
- **Oslo:** the area around Oslo S (the central station) and Karl Johans gate has almost all the tourist offerings within walking distance.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **Copenhagen:** Torvehallerne, the covered market near Nørreport, for smørrebrød and local products; Reffen, the street food area on the waterfront (seasonal), for something more casual.
- **Stockholm:** Södermalm and Östermalm hold much of the city's dining scene, from fast-casual to high-end restaurants.
- **Oslo:** the Aker Brygge area, on the waterfront, for traditional Norwegian cuisine. Keep in mind that, of the three capitals, it's the most expensive of all for eating out.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Don't assume the euro in any of the three cities**: Denmark, Sweden and Norway have three different currencies, none of them the euro.
- **Check the Tivoli Gardens calendar before planning your evening in Copenhagen**: it's open only in certain seasons, not year-round.
- **Don't take photos in Christiania in the posted areas** (the former Pusher Street in particular) or of residents without permission.
- **Don't plan on the Viking Ship Museum in Oslo**: it's closed for renovation and reopening isn't expected before 2027. You still visit Bygdøy for the Fram Museum and the Kon-Tiki Museum.
- **Book Vasamuseet and ABBA The Museum in advance** on weekends and in high season: the most popular slots sell out.
- **Budget for the highest costs in Europe**, in all three cities: meals and transport weigh on the daily budget more than anywhere else.
- **Don't walk or stand in Copenhagen's bike lanes without looking**: they're as busy as the roads, and cyclists don't slow down for distracted tourists.
- **Check the seasonal schedules of boat outings** (Stockholm archipelago, Oslofjord cruise): in winter the offering shrinks considerably compared to May to September.

## Chiusura personale
Nine days aren't enough to really get to know three capitals, but they're enough to line them up: Copenhagen by bike, Stockholm on its islands, Oslo on the fjord. They're expensive, and that needs to be factored in from the start.
