## Apertura personale
Costa Rica is best told starting from its own motto: that «Pura Vida» which I discovered is not a tourist slogan at all, but a real way of living time, slower and less anxious than usual. I understood it from the very first days, between a casado eaten in a local soda and the first howler monkey heard shouting from the trees overhead: a sound that, the first time, makes you jump more than you'd expect. Thirteen days crossing the country from one coast to the other, from the Caribbean Sea to the Pacific, make it the densest trip among the ones told here.

Overall Costa Rica is beautiful. Incredible nature, surfing, animals everywhere. But activities are very expensive, because of the American influence: bear in mind you almost always pay in dollars, and that says it all.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** tropical climate all year, hot and humid, with possible rain especially on the coasts; in case of bad weather the itinerary may change. November is one of the months when you're less likely to meet whales at Uvita (the best season is January-March or July-October), but for the rest of the country it remains a good period.
- **Documents/visa:** passport with at least 6 months of remaining validity. For tourist stays up to 180 days no visa is needed, but you must already hold the plane ticket out of the country.
- **Currency:** Costa Rican colón (CRC), approximate rate €1 = 600 CRC. In tourist areas cards are accepted, but with high fees. Better to bring cash from Italy (better still US dollars, which circulate alongside the colón) and exchange only at banks or authorized exchange offices: in tourist centers, on the street, there are scams with "more favorable" rates that end in counterfeit bills.
- **Approximate budget:** recommended starting common fund of €350 per person (collected on site, in cash, at the welcome meeting), plus a realistic additional spend of €250 each for the extra activities. Previous groups spent between €350 and €500 in total. Activities in Costa Rica are expensive compared with the rest of Central America: a day of rafting or ziplining can cost $60-120 per person. I can confirm it: activities are very expensive and you almost always pay in dollars.
- **Tips:** at restaurants they're already included (10%). For tour guides $5-10 per person is recommended; for the driver who follows the group for almost the whole itinerary figure $25 per person at the end of the tour.
- **Getting around:** private van with a driver for all the main transfers (active from early morning until evening, also usable for trips near the hotel on days with more free time), plus boats for Tortuguero and some excursions. In San José, and for the airport-center transfer, **Uber is much better than taxis**: it costs about a third less and is safer, because you can check the license plate of the incoming car.
- **Safety:** it gave me a good impression. I never felt particularly in danger, but as in every Central American country, keep your eyes open. In the evening San José can be a bit dangerous, especially for women on their own. Better to always move in a group or by Uber and avoid the center after sunset (Barrio Escalante and La California, the neighborhoods where you usually sleep, remain safe and lively in the evening too). The same goes for Puerto Viejo at night.
- **SIM/data:** the DMC can tell you where to buy a local 5 GB SIM for $5 (about 3,000 colones). Alternatively, in San José there are Claro or Kölbi stores, also at the airport (open from early morning until evening, cash only; it's better to pay in colones, the exchange rate is better).
- **Food:** the food is ordinary, a mix of American and Central American, with lots of chicken, rice and beans. The fruit, on the other hand, is incredible. The food can be spicy; if you don't like that, just always say «no spicy» when you order. The staple dish is the **casado** (rice, beans, meat or fish, vegetables and fried plantain), found everywhere in the sodas (the cheap typical restaurants).
- **Luggage:** compact 40/50/60 liter backpack or duffel bag, no wheeled suitcase. A large backpack as a "depot" and a smaller one to fill every day come in handy. You need comfortable shoes (no demanding treks, but lots of walking), water shoes mandatory for waterfalls and snorkeling, a rain jacket or poncho mandatory, high-protection mosquito repellent, sunscreen (very expensive on site) and a type A/B adapter (120V).
- **On time, which is relative here:** it's one of the most honest notes of the official Vade Mecum. In Costa Rica **Google Maps is not reliable** for real travel times: traffic, roads and unexpected events are part of the trip, like the very long waits at restaurants. That's why at lunch you often choose a lunch box or a menu ordered in advance, so as not to lose the best hours at the waterfalls.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno
### Day 1 — San José
The group arrived in dribs and drabs during the day (Uber from the airport to the center, half an hour to an hour, about $14), and until the welcome meeting at the hotel nothing was planned. San José is only worth seeing in passing. The **Mercado Central** is nice, good also for a first typical lunch, and so is the **National Museum**, the history one. Otherwise, nothing special. If there's time left there's the colonial neighborhood of **Barrio Amón**, or the **Pre-Columbian Gold Museum**, under the Plaza de la Cultura.

The first dinner was at **Jardín de Lolita**, an informal garden where you eat from food trucks all seated together: an easy way to break the ice. Alternatively there are **Colonia Arepas y Bar**, Venezuelan and Costa Rican food, or the **Costa Rica Beer Factory**, with local craft beer. Afterwards, the "boozy" ice creams of **Booze and Scoops** and a dancing night at **La Concha**, with different music every night, in the La California neighborhood, safe and lively at night too, unlike the center.

### Day 2 — Puerto Viejo, between the Caribbean and the jungle
We left in the morning for a four to four-and-a-half-hour transfer to **Puerto Viejo**, on the Caribbean coast, with sandwiches picked up along the way at Panaderia Francesa.

On arrival you rent bikes from **Rasta Lion** (+506 8970 8522, about $6, to be returned whenever you like by late afternoon, for $5): it's the best way to get around the coast. Forty minutes of pedaling away is **Punta Uva**, the best beach on the Costa Rican Caribbean for swimming, then **Playa Chiquita** and **Cocles**, where you stop for sunset happy hour. Along the road it's easy to see howler monkeys in the trees and the big golden spiders weaving their webs, harmless despite their looks. For short stretches there are tuk tuks, about $4 per person, three at most.

You have dinner at **Madre Tierra** and, if there's live music, end up at **Hot Rocks**. At night Puerto Viejo calls for a bit more attention: better to move in a group, especially girls on their own.

*Anyone who wants the most extreme rafting of the trip can arrange, at least a week ahead, the **Pacuaré** ($112, full day) on the way to Puerto Viejo. It's among the five best rivers in the world for rafting, but four hours in the water can feel long to first-timers, especially in the dry season, when the water is low. The rafting in the package comes later anyway, on the Sarapiquí, steadier and suitable even for the less experienced.*

### Day 3 — Cahuita National Park
It's an optional day, to be arranged at least a week ahead, but it's the one I recommend most: the **full Cahuita tour** with the local guide **José** (+506 7221 4153, also on WhatsApp), $50 per person in cash, free for the coordinator.

It starts with an hour of guided walking in **Cahuita National Park**, entrance included, where you're very likely to see sloths, monkeys and several species of snakes, and you swim at a beach inside the park. Cahuita, really beautiful: we saw a nurse shark, rays, several sloths, all kinds of little monkeys and more.

After lunch you visit the **Bribrí indigenous community**: the village chief explains the properties of the traditional plants and shows the little poison frogs they raise. It's a cultural experience, but don't picture it as something authentic deep in the jungle, at least not the way I experienced it. It's nice to see how they live, or used to live, and how chocolate is made, but it's not like the Mentawai, to give you an idea. Then a traditional **chocolate workshop**, with tasting, and to finish a **waterfall** at the end of a short but steep walk, about 150 meters, where you can swim. At the bar at the top you drink a **pipa fría**, fresh coconut water, with rum for those who want it.

In the Cahuita area I also recommend the **Jaguar Rescue Center**, in Puerto Viejo. It takes in injured, sick or orphaned animals, mostly monkeys, sloths, birds and reptiles: it cares for them and, when they can live free again, releases them into the forest. You visit on guided tours, every day at 9:30 and 11:30, booked on their website.

For dinner you choose among the **Beach Break Bar and Restaurant** at Playa Cocles, the tacos of **Tasty Waves**, the pizza of **Amimodo** and the **Koki Beach Restaurant**, more elegant and more expensive, where in the evening you often see a sloth crossing the roof. Afterwards, at **Selina**, the hotel-hostel where the town's nightlife is concentrated, there's karaoke.

### Day 4 — To Tortuguero, between river and jungle
A quick breakfast and off: an hour and a half by van to the port of **Moin**, then three hours by boat to **Tortuguero**, along canals immersed in the jungle. The ride is already a spectacle, with crocodiles, caimans, birds and monkeys on the banks. Tortuguero is gorgeous, it looks a lot like the Amazon and it's fantastic for nature. We had lunch on board with a typical casado.

Tortuguero is a village with no roads for cars and you get around it all on foot, among little souvenir shops. The afternoon offers two options. The **Cerro Tortuguero**, on your own ($6 for the boat plus $3 entrance), is a short climb through the jungle to a hill with a view over the whole area. At the right time (usually between July and October, but best to ask the DMC) there's **turtle nesting**: an evening excursion guided by **Juan** (+506 8500 1369, about $40 per person) to see egg-laying live.

Dinner is included at the hotel; in town there are also **Taylor's Place** and **Mi Niño**.

### Day 5 — Tortuguero: kayaking among the mangroves, then Sarapiquí
The alarm rang before dawn for the **kayak along the canals**, included: two hours in a single or double, at the best time to see animals. We got back in time for breakfast.

In late morning we boarded for **Sarapiquí**: an hour and a half by boat and two and a half hours by van. It's worth stopping at a supermarket along the road for drinks before the lodge; in Tortuguero, in front of the pier, there are a small supermarket and a free drinking-water tank.

At the **Epic Adventure Lodge**, in the middle of the forest, there's time for the pool or for walking along the river. In the evening came the **cooking class**, included: you learn a traditional dish, usually empanadas, which then becomes dinner (vegetarians and vegans should tell the lodge beforehand). If the group prefers, on the first evening at the lodge you can also have a barbecue.

### Day 6 — Rafting on the Sarapiquí River
In the morning we left the lodge for the **rafting on the Sarapiquí**, included and already paid for, with stops for water and fruit, until noon. Compared with the Pacuaré it's a steadier river all year, suitable even for those who have never rafted.

In the afternoon, if the weather holds, you can go to the **Cataratas Bajas del Toro**, about an hour's drive away: Bajos del Toro is considered the waterfall capital of Costa Rica, with the "gemelas" and the Catarata del Toro. The packed lunch must be ordered the day before (about $12 per person, at the end of the rafting), and you have to arrive by early afternoon, because the area closes in the late afternoon. Dinner included at the lodge.

### Day 7 — Arenal: waterfalls, volcano and hot springs
An hour and twenty minutes of road to **La Fortuna**, at the foot of the Arenal volcano, with the minivan at our disposal all day. La Fortuna is nice, but a bit overtouristy. The activities are expensive, but really, really good.

The first stop is the **La Fortuna Waterfall**, probably the most photographed waterfall in the country, with a natural pool at its base where you can swim (about $18-20, from the common fund). The DMC offers a **combo tour** with lunch and three activities: for $90 the waterfall, the volcano trek at Mirador El Silencio (great views, about $10 on its own) and the hot springs; for $120, instead of the trek, the **hanging bridges** of Mistico Hanging Bridges (about $30 on their own).

The best moment comes in the late afternoon, at the **natural hot springs** of **El Choyin**, a free stretch of river, different from spa-style hot springs like Termalitas de Arenal, paid but with extras such as a barbecue. All you need are river shoes, a swimsuit and a headlamp in a waterproof bag; no towels, because it may rain and there are no changing rooms.

You have dinner at **Nanku Restaurant** with live music, at **Soda Víquez**, more traditional and cheaper, or at the Mexican **Spectacular Cantina**. Usually the party then moves to **Selina** or the **Lava Lounge**.

*We did the **canyoning in the Lost Canyon** ($115-119), and it's the activity I recommend most in La Fortuna. It's on the morning of Day 8, with lunch included, or the early afternoon of Day 7. It has its own transport, so anyone not taking part still keeps the minibus.*

### Day 8 — Monteverde, in the cloud forest
A quick breakfast and an early departure. The first stop is the **hanging bridges** near La Fortuna, a bit below expectations according to some in the group. Then three to three and a half hours of road to **Monteverde**, with lunch at one of the sodas along the way.

In the early afternoon you visit a **coffee plantation**, two hours with a final tasting: it's best to book the early-afternoon slot through the DMC, which can get group rates. Alternatively there are tours that add chocolate and sugar cane, or an excursion to the Tigre Waterfalls with lunch included.

You have dinner at **Tree House**, with a supermarket next door for drinks, or at **Tico y Rico**, **The Green Restaurant** or **Sabor Tico**. In the evening there's the **night walk** in the cloud forest, an hour and a half to see the night's wildlife: the Kinkajou Night Walk Tour ($28) is considered the best. *It wasn't liked equally by everyone, especially for its length: if possible, better to ask the guide to keep it between an hour and a quarter and an hour and a half.*

### Day 9 — Zipline in Monteverde, then toward the Pacific and Uvita
Anyone who wants to start early can do **birdwatching at dawn** with Jason (+506 8636 0491, about $50 per person).

Then the **ziplines**: those in Monteverde are among the best in the country, longer than the ones in La Fortuna. There's the giant zip line, and it's awesome. To give you an idea, 100% Aventura states on its website that it has the longest cable in Costa Rica, 1,590 meters. There are two shifts, at dawn and in late morning; they cost about $60 and last three hours. Anyone who doesn't feel up to it after the third line stops and gets a 75% refund.

After lunch, for example at **Tico y Rico**, up to four hours of road awaited us toward **Uvita**, on the Pacific. The stop at the **Tárcoles bridge** is a must: below, in the river, big crocodiles gather. If time allows, a sunset on a beach before the hotel. Dinner at the **Gastro Park** in Uvita (let them know beforehand, there's often a 10% discount).

### Day 10 — Uvita: whale watching
It's **whale watching** day. In Uvita the best seasons are January-March and July-October; November-December and May-June can bring fewer encounters. The outing lasts three and a half hours, four, and costs $70 with water and fruit included. For the whale tour I recommend **Uvita Ballena Jorobada** (+506 8820 6443, also on WhatsApp). *The DMC warns against improvised operators, who often lack adequate insurance: always ask before booking.*

The afternoon is free at the beach. The coast of Uvita, with the whale-tail-shaped sandbar, the **Cola de la Ballena**, which you see mostly at low tide, is one of the most beautiful landscapes of the trip. Dinner at the hotel.

### Day 11 — Uvita: free day for surfing, horses and yoga
The last full day in Uvita goes at everyone's own pace. At **Rancho La Merced** (+506 8861 5147) we did the sunset **horseback ride** on the beach, two hours, about $52. I fell off, because I'm not an experienced rider, but it's beautiful. The animals are treated well, which matters hugely to me, and they're gentle. Highly recommended. Costa Rica is also great for **surfing**: in Uvita I went with **Ariel** (+506 6184 0390). At Nomadic Surf you rent boards for the whole day, about $70 for four. At sunset there's an hour of **yoga** on a deck with a very beautiful view, about $10, ten people at most. Or just beach, perhaps walking to the tip of the Cola de la Ballena. Dinner is on your own.

### Day 12 — Manuel Antonio National Park and return to San José
We left before dawn, with a quick breakfast, for an hour and a half of road to **Manuel Antonio National Park**, one of the best stops in Costa Rica.

The guided visit, included, lasts one to two hours, depending on pace and encounters: the guide helps you see sloths, monkeys, iguanas and hummingbirds along the trails. Then there's time for one of the most beautiful beaches in the country, inside the park. Manuel Antonio is a beautiful park, but with so many people you almost feel like you're going to the zoo. It's still very beautiful. *The park is closed on Tuesdays: if Day 12 falls on a Tuesday, the visit moves to Day 10 or 11.*

What remained was the last long transfer, three and a half or four hours, to **San José**, with a stop for souvenirs. The farewell dinner was at the **Costa Rica Beer Factory**, where we'd stopped the first night, for a last group toast with craft beer.

### Day 13 — Check-out and return
Goodbyes and departures, each with their own flight time.

## What to see / what to do
- **The boat ride to Tortuguero**, among jungle canals, crocodiles and birds: one of the best transfers of the trip, more than a simple stop along the way.
- **The free hot springs of El Choyin**, in Arenal: far more authentic (and free) than the paid "postcard" hot springs.
- **Manuel Antonio National Park**, with some of the most beautiful beaches in the country and a very high chance of seeing sloths and monkeys. Beautiful, but very crowded: it almost feels like a zoo.
- **Tortuguero**, which looks a lot like the Amazon.
- **Canyoning in La Fortuna and the giant zip line in Monteverde**, the two most adrenaline-filled activities of the trip.
- **Corcovado**, which I did and it's amazing. You can only go in with an authorized guide and a reservation (Costa Rica's tourism board says at least six months ahead). You get there by boat from Drake Bay, Sierpe or Puerto Jiménez, or on foot from the La Leona sector, near Playa Carate.
- **Whale watching in Uvita**, if you travel in the right season.
- **Cahuita**, really beautiful: nurse shark, rays, sloths and monkeys. The same tour includes the Bribri community with the chocolate workshop. It's a cultural experience, interesting for seeing how they live and how chocolate is made, but don't expect something authentic deep in the jungle: it's not like the Mentawai.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Beware of exchanging "on the street"**: in tourist centers there are scams with attractive rates followed by counterfeit bills. Exchange only at banks or authorized offices.
- **Google Maps is not reliable in Costa Rica**: traffic, road conditions and unexpected events always stretch the planned times, so it's best to plan with a margin.
- **Optional activities must be booked at least a week in advance**, especially Cahuita NP and the Pacuaré rafting, which require providers outside the DMC.
- **Beware of "street" tours** offered by anyone not going through the DMC: lower prices often mean no insurance, and in an emergency the DMC cannot step in on activities it didn't organize.
- **At restaurants, especially at lunch, better to choose lunch boxes or menus ordered in advance**: service is slow and risks eating up the best hours for the afternoon activities.
- **Budget for prices in dollars**: activities are very expensive because of the American influence, and you almost always pay in dollars.
- **See San José only in passing**: the market and the National Museum, then move on.
- **The humidity is constant**: hung-up clothes don't always dry, so it's worth packing a few extra items.
- A group playlist on Spotify for the many long transfers: a simple way to make them part of the trip instead of dead time.

## Chiusura personale
Between the Caribbean of Puerto Viejo and the Pacific of Uvita there's no real winner: the first for the jungle, the howler monkeys and the two Temazcal experienced as true spiritual ceremonies; the second for the slower pace, the beaches and, with a bit of luck in the wrong season, even a few whale sightings. Thirteen days this dense paid off, but if I were to do it again I'd slow down on Monteverde and Manuel Antonio, the two stops where I'd have liked more time to enjoy nature instead of rushing toward the next transfer.

## Continuing to Panama
An option, instead of ending the trip in San José: from the Cahuita area you carry on to Panama. We went to the border, crossed the bridge, and the Panama adventure began. If you want to combine the two countries, this is how you get there from Costa Rica.

- **Puerto Viejo or Cahuita → Sixaola:** an hour to an hour and a half by local bus to Sixaola, the Costa Rican border town. Alternatively, there are door-to-door shuttles all the way to Bocas.
- **Costa Rica exit tax:** leaving by land costs $8 per person, paid online on the Banco de Costa Rica website or at the BCR kiosks at the Sixaola border post, in dollars or colones.
- **The bridge:** since 2021 there's the new binational bridge over the Sixaola River, which replaced the old railway bridge. You walk across in a few minutes.
- **Entering Panama, at Guabito:** passport valid for at least six months. They may ask for an onward ticket, especially if you arrive by land from Costa Rica, and proof of at least $1,000 or a credit card. Panama is one hour ahead of Costa Rica.
- **Guabito → Bocas del Toro:** bus or shared taxi to Changuinola and then to Almirante. From there the water taxi reaches Bocas Town in about half an hour.

The border offices close in the late afternoon: to reach Bocas the same day, set off early in the morning.
