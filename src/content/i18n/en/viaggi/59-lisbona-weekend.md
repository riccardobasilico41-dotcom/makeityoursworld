# Lisbon in a Long Weekend

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 in progress (trip taken by Rick; itinerary and practical information verified)
**Periodo del viaggio:**
**Durata:** 4 days, 3 nights
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independently, on foot and by public transport
**Categoria:** Europa · Portogallo · Lisbona · città · storia · gastronomia

---

## Apertura personale
Lisbon is a city you experience uphill: seven hills overlooking the Tagus, a historic tram that squeezes through alleys barely wider than its own carriage, and viewpoints (the *miradouros*) that open up suddenly at the top of any old staircase. It's also a city rebuilt twice in its recent history: after the devastating earthquake of 1755, which shaped the Enlightenment-era Baixa on the grid plan that now separates the oldest neighborhoods from the river, and more recently by a tourism boom that has quickly changed its prices and its face.

Four days are enough to do both well: the Lisbon of Alfama, Belém and Baixa-Chiado, and a separate day for **Sintra**, the landscape of romantic palaces an hour away by train that is, at once, the loveliest and the most crowded day trip from the capital.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** **April-May and September-October**: mild weather, fewer tourists, temperatures that are manageable for the city's climbs. **June-August** is high season: intense heat, Tram 28 and Sintra at capacity, long lines everywhere. **November-March** is mild by European standards, but rainy.
- **Currency:** **euro**, same as in Italy: no exchange needed.
- **The hills:** Lisbon is not a flat city. Historic trams, funiculars (*elevadores*) and the **Elevador de Santa Justa**, the wrought-iron panoramic elevator, exist precisely for the steepest climbs. It's worth factoring this in when planning your days, rather than discovering it on foot.
- **Tram 28, watch out for pickpockets:** the historic line that crosses Alfama, Graça, Baixa and Estrela is also the public transport option most often reported for pickpocketing in the city, along with the more touristy funiculars. Better to take it early in the morning, both to avoid the crush and because of the risk.
- **Sintra, logistics matter more than anything:** train from **Rossio** (about 40 minutes), then the local **434** bus or a walk up to the Palácio da Pena. In high season the lines at the main palaces grow long by mid-morning: **leaving as early as possible and booking timed tickets online** makes the difference between a successful day and one spent in line.
- **Getting around the city:** metro, buses, suburban trains and trams are paid for with the contactless **Viva Viagem/Navegante** card. The center is easy to get around on foot anyway, with a bit more breath for the hills.
- **From the airport to the centre:** from **Humberto Delgado (LIS)** the metro, red line, takes **20-25 minutes** to the center with one change at Alameda or São Sebastião: about €1.90 a ride plus €0.50 for the Navegante ocasional card, sold only at the station machines; trains from about 6:30 to 1:00 · Carris buses 744 and 783 at the city fare, slow with luggage · metered taxis about €12-20 with luggage, more at night · Uber and Bolt about €10-15, from the dedicated pick-up area · the typical mistake is the taxi that takes the long way or adds made-up surcharges: insist on the meter being on.
- **Food:** the **pastel de nata**, everywhere in the city but born at **Pastéis de Belém** in 1837; **bacalhau**, in hundreds of variations; **petiscos**, small plates to share; **ginjinha**, a sour cherry liqueur served in the tiny historic kiosks of the Baixa.
- **Time zone:** one hour behind Italy all year round (UTC+0): when it's 12:00 in Italy, it's 11:00 in Lisbon. Worth keeping in mind for train times and reservations.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Alfama and Tram 28
We spent the first day in the oldest part of the city, the **Alfama**, which survived the 1755 earthquake almost intact thanks to its Arab layout of narrow alleys. It is worth starting at the top, at opening time of the **Castelo de São Jorge**, before the groups arrive: we climbed all the way up for the view over the red roofs sloping down towards the Tagus, with the walls and courtyards still quiet. Then you walk down, unhurried, following the lanes and stairways and stopping at the **miradouros**: the **Miradouro da Senhora do Monte**, on the Graça hill, and the **Miradouro das Portas do Sol** are among the finest. On the way down you pass the **Sé**, the city's Romanesque cathedral.

In the afternoon, a ride on **Tram 28**, the historic line of vintage wooden carriages that crosses Alfama, Graça, Baixa and Estrela along very tight bends and slopes (there's a dedicated experience page, with a few precautions about pickpockets). *In the middle of the day it is packed to the doors: boarding at the Martim Moniz terminus helps you get a seat, and keep your backpack in front.* In the evening you go back to Alfama for **live fado** in one of the neighbourhood's traditional fado houses: you eat while listening, and everyone falls silent during the songs.

**In practice:** Castelo de São Jorge around €15-17, tickets also on the official website · Tram 28 with the Viva Viagem/Navegante card, tickets bought on board cost more · fado houses usually charge for dinner or a minimum spend: book on Fridays and Saturdays.

### Day 2 — Belém
The day was for **Belém**, the riverside district from which the great Portuguese expeditions of the Age of Discovery set sail, about half an hour from the centre by tram 15 or by train from Cais do Sodré. In the morning we visited the **Mosteiro dos Jerónimos**, a Manueline masterpiece and UNESCO World Heritage Site, built from 1501 to celebrate Vasco da Gama's return from India. The church is free; the cloister, two storeys of carved stone, is the paid part and the one worth queuing for. Go in at opening time, because the groups arrive mid-morning.

A few minutes' walk away is the **Torre de Belém**, the fortress that symbolises the city, built on the waters of the Tagus in the early sixteenth century and also a UNESCO site. In between, a compulsory stop at **Pastéis de Belém**, the original pastry shop since 1837 where the pastel de nata recipe was born (there's a dedicated experience page): *the long queue is for takeaway, while the indoor rooms move faster.* In the late afternoon we walked back towards the centre along the river, with the last light of the day on the **Padrão dos Descobrimentos**, the monument to the Portuguese discoveries, and the large compass rose laid into the pavement in front of it.

**In practice:** Jerónimos and Torre de Belém closed on Mondays · Jerónimos cloister around €18, with a combined ticket for the tower · Torre de Belém visits run on timed slots with capped numbers; check hours and price on the Portuguese cultural heritage website before going · tram 15 from Praça da Figueira or Praça do Comércio, train from Cais do Sodré in a few minutes.

### Day 3 — Sintra
A full day outside Lisbon, to be planned accordingly: we left as early as possible on the train from **Rossio** to **Sintra**, a UNESCO site since 1995, about forty minutes away. From the station to the **Palácio Nacional da Pena**, the nineteenth-century Romantic royal palace with its coloured towers perched on a hill, you go up on local bus 434 or on foot, on a steep climb. At Pena it pays to be among the first: after mid-morning the queues grow quickly, and entry to the interior is at a fixed time, so late arrivals risk missing it.

Then the **Quinta da Regaleira**, with its famous **Poço Iniciático**, the spiral well that descends underground among Masonic symbols, and, time permitting, the ruins of the ninth-century Moorish **Castelo dos Mouros**, with walls running along the ridge. *Doing all three sites in one day only works if you start early: with little time, choose Pena and Regaleira.* We returned to Lisbon in the evening, after a stop for the local pastries (**travesseiros** and **queijadas**) in one of the historic pastry shops in the centre of Sintra. Trains to Rossio run frequently until the evening.

**In practice:** Rossio-Sintra train about 40 minutes, with the Viva Viagem/Navegante card · Palácio da Pena around €20 with timed entry, Quinta da Regaleira around €15-20, both to be booked online on the official websites · comfortable shoes: there is a lot of uphill walking between the parks.

### Day 4 — Baixa, Chiado and a last stroll
The last day was for the centre rebuilt after the 1755 earthquake: the **Baixa**, with its Enlightenment grid plan, and the **Praça do Comércio** facing the Tagus, reached by walking under the **Arco da Rua Augusta**. Early in the morning the square is almost empty, which is the best time to see it. From there you climb to the **Chiado**, the city's most elegant district, with historic bookshops and period cafés: here is the **Livraria Bertrand**, considered the oldest bookshop in the world still open, and a little further on the café **A Brasileira**, with the statue of Fernando Pessoa sitting at a table.

We went up the **Elevador de Santa Justa**, the neo-Gothic wrought-iron lift, for the view over the roofs of the Baixa and the Castelo de São Jorge in the distance. *The queue at the bottom can be long: the walkway at the top, with much of the view, can also be reached on foot from Largo do Carmo, without taking the lift.* The afternoon was left free for last shopping and a final stop at a historic kiosk for a glass of **ginjinha** before heading home, served with or without the cherries inside.

**In practice:** Elevador de Santa Justa around €5-6, covered by the Lisboa Card · to the airport, red metro line, 20-25 minutes from the centre · leave bags at the hotel or a left-luggage office and allow at least two hours before the flight.

## What to see / what to do
- **The Alfama and its miradouros**, the neighborhood that survived the 1755 earthquake.
- **Tram 28**, early in the morning to avoid the crush and the pickpockets.
- **Belém**: Mosteiro dos Jerónimos and Torre de Belém, UNESCO World Heritage sites.
- **A pastel de nata at Pastéis de Belém**, where the recipe was born.
- **Sintra**, with the Palácio da Pena and the Quinta da Regaleira, leaving as early as possible.
- **The Baixa and the Chiado**, the center rebuilt after 1755.
- **Live fado** in a fado house in the Alfama.

## Possible extensions
- **Cascais and the Boca do Inferno (+1 day):** about 40 minutes by train from Cais do Sodré station, to the coastal town that was once a royal summer residence and the rock formation of the Boca do Inferno.
- **Óbidos (+half a day):** about an hour by bus, one of the most photogenic walled medieval villages in Portugal.
- **Porto:** not a one-day extension. It's another Portuguese city, about 3 hours by train, and deserves an itinerary of its own.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
**Baixa-Chiado** is the most central and practical area, well connected with the rest of the city. The **Alfama** is the more characterful and lived-in alternative, but with steeper climbs and narrower streets.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
The **pastel de nata** at Pastéis de Belém and in neighborhood pastry shops; **bacalhau** in one of its hundreds of variations; shared **petiscos** in a place in the Bairro Alto or the Alfama; **ginjinha** at a historic kiosk in the Baixa, standing up, as tradition demands.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Take Tram 28 early in the morning**: in the middle of the day it becomes almost unusable because of the crush and the pickpockets.
- **Book Sintra tickets online with a time slot**, and leave as early as possible.
- **Don't underestimate the climbs**: plan your days taking the elevation into account, not just the distances on the map.
- **Don't look for the "real" pastel de nata only at Pastéis de Belém**: the ones from neighborhood pastry shops are still excellent and have no line.
- **Remember the time zone**: one hour behind Italy, useful to avoid mistakes with train times and reservations.
- **Bring a light umbrella off-season**: November-March is mild but rainy.

## Chiusura personale
What stays with me is the contrast between the slow rhythm of the climbs and miradouros in the city and the race against the clock to be among the first at the Palácio da Pena: two different speeds in the same weekend, which capture well the dual nature of this destination (a city to live slowly, a day trip to tackle with a method).
