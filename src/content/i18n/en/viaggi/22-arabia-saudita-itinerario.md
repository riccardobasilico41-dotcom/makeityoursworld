# Saudi Arabia in Two Weeks: Jeddah, Hegra, Riyadh and the Asir Mountains

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 in progress (itinerary and practical information verified and up to date)
**Periodo del viaggio:**
**Durata:** 12 days, 11 nights
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independent, with domestic flights, a rental car and an e-visa
**Categoria:** Medio Oriente · Arabia Saudita · archeologia · deserto · montagna

---

## Apertura personale
Until 2019 this trip couldn't be done: the tourist visa didn't exist, and you entered Saudi Arabia for work, business or pilgrimage. Then in a few months everything changed: you apply for the visa online in half an hour, it arrives by email, and you find yourself inside a country seven times the size of Italy that almost nobody has ever visited. What you find looks nothing like the Gulf neighbors: there's a Nabataean city that's Petra's twin, with one hundred and eleven tombs and nobody in front of them; an old town of coral stone that stands up on scaffolding; a capital built almost entirely after the 1970s with its original capital in mud brick right beside it; and in the southwest a region of green mountains and terraces that undercuts the very idea of Arabia. It's also a country in extremely rapid transformation and full of contradictions, and that has to be said: whoever travels there today sees it at a precise moment, which in five years will already be a different one.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** **November to March** for the whole country, with December and February as the best months for AlUla and the desert. In summer the interior goes past 45-50 degrees (113-122°F) and Jeddah becomes unbearable because of the humidity. **The exception is the Asir:** those mountains sit above 2,000 meters (6,600 ft) and their good season is summer, when Saudis go up in droves to cool off. Whoever wants to see them in winter too will find cold and fog, and has to take that into account.
- **Visa:** two routes. The **electronic tourist visa** at visa.visitsaudi.com, about 535 riyals (around 130 euros, mandatory health insurance included), valid for **one year with multiple entries** and stays of up to 90 days. Or, flying Saudia or flynas, the **free stopover visa**, valid for 4 days: it's not enough for this itinerary, but it's enough for Jeddah plus AlUla.
- **Alcohol:** **it doesn't exist in the country**, in any form and in any hotel, and bringing it in is a serious offense. There are no free zones.
- **Mecca and Medina:** **Mecca is off-limits to non-Muslims**, and signs on the highways show the mandatory detours, with checkpoints. Medina has more open rules, but the area of the Prophet's mosque remains closed. It's no formality you can get around.
- **Dress:** modest for everyone. Since 2019 foreign women **are not required to wear the abaya or cover their heads**, but arms and legs must be covered and clothes must not be tight. For men, no shorts in public. Public displays of affection should be avoided.
- **Photography:** photographing people, and women in particular, without explicit permission is a more serious problem than elsewhere in the region. Always ask, and accept a no.
- **Distances and transport:** enormous. From Jeddah to AlUla it is over 700 km, from Riyadh to Jeddah almost 950. **Domestic flights are the norm**: Saudia and flynas connect everything at low prices, and this itinerary includes three. A rental car is useful in AlUla and the Asir; the main roads are excellent and **gas costs next to nothing**. In the cities Uber and Careem work well and are cheap.
- **Payments:** the country has gone digital fast, and in cities cards and contactless work everywhere, taxis included. Cash is needed in the souqs and villages.
- **Connectivity:** excellent even in the desert along the main routes and, unlike the Emirates and Qatar, **VoIP calls are not blocked**: WhatsApp works normally.
- **Health:** no mandatory vaccinations for tourism. The insurance included in the visa is basic: it's worth adding your own. The real risk is the heat, with dehydration. Tap water is not drinkable.
- **Safety:** petty crime is very low, the trip is trouble-free. Check the official advisories for the **border areas with Yemen**, which are not recommended, and which concerns the south of the Asir.
- **Ramadan:** here it is observed more strictly than elsewhere in the Gulf. Almost everything closes by day, **eating and drinking in public in daylight is forbidden for everyone**, non-Muslims included, and life shifts entirely to the night.
- **Budget:** higher than you'd expect. AlUla has few facilities and high prices, Riyadh and Jeddah have capital-city hotels, and the domestic flights add up. On the other hand the alcohol line doesn't exist, gas costs nothing and neighborhood food is cheap.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Arrival in Jeddah
We landed at King Abdulaziz (JED), the natural gateway to western Saudi Arabia. To sleep, near Al-Balad or on the Corniche. The airport is north of the city, and the simplest way to reach the hotel is an Uber or Careem booked in the app, which costs little and saves haggling with taxis. It's worth activating an eSIM or buying a local SIM with your passport right away, because in Saudi Arabia almost everything goes through your phone: transport, site tickets, bookings.

A light first evening: the **Corniche**, with the water jet of King Fahd's Fountain, and a seafood dinner. Thursday evening is the best moment to understand where you've landed: families spread rugs on the lawn, light the barbecues and stay until midnight. The Saudi weekend falls on Friday and Saturday, and Thursday evening is its start. *The fountain runs mostly in the evening, and is best seen from the central stretch of the seafront.*

**In practice:** airport to center by Uber or Careem, times vary a lot with traffic · eSIM or SIM with your passport, WhatsApp works · the Corniche is free and busy until late · dress covered in the evening too, men included.

### Day 2 — Al-Balad
The morning in the new districts or by the sea, and **the afternoon in Al-Balad**, because that's where it should be spent.

The UNESCO-listed old town is made of tower houses in **coral stone** quarried from the Red Sea, with the **rawashin**: carved teak wood facades up to four stories high, brought from India on the same ships that carried the pilgrims. A city built with what passed through the port.

It should be seen **in the last hour of light**. By day it's a dusty neighborhood full of scaffolding: many houses are propped up, some collapsed in the years when nobody looked after them, and the restoration only started recently and is going ahead in patches. Then the light drops, catches the carved wood, and everything changes.

Inside, worth seeing: **Nassif House**, with the inner ramp built so camels could climb up, and the **Al-Alawi souq**, still working.

**In practice:** Al-Balad is explored on foot, in comfortable shoes and without hurry · arrive an hour and a half before sunset · souq shops close for prayers, life picks up again after maghrib · cash is useful at the smaller stalls · dinner in the district or on the Corniche, for mandi and Red Sea fish.

### Day 3 — Flight to AlUla
Domestic flight to AlUla (ULH), about an hour and a half. *Alternatively it's seven hours by road from Jeddah: it can be done, because the roads are excellent and gas costs next to nothing, but it takes a full day.* On arrival you pick up the rental car, which in AlUla you really need: the sites are spread over tens of kilometers along the valley and public transport is limited. Check in, drop the bags and head out in the late afternoon, when the light is better.

The afternoon served to settle in: the **old town of AlUla**, the maze of mud houses at the foot of the citadel, and **Elephant Rock** at sunset, the sandstone monolith eroded into the shape of an elephant. It's the local evening gathering spot, with fires lit and seats carved into the sand. In the old town the restored street is full of cafés and workshops, and you climb to the citadel to see the palm grove and the abandoned houses from above.

**In practice:** Saudia and flynas flights from Jeddah and Riyadh, to be booked together with the Hegra date · rental car to book ahead in high season · site hours and tickets on experiencealula.com · few, expensive places to stay between December and February: book early.

### Day 4 — Hegra
The reason for the trip. **Booking is mandatory and must be done weeks ahead**: at Hegra you enter only with a guided visit and in limited numbers, not independently and not with your own vehicle. You board the site's buses with a *rawi*, a storyteller, almost always a local.

One hundred and eleven monumental tombs carved into sandstone between the 1st century BC and the 1st century AD by the same Nabataeans as Petra. The facades are elaborate and better preserved than at Petra, because the sandstone here is harder, and the **inscriptions on the lintels are still legible**: the owner's name, the year, a warning to anyone who violates the tomb.

The piece that explains how they worked is **Qasr al-Farid**, the "lonely tomb". It was left unfinished: the upper part is done, the lower part is still raw rock, and you can tell they carved from the top down.

*It should also be said what's missing: there's no Siq to build up the surprise, there's no Treasury, and the tombs are one hundred and eleven against Petra's hundreds. What's here, and what Petra no longer has, is silence.*

**In practice:** Hegra Day Tour by bus with a guide from about 95 riyals per person, private 4x4 tour much pricier and sold per vehicle · book only on the official Experience AlUla site · lasts 2-3 hours · hat, water and closed shoes, because you walk on sand under the sun.

### Day 5 — Jabal Ikmah, Dadan and the oasis
The day almost everyone squeezes and that stands on its own.

We went to **Jabal Ikmah** early in the morning, in raking light: a gorge with walls covered in hundreds of inscriptions in Dadanitic, Lihyanite, Nabataean and ancient Arabic, left by those who passed through twenty-five hundred years ago. They call it an open-air library, and it's a kind of public register carved in stone.

Then **Dadan**, the capital of the kingdoms of Dadan and Lihyan, older than Hegra, with tombs carved high up in the wall.

The afternoon in the **AlUla oasis**: hundreds of thousands of date palms, shaded paths among the fields and dates bought from the growers. After two days of sandstone and sun, the shade of the oasis is exactly what you need.

**In practice:** Dadan and Jabal Ikmah are visited with tickets or tours booked on the official site, often with a shuttle from Winter Park · go in the first morning slot for the light on the inscriptions · the oasis paths are free, dates are bought by weight from the growers · in the middle of the day it's better to retreat to the shade.

### Day 6 — Flight to Riyadh
Domestic flight AlUla-Riyadh, about two hours. We settled in the modern center, around Olaya or King Fahd Road. Riyadh is a city built for the car, with distances of tens of kilometers and few sidewalks: you do everything by Uber or Careem, which are cheap. If you land in the morning there's time to rest for an hour before going out, because the day is concentrated in the afternoon.

The afternoon at the **Saudi National Museum**, the best in the country for understanding the history of the peninsula: it's best to see it **before** Diriyah, not after.

Then **Masmak Fort**, the mud fortress wedged among the skyscrapers from which Ibn Saud's reconquest started in 1902. Between them there are little more than a hundred years, and it's the quickest way to understand how fast this country has changed. *The area around Masmak, with the souq and Deira square, is easy to walk in the evening, when it cools down.*

**In practice:** National Museum with split hours and evening openings on some days, free or token admission, to check on visitsaudi.com · Masmak free, closed in the middle of the day and reopened in the afternoon · hours change during Ramadan · Uber or Careem for every trip.

### Day 7 — Diriyah
The morning is free in the city: the **Al-Zal souq**, the Deira district, the **Kingdom Centre** skybridge for the view from above. Souq Al-Zal is the traditional market for carpets, incense, clothes and antiques, and it's livelier after the afternoon prayer than early in the morning; the skybridge, the suspended bridge between the two tips of the tower, makes sense with a clear sky.

The **late afternoon at Diriyah**, which is when it should be seen. It's the original Saud capital, founded in the 15th century along Wadi Hanifa and built entirely of **mud brick and straw**, with exposed palm beams and triangular motifs on the facades that served to circulate air. Destroyed by the Ottomans in 1818, restored in recent years, a UNESCO site since 2010.

In the last hour of light the color goes from ochre to burnt orange. At noon the mud reflects the light and the photos come out flat.

**In practice:** At-Turaif with a ticket of about 100 riyals, available on diriyah.sa, with reduced evening rates reported at some times · evening hours until late, to check on the site · after the visit, dinner on Bujairi Terrace opposite, expensive but with a view of the walls · Diriyah is about half an hour from the center by Uber.

### Day 8 — The Edge of the World
A day that's worth the trip as much as Hegra, and is its opposite: there everything is managed and limited, here there's nothing.

It's an hour and a half from Riyadh, and the last half hour is on a track. **You need a 4x4 with a driver**: there are no signs, the phone signal comes and goes, and every year someone goes on their own and gets stuck.

You reach the top of the **Tuwaiq escarpment** and in front of you there's nothing left: three hundred meters of vertical wall and then a flat plain that continues until it vanishes in the haze. No railing, no ticket office, no kiosk.

**A serious caveat:** the edge is crumbly rock and there is no protection. The photo everyone takes sitting on the rim is taken a few centimeters from a three-hundred-meter drop.

It's best to go in the afternoon and stay for sunset, when the rock turns red, knowing that the return on the track is then done in the dark.

**In practice:** tour with a 4x4 and driver booked from Riyadh, usually a half day in the afternoon · bring water, food, a torch and a jacket, because it cools down at sunset · access may be closed by the authorities after rain or in the hottest months · no services on site.

### Day 9 — Flight to Abha
Domestic flight to Abha (AHB), about an hour and a half. You climb above 2,000 meters (6,600 ft) and everything changes: green mountains, fog, cultivated terraces. At the airport you pick up the rental car, essential in the Asir because villages and viewpoints are spread along mountain roads with no useful public transport. Take the climate seriously: after the desert you arrive in cool temperatures, and in the evening you need a sweater.

We spent the afternoon in **Abha**, among the painted houses and the market. The **Asiri qatt**, the colorful geometric patterns the women paint on the interiors of the houses, is UNESCO intangible heritage and has nothing in common with the austerity of the Najd.

*If the trip is in winter, you find cold and fog here: it's the only region of the country with an inverted calendar, and its good season is summer.*

**In practice:** Saudia and flynas flights from Riyadh and Jeddah · rental car to book ahead, especially in summer when the Asir is full of domestic tourists · drive carefully in the fog on the hairpin bends · city hotels at moderate prices, few places in the villages.

### Day 10 — Rijal Almaa
A day in the best-known village of the Asir, **Rijal Almaa**: built of stone and quartz on several levels, with facades that glitter in the sun and white windows that stand out against the gray. It's a candidate for UNESCO World Heritage and houses a museum in the restored houses. You drive down from Abha on a road of hairpin bends towards the Tihama, the coastal plain, and it's best to arrive in the morning, when the light on the stone is better and it's less hot than in the valley.

In the afternoon we drove around the mountain villages, among terraces and fog, as far as **Habala**, the village on the escarpment that was once reached only by rope ladders. Today you get there by a cable car that runs down the wall, and the viewpoint at the top is the best place to understand how steep this mountain is. *The cable car has limited hours and can stop in the wind: ask at the hotel before setting off.*

**In practice:** Abha-Rijal Almaa about an hour of mountain road · village museum with a ticket of a few tens of riyals, hours to check on site · Habala cable car paid, variable hours · dress in layers: between the valley floor and the ridges the temperature changes a lot.

### Day 11 — The mountains
The slow day: the **Asir cable car**, the trails on **Jebel Sawda** (the highest point in the country) and the **livestock and flower market**, which here is still the social event of the week. Vendors wear flower garlands on their heads, a custom that doesn't exist in the rest of the peninsula. The market is held on a fixed day of the week, different from town to town, so ask at the hotel; go early in the morning, when the herders arrive.

Asir honey is the most prized in Arabia and sells for substantial prices; then there's **areeka**, the bread with honey and clarified butter. On Jebel Sawda, at around 3,000 meters, there are junipers, fog and baboons that approach the cars: don't feed them.

*Best not to approach the border area with Yemen, which official advisories recommend against.*

**In practice:** Jebel Sawda about half an hour to an hour from Abha by car · cable cars are paid, with hours that change with the season · for honey, buy from producers or specialist shops and ask to taste · check official travel advisories before driving south.

### Day 12 — Return
Flight from Abha with a connection in Jeddah or Riyadh, and home. Abha airport is small and close to the city, but almost all international flights go through the two hubs, so it's best to book the domestic leg on the same ticket as the flight to Europe, so you don't risk the connection. If the flight is in the afternoon, the morning is enough for a last walk around Abha's market or an Arabic coffee with a view of the mountains.

Before leaving you return the rental car with a full tank, which here costs very little, and leave some margin for the road to the airport, which can be slow in the fog. If you have the annual tourist visa you can come back several times within the year, and that's a good reason to leave the Red Sea coast or the desert for a second trip.

**In practice:** leave at least 3 hours between the domestic and the international flight if the tickets are separate · car return at the airport · Asir honey travels well in checked luggage, not in hand luggage · tourist visa valid one year with multiple entries.

## What to see / what to do
- **Book Hegra weeks ahead.** You enter only with a limited-number guided visit.
- **Al-Balad and Diriyah in the last hour of light**, never at noon.
- **Jabal Ikmah early in the morning**, with raking light on the inscriptions.
- **The National Museum before Diriyah**, not after.
- **The Edge of the World with a 4x4 and a guide**, never alone.
- **The AlUla oasis**, after two days of sandstone.
- **The Jeddah Corniche on Thursday evening**, the most social thing of the trip.
- **The Asir**, the part nobody expects and that almost no itinerary includes.

## Possible extensions
- **Umluj and the Red Sea (+2 days between Jeddah and AlUla):** about a hundred white-sand islands and reefs among the least damaged on the planet, because until 2019 no tourist could get there. No infrastructure and few operators. The window is closing fast, because mega tourism projects are rising on that coast.
- **The Rub' al-Khali desert (+3 days from Riyadh):** the Empty Quarter, the largest continuous sand desert in the world, with organized multi-day expeditions.
- **The eastern coast, Dammam and Al-Ahsa (+2 days):** the largest oasis in the world, a UNESCO site, and the Persian Gulf side.
- **The Gulf on a stopover (+2-4 days):** Dubai, Abu Dhabi and Doha are all an hour and a half by air, with their stopover programs. The site has a dedicated itinerary.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **Jeddah:** near Al-Balad to stay in the historic part, or on the Corniche for the sea. The city is spread out, and location matters.
- **AlUla:** **book well in advance**. Facilities are few relative to demand and prices are high, especially between December and February, when there are festivals. They range from desert resorts to simple accommodations in the new town.
- **Riyadh:** the modern center around Olaya or King Fahd Road, for transport and restaurants. Without Uber you can't do anything: the city stretches for tens of kilometers.
- **Abha:** normal urban offering and modest prices; small properties are starting to appear in the mountain villages, but the offering is still limited.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **Jeddah:** it's the city where you eat best in the country, because centuries of pilgrimage have brought cuisines here from all over the Muslim world. Kabsa, Yemeni mandi cooked in an underground oven, foul for breakfast, Red Sea fish on the Corniche.
- **AlUla:** limited and expensive offering. Look for the oasis dates, bought from the growers.
- **Riyadh:** Najdi cuisine is drier: kabsa, jareesh (cracked wheat cooked for a long time), mutabbaq. The old neighborhoods have excellent popular restaurants for very little; the new ones, a café scene that exploded after 2019.
- **Asir:** more grains and vegetables than in the rest of the country, because of mountain agriculture. Areeka and Asir honey.
- **Everywhere:** light Arabic coffee spiced with cardamom, served with dates. It's a ritual of hospitality more than a drink, and refusing it isn't done.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Book Hegra before booking flights.** It's limited, and the whole itinerary revolves around that date.
- **Book accommodation in AlUla well in advance.** There are few and in high season they run out.
- **Don't plan the itinerary in summer**, and remember the Asir has the inverted calendar.
- **Don't try to reach Mecca.** It's off-limits to non-Muslims and there are checkpoints.
- **Don't expect alcohol.** It doesn't exist in the country, not even in international hotels.
- **Don't drive alone to the Edge of the World**, and don't get too close to the edge.
- **Always ask before photographing people**, and accept a no.
- **Factor in the domestic flights.** With these distances doing everything overland is not practical.
- **Dress in covering clothes**, men included.
- **Check the official advisories** for the border areas with Yemen if you go down into the Asir.

## Chiusura personale
What stays from this trip is the feeling of having seen something in a window that is closing. Not in a nostalgic sense (there's nothing romantic about a country you couldn't visit until 2019), but in a literal sense: Hegra opened recently and already limits access, Al-Balad is halfway through its restoration and in ten years will be something else, and tourism projects are rising on the Red Sea coast that will change the geography. Right now you stand in front of a Nabataean facade carved two thousand years ago with nobody else around, and in a few years that will be a memory told to the newcomers. The country, meanwhile, is changing faster than any guidebook can keep up with, with all the contradictions that entails and that aren't worth hiding. Going today means seeing something in motion, not something finished.
