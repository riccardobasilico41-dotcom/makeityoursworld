# Berlin in Four Days

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 work in progress (trip taken by Rick; itinerary and practical information verified)
**Periodo del viaggio:** **May-September**
**Durata:** 4 days, 3-4 nights
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independent, by public transport
**Categoria:** Europa · Germania · Berlino · storia · memoria · vita notturna

---

## Apertura personale
Berlin can't be covered in three days like other European capitals, which is why the days become four here: it's a much more spread-out and less dense city than Paris or Kraków, where getting from one neighborhood to another almost always requires public transport, and where the twentieth century left more layers than in almost any other capital on the continent: capital of the Reich, a city split in two by the Wall for twenty-eight years, finally the symbol of German reunification.

That twentieth century can't be skirted, and it wouldn't be right to try: a whole day of this itinerary is dedicated to the places of remembrance, from the Wall to the Topography of Terror. Not just any tourist stops, but places that ask for time and attention.

The rest of the city, though, is anything but only memory: Museum Island with the archaeology of half the world, and the eastern neighborhoods like Kreuzberg and Friedrichshain, grown right on the rubble of the divided city to become among the liveliest in Europe. Four days are enough to hold all this together, without rushing either half.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** **May-September** for mild weather and long days, with **June-August** the hottest and most crowded period. **April and October** are still manageable, with fewer crowds. **November-March** is cold, often near or below freezing from December to February, but **December** brings the Christmas markets scattered over dozens of spots in the city.
- **Currency:** **euro**, as in Italy. Unlike Poland and Hungary, there's nothing to convert here.
- **Cash:** despite the image of a digitalized country, in Germany (Berlin included) **cash is still more common than elsewhere in Europe**: some bars, restaurants and small shops don't accept cards or accept them only above a minimum amount. Best to always carry some cash.
- **Sundays:** the **Ladenschlussgesetz**, the German opening-hours law, keeps **almost all shops closed on Sundays** throughout Germany (restaurants, bars and pharmacies stay open). Factor it into the plan, especially if you're thinking of shopping or running errands.
- **Getting around the city:** the **BVG** network of U-Bahn, S-Bahn, trams and buses, among the most efficient in Europe. At stations without turnstiles the ticket must be **validated before boarding**: plainclothes inspections are frequent and fines steep. Unlike more compact cities, here you **need public transport** even just to go from one neighborhood to another.
- **From the airport:** from **Berlin Brandenburg (BER)**, the S-Bahn and regional trains take you to the center in about **30 minutes**.
- **The Reichstag:** the climb to the dome is **free** but requires a **mandatory online reservation**, with slots that in high season sell out days or weeks ahead, plus a security check at the entrance.
- **The Pergamonmuseum:** the north wing, with the Pergamon Altar Hall, has been **closed since 2023 for renovation** and reopening is not expected soon. Other sections remain open, including the Ishtar Gate. It's worth checking the official website before including it in the plan.
- **Checkpoint Charlie:** the actors dressed as soldiers who offer to pose for a photo **ask for payment afterward**, even if they don't say so up front.
- **Food:** the **currywurst**, born in Berlin after the war; the **döner kebab**, which arrived with Turkish immigration and became the city's signature dish; markets like the **Markthalle Neun** in Kreuzberg for international street food.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Brandenburg Gate and the government quarter
We started from the most recognizable symbol of the city, the **Brandenburg Gate**: built in 1791 with the Quadriga on top, since 1989 it has been the very image of German reunification. From here, a few minutes on foot took us to the **Reichstag**, seat of the Bundestag, with Norman Foster's **glass dome** (1999). The climb, free but by reservation, gives a 360° view over the government quarter, and in the inner corridors you can still see the inscriptions left by Soviet soldiers in 1945.

A few steps away is the **Holocaust Memorial** (Denkmal für die ermordeten Juden Europas): 2,711 concrete stelae of different heights designed by Peter Eisenman, with an underground information center that reconstructs the stories of some of the victims of the Nazi extermination. It's a place meant to be walked through in silence, not photographed as a backdrop.

In the evening we were in Mitte, between Unter den Linden and Gendarmenmarkt, the square with the German and French Cathedrals and the Konzerthaus.

### Day 2 — The Berlin Wall and the Cold War
This is the day of the divided city, and it asks for time.

We started from the **Gedenkstätte Berliner Mauer**, the memorial along **Bernauer Straße**, the best-preserved and best-documented stretch of the border. Here you see a reconstruction of the "death strip," the band of ground between the two parallel walls patrolled by border guards, and there's the documentation center that tells the stories of those who tried to escape.

Midday we moved to **Checkpoint Charlie**, the former crossing point between the American and Soviet sectors. Today it's reconstructed with a symbolic post and is one of the most touristy places in the city, with paid actors for photos: the contrast with the sobriety of Bernauer Straße is sharp, and deliberate.

In the afternoon, the **Topography of Terror** (Topographie des Terrors), the documentation center built on the site of the former Gestapo and SS headquarters, with a stretch of the Wall still standing along the outer perimeter. The exhibition reconstructs without holding back the repressive apparatus of the Nazi regime, which had its operational base here.

After a day like that, the evening should be left empty.

### Day 3 — Museum Island and East Berlin
The morning and early afternoon were for **Museum Island** (Museumsinsel), a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1999: the **Pergamonmuseum** (it's worth checking beforehand which rooms are open, given the ongoing works), the **Neues Museum** with the bust of **Nefertiti** and the **Altes Museum** with its classical art collections.

In the afternoon we moved toward **Alexanderplatz** and the neighborhood around it, heart of former East Berlin: the **Fernsehturm** (TV Tower), visible from much of the city, and the **Nikolaiviertel**, the small quarter rebuilt around the oldest church in Berlin. If you like, the **DDR Museum**, dedicated to everyday life in East Germany, is a few steps away.

You eat dinner around Alexanderplatz or on the way back toward Mitte.

### Day 4 — Kreuzberg and Friedrichshain
On the last day we left the old town for the eastern neighborhoods, grown after reunification in the spaces left free by the fall of the Wall. In the morning we were at the **East Side Gallery**: 1.3 km of the Wall still standing along the Spree in Friedrichshain, painted in 1990 by more than a hundred artists. Among the murals is the "Fraternal Kiss" between Brezhnev and Honecker.

In the early afternoon, **Kreuzberg**: the Turkish market along the **Maybachufer**, the street art in the streets around Oranienstraße, the **Markthalle Neun** for a snack among the many international street food options.

In the evening there's time for a nod to the nightlife scene that made Berlin one of the world capitals of electronic music, born right in these industrial spaces left empty by the fall of the Wall (there's a dedicated experience entry), without it being an indispensable part of the weekend.

## What to see / what to do
- **The Brandenburg Gate** and the Reichstag dome.
- **The Holocaust Memorial**, walked through in silence.
- **The Gedenkstätte Berliner Mauer** along Bernauer Straße, the most direct way to understand the Wall.
- **The Topography of Terror**, on the site of the former Gestapo and SS headquarters.
- **Museum Island**, with the Pergamonmuseum and the Neues Museum.
- **The East Side Gallery**, along the Spree in Friedrichshain.
- **The Turkish market in Kreuzberg** along the Maybachufer.
- **A nod to the Berlin techno scene**, for those interested.

## Possible extensions
- **Potsdam (+1 day):** about 30-40 minutes by S-Bahn, for Sanssouci Palace and its gardens, the summer residence of the kings of Prussia, a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **Dresden (+1-2 days):** about 2 hours by train, for the baroque old town rebuilt after the 1945 bombings. It requires a real extension, not half a day from Berlin.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
**Mitte**, the old town around the Brandenburg Gate and Museum Island, is the most convenient area for the first part of the itinerary. **Kreuzberg** and **Friedrichshain**, farther east, are the more lived-in alternative and generally cheaper, with an evening life that the old town doesn't have, at the cost of a few extra minutes on the metro to reach the museums.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
**Döner kebab** and **currywurst** as the reference street food, found everywhere in the city. The **Markthalle Neun** in Kreuzberg for a more international round, especially during the weekly Street Food Thursday (the schedule changes: worth checking beforehand).

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Book the Reichstag online** days or weeks ahead in high season.
- **Check which Pergamonmuseum rooms are open** before building the plan around Museum Island.
- **Don't get photographed for free at Checkpoint Charlie**: the payment comes afterward.
- **Always carry some cash**: not all places accept cards.
- **Remember Sunday**: almost all shops are closed by law.
- **Allow for travel times**: Berlin is much more spread out than Paris or Kraków.
- **Always validate your BVG ticket** before boarding, where there are no turnstiles.

## Chiusura personale
What stays with me is the way this city holds together things that elsewhere stay separate: a silent memorial a couple of steps from a Turkish market full of life, a stretch of Wall turned into an open-air art gallery a few metro stops from a documentation center on the Gestapo. Berlin doesn't ask you to choose between history and the present: it puts them on the same map, and that's why four days here are not a day too many.
