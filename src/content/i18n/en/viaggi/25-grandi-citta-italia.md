# Italy's Great Cities in Two Weeks: Rome, Florence, Venice, Naples

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 in progress (itinerary, entry quotas and practical information verified and up to date; the first-person passages are written as a draft to be reread and personalized)
**Periodo del viaggio:** **October to March**, which is when Italy's art cities become livable again
**Durata:** 12 days, 11 nights
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independent, getting around only by high-speed train
**Categoria:** Europa · Italia · città d'arte · archeologia · gastronomia

---

## Apertura personale
Italy's four great art cities are almost always visited the wrong way, and not out of ignorance: because of the season and the time of day. People go in April or July, stay in the center between ten and five, line up at the three famous monuments and eat within three hundred meters of them. The result is a collection of lines and an ungenerous verdict. The same four cities, from November to March, in the early mornings and evenings, are a different trip: the museums can actually be visited, the trattorie are full of locals, and in Venice at seven in the morning there's nobody around. This itinerary is built on the high-speed train, which connects all four in a few hours, and on a simple principle: every city has one capped-entry thing to book weeks ahead and one free thing that almost nobody does.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** **October to March**. April-June and September are beautiful and crowded; **July and August are to be avoided**, with temperatures in Rome topping 38 degrees (100°F) and heat waves that make visiting tiring and for some risky. November, January and February are the months when you walk into museums without lines.
- **Mandatory reservations (the part that decides the trip):**
  - **Colosseum:** **named** ticket since 2023, with the **original ID document** at the entrance (not a copy). From 2026 changing the name on a ticket is even more restricted. Buy only through the official channel, weeks ahead for the best time slots.
  - **Borghese Gallery:** entry capped in two-hour slots, which sell out.
  - **Uffizi and Accademia:** reservation effectively mandatory; without one, lines run for hours.
  - **Dome of the Florence Duomo:** **reservation with an assigned time slot**, without it you can't climb.
  - **Leonardo's Last Supper in Milan:** reservation mandatory, **openings released quarterly**, a fifteen-minute visit. It's the most common mistake of people visiting Milan: without a reservation you don't get in.
  - **Pompeii:** **named** ticket and a **cap of 20,000 visitors a day**, with time slots in summer (maximum 15,000 from 9 to 12, 5,000 from 12 on). Once the quota is used up **you cannot enter**, neither online nor at the ticket office.
- **Venice, access fee:** it applies to those who enter **as day-trippers without staying overnight**, on a certain number of high-season days. In 2026 it was charged on 60 days between April and July: **5 euros** if booking at least four days ahead, **10 euros** below that threshold. Those who stay overnight in the city are exempt but must register. Calendar and conditions change every year.
- **Transport:** the high-speed train connects everything and, booking weeks ahead, prices drop sharply. Rome-Florence 1h30, Florence-Venice 2h, Venice-Naples with a change, Rome-Naples a little over an hour. **In all four cities a car is useless or harmful**: ZTL in Florence and Rome, Area B and C in Milan, and in Venice there are simply no roads.
- **The food rule:** in all four, **don't eat within three hundred meters of a famous monument** and never go into a place with photos of the dishes outside or with someone urging you to come in. It's the rule that, more than any other, separates eating well from eating badly in Italy.
- **Free museums:** on the first Sunday of the month state museums are free, and therefore have enormous lines. Churches, on the other hand, are almost all free and contain a huge part of what you come to see.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Arrival in Rome
To sleep, **Monti or Trastevere** is best, to be in a neighborhood that's still alive. In the evening it's better to avoid the area right around Termini.

We spent the first evening getting our bearings on foot: in central Rome the distances are shorter than they seem and almost everything is within a two-kilometer radius.

We had dinner at a trattoria in **Testaccio**, not in the center. The four Roman pastas (cacio e pepe, gricia, amatriciana, carbonara) and, for those up to it, the quinto quarto (offal).

### Day 2 — Ancient Rome
We went into the **Colosseum, Roman Forum and Palatine** with the single named ticket, booked weeks earlier, and with the **original ID in our pocket**.

For the afternoon there's **Trajan's Forum** with the Markets, or the **Domus Aurea** for those who managed to book it: you visit with a hard hat inside a permanent restoration site, and that's exactly what makes it impressive.

### Day 3 — The Vatican and the churches
In the morning, the **Vatican Museums and Sistine Chapel**, with a reservation. Then St. Peter's and the dome: at the entrance the **dress code is enforced**, no bare shoulders or knees.

We spent the afternoon in the **churches**, the part of Rome nobody puts on the schedule and which is free: the three Caravaggios in San Luigi dei Francesi, the ones in Santa Maria del Popolo, the Pantheon. You put a euro in the coin box for the light and stand ten minutes in front of the Calling of St. Matthew with four people around.

### Day 4 — Underground Rome and the Appian Way
In the morning we went to the **Basilica of San Clemente**. You enter a twelfth-century basilica, descend to a fourth-century one and from there further down to a first-century Roman building with a Mithraeum. Three eras one on top of the other, two flights of stairs, four hundred meters from the Colosseum and with a fraction of the people.

In the afternoon we were on the **Appian Way**, best on a Sunday, when it's closed to traffic. You walk on a road opened in 312 BC, with the original paving stones, the tombs on either side and the pine trees. Continuing, you reach the Villa of the Quintilii and the **Parco degli Acquedotti**, where six Roman aqueducts cross a meadow with the arches still standing.

### Day 5 — High-speed train to Florence
**Rome-Florence in 1h30**, with Santa Maria Novella station a fifteen-minute walk from the Duomo.

We settled in the **Oltrarno**, to be in a neighborhood that's still alive, five minutes from the center.

We spent the afternoon among the Oltrarno's artisan workshops (restoration, gilding, leatherwork), the part of the city that's disappearing fastest.

At sunset we climbed to **San Miniato al Monte**, not to Piazzale Michelangelo. It's a hundred meters higher, has the same view, a Romanesque church from the year 1000 with a marble-inlaid facade and a tenth of the people. In the afternoon the monks sing in Gregorian chant in the crypt.

### Day 6 — The dome and the Uffizi
We took the **first morning slot for Brunelleschi's dome**, booked days in advance. It's 463 steps, but above all it's a route that passes **inside** the dome, in the cavity between the two shells, a few meters from the frescoes of the Last Judgment.

The afternoon at the **Uffizi**, with a reservation.

*Watch the closing days: the Duomo complex and Florence's state museums close on different days, and fitting them together badly is the most common planning mistake in this city.*

### Day 7 — The museum nobody visits
In the morning we went to the **Museo dell'Opera del Duomo**, which almost everyone skips because "it's just an annex museum." It holds the **original doors of the Baptistery** (the ones in the square are copies) and Michelangelo's last Pietà, the one he wanted to destroy. There are four people.

For the afternoon there are the **Brancacci Chapel** or the **Medici Chapels**, less crowded and just as important.

For dinner, a trattoria in the Oltrarno or Sant'Ambrogio, with the bistecca: it's a specific cut, three fingers thick, rare, served by the kilo, and you don't order it well done.

### Day 8 — High-speed train to Venice
**Florence-Venice in 2h**, with Santa Lucia station **inside** the city.

To sleep, **Cannaregio or Castello** is best, areas that are still lived in. **Sleeping in the center exempts you from the access fee** and above all gives you access to the two windows in which Venice is what you expected: before nine and after seven.

The first evening we did the round of the **bacari**: cicheti eaten standing at the counter with a little glass of wine, moving from one osteria to another. It costs little and you eat very well, as long as you get off the main routes.

### Day 9 — Venice at dawn and the Scuola di San Rocco
**Get up at dawn**: it's the advice that changes your opinion of this city the most. At seven the calli are empty, you hear the water slapping against the walls, the delivery boats pass, and the **Rialto fish market** has been working since five (closed Sunday and Monday).

Then **St. Mark's and the Doge's Palace**, and in the afternoon the **Scuola Grande di San Rocco**, with Tintoretto's cycle: it's the Sistine Chapel of Venice and there are ten people.

At sunset we climbed the **bell tower of San Giorgio Maggiore**, which has the best view of St. Mark's and no line.

### Day 10 — The lagoon
This is the day almost nobody does. We went to **Torcello**, forty minutes by vaporetto, with the seventh-century basilica and the mosaic Last Judgment that covers an entire wall. Venice began there, before Venice existed, and there are twenty people.

Then **Burano** for the colored houses and **Murano** for the glass, knowing they are far more heavily trodden.

*The timed vaporetto pass pays for itself on a day like this.*

### Day 11 — High-speed train to Naples
We moved to **Naples**; to sleep, the historic center or Chiaia.

In the afternoon we went to the **National Archaeological Museum**, which should be seen **before** Pompeii and not after: in the nineteenth century almost all the best mosaics, frescoes and bronzes from the Vesuvian sites were brought there, and without that stop Pompeii looks like a field of walls.

In the evening, **pizza** where it was born, in a neighborhood pizzeria, where it costs as much as a sandwich and you wait in line in the street with a number.

### Day 12 — Pompeii and Herculaneum, and the trip home
We entered **Pompeii at opening time**, with the named ticket already booked: sixty-six hectares without shade, which in the middle hours of summer are tiring to the point of being risky.

If time allows, in the afternoon there's **Herculaneum**. It's a tenth of the area but, buried by mud rather than ash, it preserves the upper floors, the charred beams and even the furniture. You walk inside two-story houses with the stairs still in place.

Then the trip home from Naples.

## What to see / what to do
- **Go from October to March.** It's the choice that changes everything else the most.
- **Book the Colosseum, Uffizi, dome, Borghese and Pompeii weeks ahead**, and bring the original ID where the ticket is named.
- **San Clemente in Rome**, three overlapping eras four hundred meters from the Colosseum.
- **The Appian Way on a Sunday.**
- **The churches of Rome**, which are free and contain the Caravaggios.
- **The Museo dell'Opera del Duomo in Florence**, which almost everyone skips.
- **San Miniato al Monte** instead of Piazzale Michelangelo.
- **Venice at seven in the morning**, the one serious reason to sleep in the center.
- **Torcello**, where Venice began.
- **The Archaeological Museum before Pompeii**, and **Herculaneum after Pompeii**.

## Possible extensions
- **Milan (+2 days):** the Duomo terraces, which almost everyone skips to see only the interior, and the **Last Supper**, bookable only when quarterly openings are released, months ahead. Plus the Alpine lakes an hour away.
- **Amalfi Coast and Capri (+3 days from Naples):** by ferry rather than car, because in summer the Amalfi coast road is one continuous traffic jam.
- **Wine routes (+4-6 days):** Langhe, Valpolicella or Chianti fit well with this itinerary. There's a dedicated itinerary on the site.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **Rome:** Monti or Trastevere for the lively neighborhood, Prati for the prices and the metro to the Vatican, Testaccio for eating well.
- **Florence:** Oltrarno or Sant'Ambrogio. The quadrilateral between the Duomo and the Signoria is very convenient, expensive, and empty of residents in the evening.
- **Venice:** Cannaregio or Castello. **Sleeping in the center is the choice that counts**: it exempts you from the access fee and gives you the two windows in which the city is what you expected.
- **Naples:** the historic center to be inside the city, Chiaia for a quieter area, Vomero for the view.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **Rome:** the four pastas and the quinto quarto (coda alla vaccinara, tripe, coratella) in a trattoria in Testaccio or Pigneto. Carciofo alla giudia in the Ghetto in season, supplì as a snack.
- **Florence:** **lampredotto** sandwiches at the stands, the most Florentine thing there is. Ribollita, pappa al pomodoro, chicken liver crostini and the bistecca. Sant'Ambrogio market, not the San Lorenzo one.
- **Venice:** the **round of bacari** with cicheti at the counter. Sarde in saor, baccalà mantecato, moeche in season. Away from the San Marco-Rialto axes, where the value for money is the worst in Italy.
- **Naples:** pizza, ragù that cooks for hours, genovese, hot sfogliatella riccia in the morning and coffee standing up. And the street pizza a portafoglio, two euros.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Don't go between July and August.**
- **Don't show up without a reservation** at the Colosseum, Uffizi, dome, Borghese, Last Supper and Pompeii: in all these cases you don't get in.
- **Bring the original ID** where the ticket is named: the Colosseum and Pompeii check it, and a copy isn't enough.
- **Don't buy tickets from street resellers.**
- **Don't eat within three hundred meters of a monument.**
- **Don't judge Venice between ten and five.**
- **Don't do the Vatican and the Colosseum on the same day.**
- **Don't drive into the centers of Florence, Rome and Milan**: ZTL, Area B and Area C, with fines that arrive months later.
- **Check the closing days**, different for every museum: they are the most frequent planning mistake.

## Chiusura personale
What stays with me, after twelve days in four cities I thought I knew, is that the value is almost always where there's no line. Four hundred meters from the Colosseum you descend into a fourth-century basilica and then into a first-century temple of Mithras, and there are six people. A hundred meters from Piazzale Michelangelo there's a church from the year 1000 where in the afternoon the monks sing in Gregorian chant, and there's nobody. Forty minutes by vaporetto from St. Mark's there's the basilica where Venice began, with a mosaic Last Judgment as big as a wall, and there are twenty people. It's not a search for the exclusive: it's that this country has an amount of stuff so out of proportion to the tourist flows that you only need to move three hundred meters, or an hour, to find yourself almost alone in front of something that elsewhere would be the national monument.
