# Prague in a Long Weekend

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 in progress (trip taken by Rick; itinerary and practical information verified)
**Periodo del viaggio:** **April-May or September-October**
**Durata:** 3-4 days, 3 nights
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independently, all on foot and by public transport
**Categoria:** Europa · Repubblica Ceca · Praga · storia · architettura · gastronomia

---

## Apertura personale
More than any other capital in Central Europe, Prague came through the twentieth century without losing its medieval and Baroque skin: while Warsaw was razed to the ground and Dresden burned under the bombs, Prague's historic center has reached today almost intact, earning it the nickname "city of a hundred spires." It walks like a fourteenth-century city because it still truly is one, not a reconstruction, unlike so many other European historic centers rebuilt after 1945.

Three or four days are enough to do the essential Prague well: the Old Town with the Astronomical Clock, Charles Bridge leading to Malá Strana and the Castle, the Jewish quarter of Josefov, and Vyšehrad as a quiet counterpoint for those who have already seen the bulk of the center's crowds. It's also a city where it's worth arriving prepared on a couple of very practical things (the currency is not the euro, and around the main square there are rather well-established schemes of inflated bills) more than on an endless list of monuments to tick off.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** **April-May and September-October**, with mild weather, fewer crowds and shorter lines. **June-August** is high season: heat and lots of people, especially on Charles Bridge and in Old Town Square. **November-March** is cold, often below freezing with snow possible, but the city holds up well in winter too, and in December Old Town Square and Wenceslas Square host two of the best-known Christmas markets in Central Europe.
- **Currency:** the Czech Republic is in the European Union but **not in the eurozone**: you pay in Czech koruna (CZK), not in euros, and it's the most common surprise for those who arrive assuming the opposite. Cards and contactless work almost everywhere. Better not to pay in euros at venues that offer it, because the "converted" rate is almost always unfavorable.
- **Watch out for restaurants around Old Town Square and on Karlova street:** it's the area with the most reports of inflated bills. Menus without prices, unrequested but charged "welcome drinks," bread and appetizers left on the table and put on the check as if they'd been ordered. It's worth always checking the menu and prices before ordering, and asking for confirmation if something arrives that you didn't ask for.
- **Watch out for pickpockets on tram 22:** it's the line that goes up toward the Castle and it's the most reported for theft by distraction in pairs, especially in the crowded months. On the metro and trams, at rush hour, the usual caution applies.
- **Fake police officers:** a minor but documented risk, especially in the most touristy areas. They're people who introduce themselves as police and ask for your documents or the immediate payment of a "fine" in cash. A real officer on the street never does this: if in doubt, ask to go to the nearest police station.
- **Getting around the city:** the historic center is all on foot. The PID network's metro, trams and buses are extensive and punctual (timed tickets, a few CZK), with mandatory validation on board. You don't need a car.
- **From the airport:** from **Václav Havel Airport Prague (PRG)** there's no direct metro connection: you take a regular bus (119 or 191) to the Nádraží Veleslavín metro station (line A), in **15-20 minutes**.
- **Prague Castle:** the outer courtyards and the nave of St. Vitus Cathedral are **free**; for the interiors (Cathedral, Old Royal Palace, St. George's Basilica, Golden Lane) you need the Circuit A ticket, around **450 CZK**.
- **Josefov, the Jewish quarter:** the Jewish Town Ticket (about **600 CZK**) covers six historic sites, that is five synagogues and the Old Jewish Cemetery; for the Old-New Synagogue, the oldest still-active one in Europe, a separate ticket is needed.
- **Food:** **svíčková** (beef roast in a cream sauce with knedlíky, sliced bread dumplings), **guláš**, **trdelník** (the rolled street sweet, by now more a tourist icon than a traditional dish). **Hospodas**, the traditional pubs, remain the most genuine and cheapest way to eat, beer included.
- **Beer:** the Czech Republic has the highest per capita consumption in the world, and in non-touristy venues beer usually costs less than bottled water. A half-liter on tap in a neighborhood pub costs roughly **55-80 CZK**, versus 100-150 CZK (or more) in venues on the main square.
- **Hours:** some Josefov sites close on Saturdays (Shabbat) and on Jewish holidays: check before building the day devoted to the quarter.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Staré Město and Old Town Square
We started from the heart of the city: **Staroměstské náměstí**, Old Town Square, dominated by the town hall's **Astronomical Clock Tower**. The clock was built starting in 1410: it's the third-oldest astronomical clock in the world and the only one of that era still working. Every hour, from 9 to 23, the mechanical figures of the twelve Apostles appear while the skeleton of Death pulls the bell rope. It's worth being in the square on the hour.

On the opposite side of the square rises the Gothic **Church of Our Lady before Týn**, with the two dark spires that have become the symbol of the Prague skyline, and not far away is the Baroque **Church of St. Nicholas** (not to be confused with the one of the same name in Malá Strana). Then we got lost in the streets of Staré Město, between the Powder Tower and the Art Nouveau Municipal House.

In the evening we stayed in the center, but carefully: the restaurants on the square and on Karlova street are the area with the most reports of inflated bills. For dinner it's best to walk a few blocks away.

### Day 2 — Charles Bridge, Malá Strana and Prague Castle
We crossed **Charles Bridge** early in the morning, before 8, to avoid the bulk of the crowd. It's 516 Gothic meters, built starting in 1357 on the commission of Charles IV, with more than seventy Baroque statues (all copies: the originals are kept under cover in museums). The oldest and most venerated is that of **St. John of Nepomuk**, from 1683.

On the other side we entered **Malá Strana**, the Baroque district at the foot of the Castle: the Church of St. Nicholas with its green dome, the **John Lennon Wall**, covered in graffiti for decades, and **Kampa Island** on the Vltava, with its glimpses of the river.

In the afternoon we climbed the hill of **Prague Castle**, one of the largest enclosed castle complexes in the world: the Gothic **St. Vitus Cathedral**, in the middle of the courtyard; the Old Royal Palace; St. George's Basilica; **Golden Lane**, the row of colorful little houses where craftsmen and alchemists once lived. The courtyards and the Cathedral nave are free; for the interiors you need the Circuit A ticket.

In the evening you go back to Malá Strana, or head back at an easy pace toward Staré Město.

### Day 3 — Josefov, the Jewish Quarter
We devoted the whole morning to **Josefov**, the former Jewish quarter in the heart of Staré Město. With the Jewish Town Ticket you visit six sites: the **Old Jewish Cemetery**, with thousands of tombstones stacked in layers on ground that has stayed the same for centuries, and the **Pinkas** synagogue (today a memorial with the names of the Czech victims of the Holocaust), the **Maisel**, **Klausen** and **Spanish** synagogues, the latter with one of the most surprising Moorish interiors in the city. For the **Old-New Synagogue**, the oldest active synagogue in Europe, a separate ticket is needed.

The afternoon is freer: you can go back to something glimpsed in passing in the first two days or take a stroll through the nineteenth-century **Nové Město** (New Town), whose main axis is **Wenceslas Square**.

In the evening it's worth going to a hospoda outside the most touristy center (Žižkov and Vinohrady are the most lived-in areas) for a round of Czech beers at the city's real prices.

### Day 4 — Vyšehrad and a last stroll
In the morning we went to **Vyšehrad**, the fortress on the hill south of the center, less visited than the Castle but with over a thousand years of history: according to the founding legend of Prague, it was here that Princess **Libuše** prophesied the birth of the city. From the walls you get some of the widest views over Prague and the Vltava, with far fewer people than at the Castle: a quiet counterpoint after three days of historic center.

In the early afternoon we went back to the center for the last stroll: whatever had been left out on the previous days, a last coffee in Old Town Square, the last shopping.

In the evening we had the last dinner of the weekend in the center.

## What to see / what to do
- **Old Town Square and the Astronomical Clock**, on the hour.
- **Charles Bridge**, early in the morning to avoid the crowd.
- **Prague Castle and St. Vitus Cathedral**, the largest enclosed castle complex in the world.
- **Malá Strana**, between the John Lennon Wall and Kampa Island.
- **Josefov**, with the historic synagogues and the Old Jewish Cemetery.
- **Vyšehrad**, the quiet counterpoint, with the legend of Libuše.
- **An evening in a hospoda** outside the most touristy center, for beer at real prices.

## Possible extensions
- **Kutná Hora (+1 day, or half a day):** about an hour by train, for the Sedlec Ossuary, decorated entirely with human bones, and the Cathedral of St. Barbara, both UNESCO World Heritage sites. It's the classic day trip from Prague.
- **Karlovy Vary:** the historic spa town of western Bohemia, about 2 hours by bus, for those with more time who are looking for a slower pace.
- **Vienna:** with a direct train of about 4 hours, it's the natural combination with this weekend for those with more days available. There's a dedicated itinerary on the site.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
**Staré Město** is the most convenient choice, you can do everything on foot, but it's also the most expensive area. **Malá Strana** is just as central and quieter, at the foot of the Castle. **Vinohrady** and **Žižkov**, just outside the historic core but well connected by tram, are the more lived-in and cheaper alternatives.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
Neighborhood **hospodas**, outside the most touristy center, for svíčková and guláš at real prices, beer included. In the center it's worth walking a few blocks away from Old Town Square and Karlova street, where inflated bills are the norm more than the exception.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Always check the menu and prices** before ordering at restaurants around Old Town Square and on Karlova street.
- **Don't follow anyone on the street who introduces themselves as a police officer** and asks for money: a real officer doesn't do that.
- **Be careful on tram 22**, the line most reported for pickpocketing toward the Castle.
- **Pay in koruna** and don't expect euros just because you're in the European Union.
- **Check the Josefov hours**: some sites close on Saturdays and Jewish holidays.
- **Get to Charles Bridge early**, before 8, to avoid the bulk of the crowd.
- **Dress in layers**, especially in winter: the cold in Prague is biting, but with the right clothing it's manageable.

## Chiusura personale
What stays with me is the contrast between the two faces of the city: the medieval square with the clock marking every hour in front of thousands of people and, a few minutes away by tram, the silent walls of Vyšehrad, where the same city tells its story with far fewer people around. Prague is both things at once, and it's the reason a weekend here is worth the reputation it carries.
