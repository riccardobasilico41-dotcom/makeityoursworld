# Budapest in a Long Weekend

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 in progress (trip made by Rick; itinerary and practical information verified)
**Periodo del viaggio:**
**Durata:** 3-4 days, 3 nights
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independently, all on foot and by public transport
**Categoria:** Europa · Ungheria · Budapest · terme · vita notturna · gastronomia

## Apertura personale
Budapest was not always a single city: until 1873 Buda, Óbuda and Pest were three separate cities on the two banks of the Danube, and you can still feel it today. On one side Buda, hilly, with the royal castle and the medieval district around Matthias Church; on the other Pest, flat, with the great 19th-century boulevards, the Parliament and the Jewish Quarter. In between, the river that holds them together and that in the evening gives back the most photographed view in Central Europe.

There is also something you do in Budapest that no other European capital does with the same ease: stepping into an open-air thermal pool, inside a historic building, in the middle of the city. Budapest sits on more than a hundred thermal springs and is officially known as the city of spas: an activity to put on the calendar the way you would a museum, not to dismiss as a wellness break.

Three or four days are enough to do everything well: the Buda side, the Pest side, a day at the baths and, if your body holds up, an evening among the ruin bars of the Jewish Quarter, the most original nightlife phenomenon to be born in a European capital in the last twenty years.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** **April-May and September-October**, mild weather and fewer crowds. **June-August** is high season: hot and crowded, especially around the Fisherman's Bastion and the Széchenyi Baths. **November-March** is cold, often below zero, but the outdoor pools of the baths steaming in the snow are perhaps the best time to experience them, and in December the Christmas markets in Vörösmarty Square and in front of Saint Stephen's Basilica are among the best known in Central Europe.
- **Currency:** Hungary is in the European Union but **not in the eurozone**: you pay in forints (HUF), not in euros. The numbers are big (a coffee 800-1,200 HUF, a dinner 4,000-8,000 HUF per person), and confusing "a thousand" with "ten thousand" is the most common mistake of the first days. It's best to get used right away to counting the zeros before paying.
- **Beware of currency exchanges:** around Váci utca and in the more touristy areas some exchange offices display showy rates that only apply to very large amounts, or hide a high commission in small print. Better an exchange office with a full and transparent rate board, or withdraw HUF directly from a bank ATM.
- **The baths, how they work:** day ticket roughly **€20-30**, with different options (entry only, with locker, with private cabin). **Towel and slippers are almost never included**: you rent them on site for a fee or, spending less, bring them from home. Between changing room, queues and time in the pools, you need at least **two and a half to three hours** to enjoy them.
- **Beware of venues "recommended on the street":** in the Jewish Quarter (Erzsébetváros) and in the more touristy areas the same scheme documented in other Central European capitals like Krakow and Prague is at work. Street promoters, often girls, invite you to go into "a fantastic place"; then wildly inflated bills arrive for a few drinks, with bouncers discouraging you from leaving until you pay. Anyone who approaches you on the street suggesting a venue is never to be followed, period.
- **Getting around the city:** the BKK network is very efficient. The M1 metro line, opened in 1896, is the first in continental Europe (now a UNESCO World Heritage site); trams, buses and three other metro lines complete the network. The ticket must be validated before boarding: plainclothes inspectors issue heavy fines to those traveling without a valid ticket. You don't need a car.
- **From the airport to the centre:** from **Liszt Ferenc (BUD)** the **100E** bus reaches the centre (Kálvin tér, Astoria, Deák Ferenc tér) in 35-40 minutes: 2,500 HUF since 1 October 2025, paid by card or phone right on board or in the BudapestGO app (ordinary BKK tickets are not valid) · **200E** bus to the M3 metro at Kőbánya-Kispest, then the metro: 50-60 minutes, with two single tickets of about 500 HUF, both to be validated · **official Főtaxi taxi**, the only one with a rank outside terminals 2A and 2B: regulated meter, starting at 2,100 HUF from the airport (since 1 August 2026), about 12,000-15,000 HUF to central Pest · **Bolt** from the zone shown in the app, at similar prices · never accept rides from people offering them in arrivals.
- **The Parliament:** the interior guided tour, which includes the Holy Crown of Saint Stephen, has limited entries and in high season is often **sold out days ahead**: better to book online before leaving.
- **Food:** **goulash (gulyás)**, here more soup than stew; **lángos**, a disc of fried dough with sour cream and cheese, the street food par excellence; **paprikás csirke** (chicken paprikash); among the desserts, **rétes** (strudel) and **Dobos** torte. The **Central Market Hall** (Nagycsarnok, 1897) is the best place to taste local products at lower prices than the restaurants in the center.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Buda Hill and the Fisherman's Bastion
We started from the hilly side of the city: **Buda Castle**, for centuries the seat of Hungarian royal power and now of museums, with the view over the Danube and Pest from the panoramic terrace. A few steps further north we climbed to the **Fisherman's Bastion** (Halászbástya), built in 1902 as a viewpoint and not as a fortification. The seven white turrets represent the seven Magyar tribes that founded Hungary, and it is one of the most photographed views in the city, especially at sunset.

Next to it is **Matthias Church** (Mátyás-templom), with its polychrome glazed tile roof: royal coronations took place here, and during the Ottoman occupation of the 16th and 17th centuries it was turned into a mosque.

In the afternoon we went down toward the **Chain Bridge** (Széchenyi lánchíd), the first permanent bridge between Buda and Pest, opened in 1849. We crossed it on foot, with the Parliament on one side and the castle on the other: it's the easiest way to get over to the Pest bank.

In the evening we walked around the center of Pest, to get the measure of the city before the following days.

**In practice:** from central Pest to the castle by bus 16 or by the Budavári Sikló funicular from Clark Ádám Square, which often has a queue in summer · the upper terrace of the Fisherman's Bastion and Matthias Church have separate tickets costing a few thousand forints · a lot of uphill walking on cobbles: comfortable shoes.

### Day 2 — The Parliament and the Jewish Quarter
We devoted the morning to the **Hungarian Parliament** (Országház), completed in 1904, among the largest parliament buildings in the world. The guided tour (to be booked in advance) takes you into the main halls and up to the Holy Crown of Saint Stephen, kept under the central dome.

In the early afternoon we went to the **Great Synagogue on Dohány Street**, in Moorish style, the largest in Europe and the second in the world after the one in New York. In the memorial garden is Imre Varga's **Tree of Life**, a metal sculpture in the shape of a weeping willow dedicated to the Hungarian victims of the Shoah: one of the most sober and important places in the city.

In the late afternoon we entered the **Jewish Quarter** (Erzsébetváros), now the center of the ruin bars, for a first exploration on foot through the streets, before going back in the evening for the tour of the venues (see the dedicated experience page).

**In practice:** Parliament about 7,000 forints for EU citizens, tour of about 45 minutes at fixed times and languages, tickets to book online several days ahead · ID required at the entrance · the Great Synagogue closes on Saturdays and Jewish holidays, shoulders covered to enter.

### Day 3 — The Baths: Széchenyi or Gellért
We kept a day apart for the most characteristic experience of Budapest: the **baths**. The **Széchenyi Baths**, in the City Park, are the largest complex in Europe, with three outdoor pools that are hot all year round; in winter the steam rising from the water among the snow is probably the most famous image of the city. The **Gellért Baths**, at the foot of the hill of the same name on the Buda side, are the Art Nouveau alternative, more intimate and elegant, with mosaics and colored stained glass around the main pool.

Between changing room, locker queues and time in the pools, it's best to allow **at least two and a half to three hours**: it's not a one-hour stop between two museums.

In the afternoon, those who chose Széchenyi have the **City Park** (Városliget) around them, with Vajdahunyad Castle and Heroes' Square; those who chose Gellért can climb **Gellért Hill** and the Citadel, for another panorama over the city and the Danube.

*Note: the Gellért Baths closed on 1 October 2025 for a renovation lasting several years, and reopening is not expected before 2028; on the Buda side the nearest alternative today is the Rudas Baths.*

**In practice:** Széchenyi by the yellow metro line M1 to Széchenyi fürdő · day ticket with locker around 30-40 euros depending on the day, cheaper online and on weekdays · bring (or rent) swimsuit, flip-flops and towel · early morning or late afternoon are the least crowded times.

### Day 4 — Danube, Margaret Island and the Last Round
In the morning we went to **Margaret Island** (Margitsziget), the car-free park in the middle of the Danube, with the musical fountain and its smaller baths: a slower pace after three days of city. You enter from the south end, from **Margaret Bridge**, and can cross the island on foot or by rented bike, past the Japanese garden, the water tower and the ruins of the Dominican convent where Saint Margaret lived. *The musical fountain shows run in the warm season: in winter the island is still pleasant, but barer.*

In the early afternoon we headed back toward the center for the last round: the climb to the **Dome of Saint Stephen's Basilica**, with the panorama over the rooftops of Pest, and a stop at the **Central Market Hall** for last purchases among spices, paprika and souvenirs. The upper floor of the market has stalls selling lángos and Hungarian dishes, handy for a late lunch. In the evening, the easiest way to end the weekend is a **cruise or a walk along the Danube**, to see the Parliament and Buda Castle lit up from the water: the lights come on at sunset, the best time to board.

**In practice:** Margaret Island by trams 4 and 6 to Margaret Bridge, or by bus 26, which crosses it · the Basilica dome is reached on foot or by lift, separate ticket of a few thousand forints, on Sundays the church opens only in the afternoon · Central Market Hall closed on Sundays and open only until early afternoon on Saturdays · evening cruises last about an hour, best booked in summer.

## What to see / what to do
- **The Fisherman's Bastion** and Matthias Church, on Buda Hill.
- **The Hungarian Parliament**, with the interior guided tour or from the opposite bank of the Danube.
- **The Great Synagogue on Dohány Street** and the Tree of Life in the memorial garden.
- **The Széchenyi Baths** (or Rudas, with Gellért closed until 2028), devoting at least two and a half hours.
- **The Chain Bridge**, crossed on foot at sunset.
- **The ruin bars of the Jewish Quarter**, starting with Szimpla Kert.
- **Margaret Island**, for a slower afternoon.
- **An evening cruise on the Danube**, to see the monuments lit up from the water.

## Possible extensions
- **Szentendre (+half a day):** an artsy town on the Danube, about 40 minutes from Budapest by suburban train (HÉV), with colorful Baroque houses and art galleries; it is the most classic day trip.
- **Lake Balaton (+1-2 days):** the largest lake in Central Europe, about an hour and a half by train, the summer beach destination of Hungarians. It requires a real extension: it is not a half day from Budapest.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
The **Pest side**, between the Parliament, Saint Stephen's Basilica and the Jewish Quarter, is the most convenient area and the liveliest in the evening, a few minutes' walk from most points of interest. The **Buda side** is quieter and more scenic, but less practical for those who want to walk back from an evening in the Jewish Quarter.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
The **Central Market Hall** for the first tastes at honest prices, lángos and local products included. The **goulash** and the **paprikás csirke** in the traditional restaurants of the center, being careful with menus without clear prices, more frequent in the tourist streets around Váci utca.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Don't follow anyone who approaches you on the street suggesting a bar**: it's the classic scheme of inflated bills, especially in the Jewish Quarter.
- **Learn to read the zeros of the forint** before paying any bill.
- **Book the Parliament tour online** days ahead in high season.
- **Bring a towel and slippers from home** for the baths, or allow for the rental cost.
- **Check the rates carefully at the exchange offices** near Váci utca before exchanging.
- **Devote at least half a day to the baths**, not an hour between two stops.
- **Always validate your public transport ticket**: inspections are frequent.

## Chiusura personale
The one piece of advice I'd give about Budapest: give the baths at least half a day, not an hour squeezed between two stops. And in the Jewish Quarter, don't follow anyone who approaches you in the street suggesting a bar.
