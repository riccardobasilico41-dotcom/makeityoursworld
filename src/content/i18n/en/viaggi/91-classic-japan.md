# Classic Japan in 17 Days: Tokyo, Hakone, the Japanese Alps, Kyoto, Kōya-san and Miyajima

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 in progress (places Rick visited, put together into one practical route; practical information checked in October-November 2026, to recheck before departure; first-person memories only those given by Rick, none invented)
**Periodo del viaggio:**
**Durata:** 17 days, 16 nights; 14- and 21-day variants at the end
**Compagni di viaggio:** independently, by train with a 14-day JR Pass plus local buses (Nakasendō, Kamikochi, Shirakawa-go) and the private Nankai line for Kōya-san
**Categoria:** Asia · Giappone · cultura · Alpi giapponesi · villaggi storici · onsen · on the road

## Apertura personale
The classic trip to Japan runs almost entirely along the Pacific coast: Tokyo, Fuji, Kyoto, Osaka, Hiroshima. This itinerary keeps that backbone and adds the interior, the part many people skip on a first trip. The Japanese Alps, the old Nakasendō road between Magome and Tsumago, Takayama with its morning markets, the thatched-roof houses of Shirakawa-go, Kanazawa and the sacred mountain of Kōya-san.

The site already has another trip to Japan, [the 12 April days among sakura, Fuji and Kyoto](/viaggi/giappone-itinerario). This one is something else. It has more days, fewer big cities and more nights in ryokan, shukubō and mountain villages. Of Tokyo, Kyoto and Osaka it keeps the minimum: if you want the detail of those days, you'll find it there.

A spoiler from my own trip: the classic route is packed with tourists everywhere, Kyoto above all.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** late April to early June and October to mid-November. Kamikochi opens in mid-April and closes in mid-November (in 2026 from April 17 to November 15). June-July is the rainy season over most of Honshu, August is hot and humid. In winter Shirakawa-go and Takayama are under snow: beautiful, but Kamikochi is closed and the Nakasendō can have snow on its stone steps.
- **Documents:** Italian citizens need no tourist visa for up to 90 days, with a passport valid for the whole stay.
- **Currency:** yen. Cards work almost everywhere in the cities; in mountain villages, in the Kamikochi lodges and in some shukubō you need cash.
- **JR Pass:** in 2026 the ordinary 14-day pass costs 80,000 yen bought online (84,000 from an agency abroad), the 7-day one 50,000. It covers the Hikari and Kodama Shinkansen, the Hokuriku Shinkansen, and the Hida and Thunderbird limited expresses. Nozomi trains need a separate special ticket (4,960 yen between Tokyo and Kyoto). It doesn't cover the buses to Magome, Kamikochi and Shirakawa-go, the Odakyu lines to Hakone or the Nankai to Kōya-san: add up the legs before you buy it.
- **Buses to book:** some Nohi bus runs between Takayama, Shirakawa-go and Kanazawa are reservation only. The direct Matsumoto-Takayama bus is reservation only; times and rules for the Kamikochi buses are on the operators' websites. Better to check as soon as you have your dates.
- **Luggage:** a trolley on the Nakasendō and in Kamikochi is a problem. Between Magome and Tsumago there's the tourist offices' luggage service; for the rest you use takkyubin, hotel-to-hotel shipping, arranged the day before.
- **Where to sleep:** at least one night in a ryokan with onsen (Hakone), one in a Nakasendō village, one in a shukubō at Kōya-san. The gasshō houses in Shirakawa-go that offer lodging are few and need booking well ahead.
- **From the airport to the centre:** from Narita (NRT) the Narita Express reaches Tokyo Station in about an hour, about 3,100-3,300 yen with a reserved seat; the Skyliner gets to Ueno in about 40 minutes, about 2,600 yen; buses to Tokyo Station cost about 1,300-1,500 yen · from Haneda (HND) the Keikyu line goes to Shinagawa in about 15 minutes, about 300-330 yen, the monorail to Hamamatsuchō in about 20 · the last services leave around midnight, and taxis are very expensive · leaving from Kansai (KIX), the Haruka from Kyoto takes about 75-80 minutes, about 3,000-3,600 yen; from Namba, Nankai's Rapi:t costs about 1,500 yen · the classic mistake: you can't board the Narita Express or Skyliner with an IC card alone, you need the seat ticket.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Arrival in Tokyo
Landing at Narita or Haneda, an IC card (Suica or PASMO) loaded for subway and buses, and an easy first evening. From Haneda the centre is close, by monorail or the Keikyu line; from Narita it takes about an hour on the Narita Express to Tokyo Station, or the Keisei Skyliner to Ueno. The IC card is sold at station machines or can be added to your phone, and it also works in convenience stores and drink machines. *If you arrive in the morning after an overnight flight, hold out until evening with a gentle walk: going to sleep at three in the afternoon stretches jet lag over several days.*

The JR Pass should not be activated today: for the first three days you get around by subway, and the 14-day pass has to cover the long part of the trip. To stay, an area convenient for the trains on day 3: Shinjuku, or Tokyo Station. Shinjuku is the starting point of the Odakyu line to Hakone and stays lively late; Tokyo Station is quieter and handy for the Shinkansen. The first evening, eat near the hotel, at a ramen or soba place or an izakaya, and go to bed early.

**In practice:** Narita Express and Skyliner with reserved seats, tickets on arrival at the airport · IC card rechargeable with cash at the machines · Tokyo hotel rooms are small: send large suitcases ahead if you can.

### Day 2 — Tokyo, a single day
One full day, picking one side of the city. The old side: Asakusa and Senso-ji early, Ueno, Yanaka with its neighborhood streets. At seven in the morning Senso-ji has its shopping street still closed and the temple almost empty; Ueno has its park and museums, and Yanaka, on foot from Ueno, is one of the few districts that survived the bombings, with small temples, cemeteries and workshops. Lunch in Ueno, at the Ameyoko market.

Or the new side: Meiji-Jingu, Harajuku, Shibuya and Shinjuku in the evening. The Meiji shrine stands in a forest and is free to visit from sunrise to sunset; from there walk down to Harajuku and Omotesando, then Shibuya with the most photographed crossing in the world, and in the evening Shinjuku with the alleys of Omoide Yokocho and the bars of Golden Gai. Tokyo gets short shrift, on purpose: the real trip starts tomorrow. If you have two extra days, put them here; the addresses are in the [sakura trip](/viaggi/giappone-itinerario). *Tomorrow you leave for Hakone with a small backpack: in the evening pack the suitcase to send ahead or leave in storage.*

*My memory: Tokyo would deserve a holiday of its own.*

**In practice:** Senso-ji and Meiji-Jingu free to enter · subway with the IC card, or a Tokyo subway pass for 24, 48 or 72 hours · takkyubin from the hotel to send your suitcase a few days ahead.

### Day 3 — Hakone, the first ryokan
From Shinjuku you go to Hakone on the Odakyu line, or by Shinkansen to Odawara. Odakyu's Romancecar takes about 80 minutes to Hakone-Yumoto, with a reserved seat at a supplement. The classic route is a loop: mountain train, cable car, ropeway over the Owakudani fumaroles, boat across Lake Ashi, with Fuji visible only on clear days. The Odakyu Hakone Freepass covers almost everything. Start early and go in this order: train to Gora, cable car to Sounzan, ropeway with a stop at Owakudani, boat from Togendai, and bus back towards Hakone-Yumoto or to your ryokan.

The evening is the reason for the stop: a ryokan with onsen, kaiseki dinner and futon. Ryokan check-in is usually in the afternoon, and dinner is at a fixed time: arrive in time to bathe before dinner, in the yukata provided in the room. The futon is laid out while you are at dinner. *Fuji is often covered from Hakone: if you want it at all costs, take the day trip to Kawaguchiko described in the sakura trip.* In strong wind or with volcanic emissions the Owakudani ropeway stops, and replacement buses lengthen the loop.

**In practice:** Hakone Freepass from Shinjuku about 7,100 yen for 2 days (adults, Odakyu price), Romancecar extra · luggage storage and delivery to your hotel from Hakone-Yumoto station · ryokan to book months ahead for weekends and autumn.

### Day 4 — Toward the Nakasendō: Magome
From Odawara the Shinkansen takes you to Nagoya, then the Shinano limited express to Nakatsugawa and the bus to **Magome**. This is where you activate the JR Pass, if you chose one. The Hikari Shinkansen takes just over an hour to Nagoya, the Shinano under an hour to Nakatsugawa. The bus to Magome leaves from the station forecourt, roughly hourly: between train and bus you may wait from half an hour to an hour. The journey from the ryokan to Magome takes most of the morning.

Magome is an Edo-period post town, built uphill along the old road that linked Kyoto and Edo through the mountains. The main street is paved and steep, with wooden houses, water wheels and shops selling chestnut sweets. You arrive in the afternoon, when the day-trip groups leave. You sleep in a minshuku in the village or in Tsumago. *In minshuku dinner and breakfast are served at fixed times and the bath is shared: book early, there are few rooms.*

**In practice:** Nakatsugawa-Magome bus about 25-30 minutes, about 800 yen, not covered by the JR Pass · cash for the bus, the minshuku and the shops · if you are walking from Magome tomorrow, leave your bags at the tourist office in the morning.

### Day 5 — On foot from Magome to Tsumago, then Matsumoto
The most famous stretch of the Nakasendō: about 8 km, 2-3 hours of walking through woods, waterfalls and a teahouse halfway, with Magome pass at about 790 metres. From late March to late November the tourist offices of the two villages send luggage on: drop it between 8:30 and 11:30 and collect it on arrival after 13:00, at 1,000 yen per item. Starting from Magome you climb in the first part and descend towards Tsumago, the easier direction. Along the trail there are bells against bears: ring them as you pass.

**Tsumago** is the village its residents restored and have protected since 1968. Take your time, have lunch and collect your bags. From Nagiso station the Chuo line train takes you to **Matsumoto**. The station is about an hour's walk or a few minutes by bus or taxi from Tsumago; only some limited expresses stop at Nagiso, and with local trains you need a change. If you arrive in time, you can see the castle today. *Matsumoto Castle is one of the few in Japan with its original wooden keep: the inside stairs are very steep, so wear comfortable shoes and keep your hands free.*

**In practice:** Magome-Tsumago luggage service 1,000 yen per item · Matsumoto Castle from 8:30 to 17:00, last entry 16:30, adults about 1,200-1,300 yen · for tomorrow, arrange the Kamikochi bus and luggage sent to Takayama at the hotel tonight.

### Day 6 — Kamikochi and the road to Takayama
**Kamikochi** is a valley at about 1,500 metres in Chubu Sangaku National Park, closed to private cars: you can only get there by bus or taxi from the Sawando and Hirayu car parks. From Matsumoto take the Alpico train to Shin-Shimashima and then the bus, or a direct bus: about an hour and a half in total. The basic walk goes from Taisho pond to Kappa bridge, flat, between the Azusa river and the Hotaka peaks. Taisho pond was formed by the 1915 eruption of Mount Yake; from Kappa bridge you see the Hotaka range.

If you have the legs, continue to Myojin, about an extra hour each way, on the riverside path. In the afternoon the bus goes down to Hirayu Onsen and from there to **Takayama**. It is best to send your luggage straight to Takayama the day before. From Kamikochi to Hirayu the bus takes about half an hour, from Hirayu to Takayama about an hour. *Out of season, from mid-November to mid-April, Kamikochi is closed: take the direct Matsumoto-Takayama bus and gain half a day for Matsumoto Castle.* Bears and monkeys live in Kamikochi: don't leave food around and don't feed them leftovers.

**In practice:** 2026 season from 17 April to 15 November, private cars always banned · Kamikochi bus tickets at the bus station, with the return to buy at the Kamikochi terminal · warm layers even in summer, at 1,500 metres mornings are cool.

### Day 7 — Takayama
Start early with the **morning markets**, along the Miyagawa river and in front of Takayama Jinya, the old seat of local government. The Miyagawa market opens around seven, an hour later in winter, and closes at noon: vegetables, pickles, miso, rice sweets and wooden objects. Visit the Jinya right after, with its Edo-period office rooms and rice storehouses.

Then **Sanmachi Suji**, the streets of dark wooden houses with sake breweries, recognisable by the cedar balls hanging at the entrance. Many offer paid tastings, and before ten the streets are still quiet. In the afternoon Hida Folk Village, an open-air museum of mountain houses, useful even if you then don't sleep in Shirakawa-go. It is a few minutes by bus from the station. For dinner Hida beef, as steak or as street skewers. *In Takayama restaurants close early: for dinner, book or sit down before seven.*

**In practice:** Miyagawa market roughly from 7:00 to 12:00, from 8:00 in winter · Hida Folk Village about 700 yen, open from 8:30 to 17:00 · for tomorrow, book the Nohi bus to Shirakawa-go and send the suitcase to Kanazawa.

### Day 8 — Shirakawa-go and Kanazawa
The Nohi bus runs from Takayama to **Shirakawa-go** and on to Kanazawa: just over two hours for the whole route. Some services are reservation-only; prices and times on the Nohi website. The Takayama-Shirakawa-go leg takes under an hour; take a morning bus and leave in the afternoon, with at least three or four hours in the village. At the terminal there are lockers and a luggage room, but space is limited.

Ogimachi village has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1995 for its gasshō-zukuri houses, with steep thatched roofs like hands joined in prayer. The Shiroyama viewpoint gives the overall view. You get there on foot uphill in about twenty minutes, or by shuttle. Walk through it discreetly: these are lived-in houses, and many cannot be visited. Some of the largest, such as the Wada house, are open as museums for a small fee and show the multi-storey interior, with the attics where silkworms were raised. In the afternoon another bus continues to **Kanazawa**. If you booked in time, sleep in a gasshō house and continue tomorrow. *In the evening, once the last bus has left, the village goes quiet again: that is the advantage of staying overnight.*

**In practice:** Takayama-Shirakawa-go-Kanazawa about 2 hours 5 to 2 hours 20 in total, bus not covered by the JR Pass · in Shirakawa-go leave no rubbish, take it with you · on winter illumination evenings access is regulated and bookings are made months ahead.

### Day 9 — Kanazawa
Kanazawa was spared from wartime bombing and kept much of the city of samurai and merchants. **Kenroku-en**, one of Japan's most celebrated gardens, should be seen at opening time. From 1 March to 15 October it opens at seven, in winter at eight, and in the first hour you walk among ponds, stone lanterns and rope-supported pines without the crowds of groups. Opposite is the castle park, free to enter.

Then the castle, the **Higashi Chaya** teahouse district, the Nagamachi samurai district and the **21st Century Museum** of contemporary art. Higashi Chaya has slatted wooden facades and some teahouses open to visitors, Nagamachi earthen walls and canals, the museum a circular glass building with a free section and paid exhibitions. For lunch **Omicho** market, for fish from the Sea of Japan. *Many Omicho stalls close in the early afternoon, and the best-known sushi places already have queues at noon: go early.*

**In practice:** Kenroku-en about 320 yen, 7:00-18:00 from March to mid-October, 8:00-17:00 the rest of the year · 21st Century Museum closed on Mondays, exhibition tickets around 1,200 yen · Kanazawa is easy on foot and with the city-centre buses.

### Day 10 — From Kanazawa to Kyoto
Hokuriku Shinkansen to Tsuruga and Thunderbird limited express to Kyoto, with one change at Tsuruga, all covered by the JR Pass. About two hours in total, and the change at Tsuruga is in the same station, on different levels. Reserve seats on the Thunderbird the day before, especially at weekends. Your suitcase, if sent ahead, arrives directly at the Kyoto hotel.

Arrival for lunch. The afternoon is for Higashiyama, on foot from Kiyomizu-dera towards Gion. Climb to Kiyomizu-dera with its wooden terrace above the forest, walk down the lanes of Sannenzaka and Ninenzaka, then Yasaka shrine and the streets of Gion towards evening. Kyoto needs more days than this trip gives it: here are the two you need to see the centre, the rest is in the sakura trip. *Some private alleys in Gion are closed to tourists, with signs and fines, and maiko are not stopped for photos.*

**In practice:** Kanazawa-Kyoto about 2 hours with the change at Tsuruga, seat reserved on the JR Pass · Kiyomizu-dera has a paid entry and opens early in the morning · in Kyoto buses are slow and crowded: walk, take the subway, and share taxis between two or more.

### Day 11 — Kyoto
**Fushimi Inari** early, climbing beyond the part everyone photographs. It is always open and free; you get there on the JR Nara line to Inari station, two stops from Kyoto. The most famous torii tunnels are at the start, and after the first viewpoint the crowds thin out: the full loop of the mountain takes two or three hours.

*My memory: I recommend exploring Kyoto early in the morning, to get a more authentic feel. At Fushimi Inari, though, there are always people, whatever time you go; the higher you climb, the fewer there are. I went all the way to the top: the view is nothing special, but the experience is lovely.*

Then the north-west of the city: Kinkaku-ji, the sand garden of Ryoan-ji, Arashiyama in the afternoon. Kinkaku-ji and Ryoan-ji are close, about twenty minutes' walk from each other, and are reached by bus or taxi. In Arashiyama the bamboo grove is packed at midday: focus on the Tenryu-ji garden and the riverbank. The day is dense. If you prefer a slower pace, replace Arashiyama with the zen temple district of Daitoku-ji. *In autumn and spring Kinkaku-ji has long queues: arriving at opening time changes the visit.*

**In practice:** Fushimi Inari free and open 24 hours · Kinkaku-ji about 500 yen, Ryoan-ji about 600 · from Arashiyama back to Kyoto by JR train with the pass.

### Day 12 — Nara
Half a day, or a full one at a relaxed pace. From Kyoto you get there on the JR Nara Line. The rapid takes under an hour, and from JR Nara station to the park is about a twenty-minute walk. Leave early and reach Todaiji before the school groups, who arrive mid-morning.

Todaiji with the Great Buddha, Kasuga Taisha with its lanterns, the sika deer roaming free in the park. The Daibutsuden is one of the largest wooden buildings in the world and houses the bronze Buddha statue; from the temple a forest path leads to Kasuga Taisha, with thousands of stone and bronze lanterns. The deer approach anyone who buys the special crackers: *they can push and bite, so hand out crackers one at a time and keep maps in your pocket.* Return to Kyoto for the night, or move on to Osaka to be closer to tomorrow's Nankai line.

*My memory: in Nara I ate out-of-this-world tuna at **Maguro Koya**, truly delicious.*

**In practice:** Todaiji Daibutsuden 800 yen · Kasuga Taisha with a paid area around the main sanctuary · from Nara to Osaka by JR train in under an hour, to stay in Namba.

### Day 13 — Kōya-san, the night at the temple
From Osaka Namba the Nankai line climbs to Gokurakubashi, then a cable car and a bus take you up to the **Kōya-san** plateau, the centre of Shingon Buddhism founded by Kūkai in the 9th century. The Koya limited express takes about an hour and twenty minutes to Gokurakubashi, with a reserved seat at a supplement; rapids without a supplement take a little longer. The cable car climbs in a few minutes. Nankai sells a ticket that includes the return train, cable car and buses on the plateau.

On the plateau there are more than a hundred temples, and many offer lodging: the **shukubō**. You sleep on tatami and have dinner with the monks' vegetarian cuisine, shojin ryori. Check-in is in the afternoon and dinner is early, around half past five or six. Before dinner, Kongobu-ji and the Danjo Garan complex. Kongobu-ji is the school's head temple, with its stone garden and painted sliding doors; the Danjo Garan has the great red pagoda. *The status of the cable car and the connections should be checked on the Nankai website before you go.* The JR Pass does not cover the Nankai line.

**In practice:** Koya limited express about 80 minutes, supplement around 1,100 yen · shukubō to book in advance, cash useful · at 800 metres evenings are cool even in summer and cold in winter.

### Day 14 — Okunoin at dawn and back to Osaka
In the morning you join the monks' prayer, then walk through **Okunoin**, the largest cemetery in Japan: about two kilometres among centuries-old cedars and more than two hundred thousand graves and monuments, up to Kūkai's mausoleum. The prayer is at dawn, usually around six or half past six, and lasts about half an hour; afterwards there is a vegetarian breakfast. You enter Okunoin from Ichinohashi bridge and follow the avenue among the graves of feudal lords and corporate memorials.

*After Gobyo bridge, in front of the lantern hall and the mausoleum, no photos and no eating: it is the most sacred part.* In the afternoon you go down to **Osaka**, by bus, cable car and Nankai to Namba, about two hours in total. The evening is for Dotonbori and Shinsekai, with okonomiyaki and takoyaki. Dotonbori is the street of neon signs on the canal, Shinsekai a working-class district from the early twentieth century around the Tsutenkaku tower, with fried kushikatsu.

*My memory: the Dotonbori area is packed, and my friends had mixed opinions about it, but I absolutely loved it.*

**In practice:** Okunoin free, accessible in the evening too · from the plateau to Namba about two hours · in Osaka stay in Namba or Umeda; for tomorrow, the Shin-Osaka area or a direct line to the Shinkansen is handy.

### Day 15 — Hiroshima and Miyajima
The Shinkansen takes you to Hiroshima from Shin-Osaka. With the JR Pass you take Sakura or Hikari trains, and the trip takes about an hour and a half. From Hiroshima station to the Peace Park take the tram, paid separately, or a taxi. The **Peace Park and Museum**, with the time they ask of you. The museum opens early in the warmer months, and the first hour is the quietest; the visit takes at least an hour and a half. In the park, the Atomic Bomb Dome, the Cenotaph and the Children's Peace Monument with its paper cranes.

Then a local train and the JR ferry to **Miyajima**, both covered by the pass. The Sanyo line train reaches Miyajimaguchi in about half an hour, and the ferry crosses in ten minutes. It is best to sleep on the island: in the evening, when the day-trippers leave, Itsukushima shrine and the torii in the water are another place. *The torii seems to float at high tide, and at low tide you can walk to it: tide tables are on the island's tourism website.*

*My memory: Hiroshima pushed me to my limit: towards the end I had to speed up, because emotionally it was "too much". I liked Miyajima, but I wouldn't call it essential, and the same goes for Hiroshima: if you have fewer days, I'd cut this part (my personal opinion).*

**In practice:** Peace Museum 200 yen for adults, hours that change with the season · 100 yen tax for anyone landing on Miyajima, not covered by the JR Pass, paid at the machine before boarding · few places to stay on the island, book early.

### Day 16 — Miyajima and the way back
In the morning Mount Misen, on foot or by ropeway, with the view over the Seto Inland Sea. On foot it takes an hour and a half to two hours from the Daisho-in trail, among temples and stone steps; the ropeway takes you almost to the top and from there you walk another twenty minutes or so. Start early, to have the summit to yourself and the morning light on the islands.

Then ferry and Shinkansen towards Osaka, for those flying from Kansai, or towards Tokyo, for those leaving from there: a long journey, the last full day of the JR Pass. *Without the Nozomi the trip to Tokyo is longer and usually requires a change; with the special ticket for pass holders, about 6,500 yen between Hiroshima and Tokyo, you can also ride the Nozomi.* If you are heading to Osaka or Kyoto you arrive in the afternoon and have the evening for last purchases.

**In practice:** Misen ropeway paid, suspended in strong wind · on Miyajima the deer snatch food from your hands, eat somewhere sheltered · reserve Shinkansen seats with the pass the day before.

### Day 17 — Return home
Departure from Kansai or Tokyo. From Kansai, the Haruka reaches the airport from Kyoto and Osaka. From Kyoto it takes about 75-80 minutes, from Tennoji and Shin-Osaka a little less; without a pass the ticket costs about 3,000-3,600 yen, depending on the seat. From Tokyo, leave on the Narita Express for Narita, or the monorail or Keikyu for Haneda.

*Arrive at the airport three hours ahead for intercontinental flights, and for tax-free purchases keep passport, receipts and goods to hand for the customs check.* IC cards with remaining credit can be used for last purchases at the airport, in convenience stores and drink machines.

**In practice:** Haruka with non-reserved or reserved seats, different prices · Kyoto-Kansai airport about 75-80 minutes · heavy bags sent to the airport by takkyubin the day before.

## What to see / what to do
- **The Nakasendō between Magome and Tsumago:** eight kilometers on foot on a road from three hundred years ago, with your luggage traveling on its own.
- **Kamikochi:** the simplest way to see the Japanese Alps up close, on flat trails. Only from mid-April to mid-November.
- **Takayama at dawn:** the morning markets and the Sanmachi streets before ten.
- **Shirakawa-go:** the gasshō houses, best seen also in the evening or early morning if you sleep there.
- **Kenroku-en and Higashi Chaya in Kanazawa.**
- **A night in a shukubō at Kōya-san**, with the morning prayer and Okunoin.
- **Miyajima in the evening**, after the last wave of day trips.

## Variants
**14 days.** Drop Hakone (go from Tokyo straight to Nagoya) and Nara becomes a half day from Kyoto. Skip Kōya-san or Hiroshima: keeping both in 14 days means rushing.

**21 days.** Two more days in Tokyo, with the trip to Nikko or Kawaguchiko. One more day in Kyoto. A night in Hirayu or Okuhida Onsen between Kamikochi and Takayama. Himeji Castle between Osaka and Hiroshima.

**In winter (December-March).** Kamikochi is closed. Shirakawa-go under snow is the show of the season, and in January there are a few evenings of illumination (in 2027 on January 11, 17, 24 and 31). On those dates access is regulated and lodging is assigned by lottery, months ahead. If you want to ski, add a few days in Hakuba, described in the [Hokkaido and skiing trip](/viaggi/hokkaido-sci-giappone).

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Do the JR Pass math.** This route uses many buses and private lines the pass doesn't cover. The 14-day pass pays off if you activate it on day 4 and finish with the return to Tokyo; without the return, single tickets may cost less.
- **Book the buses as soon as you have your dates:** Nohi for Shirakawa-go, the Matsumoto-Takayama bus, and the gasshō lodgings.
- **Ship your luggage** between Matsumoto and Takayama and between Takayama and Kanazawa. In the villages and in Kamikochi you get around with a small backpack.
- **Cash** for minshuku, the morning markets, the shukubō.
- **Respect in lived-in villages:** in Shirakawa-go and on the Nakasendō, don't enter private courtyards and don't leave trash: take your rubbish with you.
- **Onsen and tattoos:** many ryokan still ban them in the shared baths. Rooms with a private bath or private rental baths, the kashikiri, solve it.

## Chiusura personale
I found the classic route packed with tourists everywhere, Kyoto above all, which I recommend exploring early in the morning. At Fushimi Inari I climbed all the way to the top: the view is nothing special, but the experience is lovely. Tokyo would deserve a holiday of its own, I absolutely loved Dotonbori, and in Nara I ate out-of-this-world tuna at Maguro Koya.

Hiroshima pushed me to my limit: towards the end I had to speed up, because emotionally it was "too much". If you have fewer days, in my opinion you can cut Hiroshima and Miyajima.
