# A Ski Week in Italy: How to Choose Where, When and How Much to Spend

## Apertura personale
The ski week is the Italian trip that more than any other gets decided out of habit: you go where you've always gone, the week you've always gone, and you pay what you've always paid. Which is a pity, because in the same ski area, in the same apartment, the same week can cost double or half depending on the date, and because the criterion that matters most today, **altitude**, almost never enters the decision. With the winters of recent years, below eighteen hundred meters natural snow is no longer a guarantee, and choosing a resort for its fame rather than its elevation is the quickest way to find a white ribbon in the middle of the grass. This itinerary is built on one Dolomites ski area as an example, but the useful part is the criteria: they apply everywhere.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go — the choice that matters most:**
  - **Christmas and New Year:** the two most expensive weeks of the year, with differences of **as much as forty percent** compared with January. Crowded slopes, lines, bookings months ahead.
  - **January after Epiphany:** the **best window by far** for value. Snow is generally excellent, ski areas fully open, slopes empty on weekdays, prices dropping right after the 6th. In return, intense cold and short days.
  - **February:** statistically the most reliable snow, but it coincides with school ski weeks, which vary from region to region. High prices and lines at midday.
  - **March and early April:** long days, sun, lunches on the terraces, falling prices. But the snow transforms (hard in the morning, mushy in the afternoon) and at low altitude it can disappear. **You need high altitude.**
- **The altitude criterion:** above **two thousand meters** at the base you can ski with reasonable certainty; below **eighteen hundred** you depend on artificial snow. Today it is the most important parameter, more than size or fame.
- **Ski pass:** in **Dolomiti Superski** the day pass in high season is around **86 euros** (about 77 in low season) and the season pass around **970**. These figures are indicative and worth rechecking every year. **Buying the ski pass and lodging as a package saves real money** compared with booking them separately.
- **Rental:** €25-45 a day for a full set, with discounts on weekly packages. Booking online before leaving costs less than showing up at the shop.
- **Road:** **chains or winter tires are required by law** on much of the mountain network in winter, with dates that vary from region to region. It is an obligation you can be fined for, not a recommendation.
- **Insurance — the point almost nobody checks:** in Italy **rescue on the slopes and in the mountains is paid for**, and in several regions a helicopter rescue costs thousands of euros. Many ski passes include basic coverage: check exactly what it covers. Since 2022 **liability insurance is required by law for anyone skiing on the slopes**, and you need to make sure your own policy includes it.
- **Off-piste:** **avalanche transceiver, shovel and probe, and knowing how to use them.** The avalanche bulletin is public and daily, on a scale of 1 to 5, and must be read before leaving the groomed runs. It's not something to improvise.
- **Where to sleep:** in winter **being on the slopes matters**: half an hour of shuttle in ski boots twice a day weighs more than it seems when you book.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Arrival and Picking Up the Gear
We arrived in the afternoon and picked up the ski pass and rental, both booked online before leaving: it costs less and you skip the first-day line.

The first evening was light. The ski week is won on stamina, and whoever goes wild on day one arrives at Thursday wrecked.

**To check right away:** exactly what the insurance included in the ski pass covers, and whether your own ski liability cover is in order.

### Day 2 — Warm-Up and Reconnaissance
It's the day to get to know the ski area: we skied the nearby sectors, looked at where the sun hits at different hours, and scouted the huts.

**A practical tip that works everywhere:** ski the east-facing slope in the morning and the west-facing one in the afternoon, following the sun. On the transformed snow of March the rule matters even more.

Lunch in a mountain hut, which in Italy is half the reason you ski: canederli, polenta, goulash, no self-service.

### Day 3 — The Sellaronda Loop
It's the day that makes it worth coming to the Dolomites: the **full loop around the Sella massif**, four passes and four valleys, about 26 kilometers of descents and as many of lifts.

**You start at nine sharp**, no later. The last lifts close in early afternoon, and anyone who starts late risks ending up on the wrong side of the massif, with two hours of valley bus to get back to the car.

**Counterclockwise** (green signs), the less traveled direction and with fewer lines.

**To check the night before:** the wind. It's what closes the pass lifts and interrupts the loop.

### Day 4 — Technical Day or Active Rest
Midweek the legs make themselves felt. There are two ways to go.

For good skiers, a day on a more demanding sector, with black runs and, if conditions allow and you have self-rescue gear, a stretch of off-piste with a guide.

For anyone who wants to unplug, **snowshoes or cross-country skiing**, which give you the winter mountains in silence, without the effort of the descent. Or a morning at the thermal baths, which are found everywhere in South Tyrol and Trentino.

### Day 5 — Ski Touring, or the Mountain Without Lifts
It's the day that changes your relationship with winter. You climb with **climbing skins** under your skis, for two hours, in silence, while a little further on the lifts carry hundreds of people up the same slope. Then you take off the skins and descend on snow no one has touched.

**The first time you go with a mountain guide**, after a self-rescue course. Transceiver, shovel and probe are useless if you don't know how to use them, and **avalanche risk is read in a public bulletin and is constantly underestimated**.

*Risk-free alternative: a marked snowshoe route, which gives ninety percent of the silence with a fraction of the exposure.*

### Day 6 — The Long Day
It's the last full day, to be made the most of: first chairlift at opening, when the slopes are freshly groomed and there's nobody there.

It's also the day of the **long lunch in a booked mountain hut** (in high season the best ones are booked even for midday). It's what distinguishes Italian skiing from French: here you sit down.

For anyone with energy left there's **night skiing**, where the lit slopes stay open.

### Day 7 — Last Morning and Return
Half a day on skis, returning the rental and departure. Half-day rates exist and are worth using.

**To factor in:** Saturday morning, on the return roads from the Alpine valley floors, there is always traffic. Either you leave very early or you leave in the afternoon.

## What to see / what to do
- **Choose the date before the place.** January after Epiphany is worth a forty percent discount on the exact same trip.
- **Choose the ski area by altitude**, not by fame.
- **Book the ski pass and lodging as a package**, and the rental online.
- **The Sellaronda at nine, counterclockwise**, checking the wind the night before.
- **Check the insurance**: in Italy rescue is paid for and ski liability cover is mandatory.
- **Have lunch in a mountain hut, seated**: it's the reason you ski here and not elsewhere.
- **A day without lifts**, with snowshoes or ski touring.
- **Follow the sun:** east-facing slope in the morning, west in the afternoon.

## Possible extensions
- **Cervinia-Zermatt (+2-3 days):** for the highest altitude in the Italian Alps and the international link, skiing beneath the Matterhorn from both sides.
- **Livigno (+3-4 days):** a duty-free zone, reliable snow, one of the best snowparks in Europe and gear that costs a good deal less.
- **Monterosa Ski (+3-4 days):** the Italian reference for freeride, with three Walser valleys and an identity that purpose-built resorts lack.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **On the slopes, if possible.** Ski-in/ski-out options cost more and are generally worth it: half an hour of shuttle in ski boots, twice a day for six days, is three wasted hours.
- **Half board in a hotel**: still the most economical format for a week, especially in packages that include ski pass and rental.
- **Apartment** for anyone traveling in a group or with children: grocery costs plunge, and in many resorts the guest card includes the valley ski buses.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **At altitude, seated.** It's the Italian advantage on snow: slope-side huts aren't canteens. Canederli, polenta, goulash and casunziei in the Dolomites; polenta concia and fonduta in Valle d'Aosta; pizzoccheri in Valtellina.
- **In high season the best huts are booked even for lunch**, and anyone who doesn't know it eats wherever they end up.
- **In the valley:** half board takes care of dinner, but it's worth going out at least a couple of evenings to the village trattorias, which cost less than the hotel and cook better.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Don't book at Christmas or New Year if you can choose.** They are the two most expensive weeks of the year, no comparison.
- **Don't choose the resort by fame:** today, below eighteen hundred meters, snow is a gamble.
- **Don't buy the ski pass and rental separately** when the package exists.
- **Don't hit the road without chains or winter tires:** it's required by law, and you risk a fine.
- **Check the insurance**, because in Italy rescue is paid for and ski liability cover is required by law.
- **Don't leave the groomed runs without a transceiver, shovel, probe and the ability to use them**, and without having read the avalanche bulletin.
- **Don't start the Sellaronda after nine**, and check the wind.
- **Don't underestimate Saturday traffic** on the return roads.

## Chiusura personale
What you understand after a few ski weeks is that the pleasure isn't in the kilometers. You can spend a day grinding out runs and come home in the evening remembering nothing, or do half those kilometers, stop at eleven in a hut at twenty-four hundred meters, order something hot and sit for twenty minutes in the sun with a pink rock wall in front of you. In France they do the first better; the second, here, nobody does better than we do. And then there's that morning (it happens once a week, if you're lucky) when you take the first chairlift with the slope freshly groomed, there's still nobody around, and you ski down something smooth and perfect that in an hour won't exist anymore. For that half hour, in the end, you pay for everything else.
