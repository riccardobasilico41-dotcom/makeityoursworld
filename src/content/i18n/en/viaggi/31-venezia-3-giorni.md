# Venice in 3 Days, Away from the Rialto-San Marco Axis

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 work in progress (itinerary and practical information verified; the first-person passages are written as a draft to reread and personalize)
**Periodo del viaggio:**
**Durata:** 3 days, 2 nights
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independent, all on foot and by vaporetto
**Categoria:** Europa · Italia · Venezia · arte · laguna · gastronomia

---

## Apertura personale
Venice is the most misunderstood city in Italy, and the reason is that the vast majority of people who go see it for six hours, in June, between Piazzale Roma and Piazza San Marco, that is, along the one corridor of the city that is completely saturated. With that experience in your pocket it's reasonable to conclude that Venice is a theme park.

It takes very little to turn it upside down: **sleep there** and **walk in the wrong direction**. Venice has six sestieri and tourists concentrate in half a sestiere; at seven in the evening, when the day trips have left, the campi of Castello and Cannaregio empty out and the Venetians remain. At seven in the morning, on the Rialto Bridge, you can be alone.

Three days is the right measure to do the big things without giving up the rest: and the rest, here, is the best part.

*My memory: Venice is beautiful, all of it. Seriously.*

## Scheda pratica
- **Access fee:** on certain dates Venice applies **a fee for those entering the city for the day**, with **a reservation and a code to show**. **Anyone staying overnight in the city is exempt but must still register** (usually the accommodation does it, and applies the tourist tax separately). **Dates, amounts and procedures have changed several times since its introduction**: check the municipality's website before leaving, because the fine for those not in order is steep.
- **When to go:** **from November to March, outside Carnival**, Venice is itself: fog, few people, low light, reasonable prices. **June to August is the worst time**: hot, humid, saturated, and in summer the minor canals have a smell no guidebook mentions. **April-May and October** are the compromise. **Carnival, the Redentore (July) and the Regata Storica (September)** are spectacular and at the same time unmanageable: you go on purpose or you avoid them.
- **Acqua alta:** it mostly concerns **autumn and winter**. The **MOSE** movable barrier system has come into operation and has greatly reduced flooding, **but has not eliminated it**: it is activated above thresholds decided case by case, and the smaller tides get through. The municipality's Tide Center publishes forecasts and the city sounds the sirens. In practice: check the forecasts the night before, and **light rubber boots in your pack** if you go in season. Raised walkways cover the main routes, not all of them.
- **Useful bookings:**
  - **St. Mark's Basilica:** entry itself costs about €10, but **online booking with a time slot skips a line that in high season is an hour or more**, and is worth every cent. The **Pala d'Oro** and the **St. Mark's Museum with the Loggia dei Cavalli** are additional tickets: **do the Loggia**, because you step out onto the balcony above the square. **Dress code: shoulders and knees covered**, and **large backpacks are not allowed**. There's a free cloakroom in a nearby calle, and anyone who doesn't know loses half an hour.
  - **Doge's Palace:** combined ticket with the museums of Piazza San Marco. The **Secret Itineraries**, which lead into the administrative rooms, the torture chamber and the Piombi from which Casanova escaped, **are by reservation, in small groups, and sell out early**.
- **Vaporetti:** the **single ticket is very expensive** and is good for little more than an hour. **Day passes or multi-day passes pay for themselves on the third ride** and are almost mandatory if you do the islands. Note: **Venice is explored on foot**; the vaporetto is for the Grand Canal, the islands and long transfers.
- **The gondola ferry (traghetto):** at certain points on the Grand Canal, where there are no bridges, **gondolas ferry across from one bank to the other for about 2 euros**, and you stand up like the Venetians. It's the cheapest way to get on a gondola and almost no tourist knows it. **The active lines change over time:** ask on the spot which are running.
- **The gondola:** it has an **official rate set by the municipality**, **per gondola and not per person** (up to five passengers), with **a higher rate in the evening** and a surcharge for a serenade. Haggling is pointless, but **checking the rate in force before you board is worthwhile**, because it's the area with the most misunderstandings.
- **Chorus Pass:** a combined pass for about fifteen historic churches, the densest and least crowded artistic heritage in the city. It pays off from the third or fourth church.
- **Food:** the **bacari** are the taverns where you eat **cicchetti** standing at the counter, with an **ombra**, a glass of wine. **Sarde in saor** (with onions, raisins and pine nuts, of seafaring origin), **baccalà mantecato**, **folpetti**, **liver Venetian-style**, **risotto di gò** from the lagoon, and **moeche**, the crabs caught in the brief moment of molting when they have a soft shell (**seasonal, spring and autumn**). The **spritz** was born here, and the Venetian version is more bitter than the one drunk in the rest of Italy.
- **From the airport to the centre:** from **Marco Polo (VCE)** the ATVO **bus** goes to **Piazzale Roma** in about 20 minutes (about €10, €18 return) and ACTV's line 5 Aerobus in about 25 minutes for the same price, also as a combined ticket with the vaporetto; tickets at the arrivals ticket office, at the machines or online, buses until late at night · the **Alilaguna motorboat** reaches San Marco and Fondamente Nove in about 75 minutes (about €15-18): slow, scenic, pricier than the bus, and not included in ACTV passes · private water taxi from about €120-150 per boat, shared about €35-40 per person, only at the official desks · **There are no cars in the city:** from the terminal you continue on foot or by vaporetto, **with your luggage, over the bridges, by hand**. Anyone who brings big suitcases to a city of bridges regrets it immediately.
- **Getting your bearings:** house numbers follow the sestiere, not the street, and the only sensible way to move around is **the yellow signs painted on the walls**: "per Rialto", "per San Marco", "per Ferrovia". **Getting lost is expected, and should be counted as an activity, not an accident.**

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — San Marco, But at the Right Hour
Venice's rule is that famous places should be done **early or late**, never in the middle.

We entered **St. Mark's Basilica** with a reservation, just after opening. Inside, the effect is all in the **8,000 square meters of gold-ground mosaics** covering the domes and vaults. The basilica is built on a Greek-cross plan with five domes, and is Byzantine, not Western, architecture: Venice looked to Constantinople, and the basilica says so before any history book. The floor is an **undulating mosaic**, deformed by centuries of settling on the mud of the lagoon. The **Pala d'Oro** behind the altar is a slab of gold with thousands of Byzantine stones and enamels. The **Loggia dei Cavalli** is worth the supplement: the four bronze horses displayed inside are the originals (**looted from Constantinople in 1204, taken to Paris by Napoleon and returned**), the ones on the facade are copies. From the loggia you step out above the square.

Then the **Doge's Palace**, which was not a royal palace but **the seat of government, courts and prisons all together**. There's the Hall of the Great Council with Tintoretto's **Paradise**, among the largest canvas paintings in the world, and the sequence of doges with **a black panel in place of the portrait of Marin Falier**, beheaded for treason and erased from official memory. You exit across the **Bridge of Sighs** toward the new prisons: you see it from inside, not from the postcard. If you booked, the **Secret Itineraries** are the best version of the visit.

In the afternoon, **don't go up the St. Mark's bell tower**: there's a line and above all from the top **you can't see the bell tower**, which is exactly what you want in the photo. We took the vaporetto instead to **San Giorgio Maggiore**, across the basin. The church is by Palladio, the **bell tower has an elevator**, and from up there you look at **the whole of Piazza San Marco with its bell tower**: it's the best view of the city. It costs little and often there's no one.

In the evening we were in **Castello**, the sestiere right behind San Marco: ten minutes' walk east is enough and the city changes completely.

**In practice:** San Marco with an online booking at opening time, shoulders and knees covered, large backpacks at the free left-luggage in a nearby calle · Pala d'Oro and Loggia dei Cavalli on extra tickets · Palazzo Ducale with a combined ticket, Secret Itineraries to book · vaporetto to San Giorgio Maggiore, bell tower with a lift and a modest ticket · check beforehand whether your date falls under the access fee.

### Day 2 — Dorsoduro, the Frari and San Rocco
The day of great art, almost all on foot and almost all without lines.

The morning at the **Gallerie dell'Accademia**, which collect Venetian painting from the fourteenth to the eighteenth century: Bellini, Carpaccio with his narrative cycles, Giorgione with **The Tempest**, Tintoretto, Veronese with the **Feast in the House of Levi**. It was a Last Supper, and the painter **renamed it after a trial by the Inquisition**, which accused him of putting dwarfs, jesters and dogs in it.

Then **Santa Maria Gloriosa dei Frari**, the great Gothic church of the Franciscans. Above the altar is Titian's **Assumption**, which changed the scale of Venetian painting and is still in the spot it was painted for: you enter from the back and see it on axis, at the end of the nave, just as it was designed. Inside are also **Titian's tomb** and the funerary monument to **Canova**, in the shape of a pyramid.

Two hundred meters away is the **Scuola Grande di San Rocco**, the most underrated thing in Venice. **Tintoretto worked there for over twenty years** and filled two floors with canvases, from ceiling to walls. **There are hand mirrors** to look at the ceilings without breaking your neck. There's never a line, and it's worth the trip on its own.

The afternoon in **Dorsoduro**, the most livable sestiere: the **Peggy Guggenheim Collection** in the unfinished palace on the Grand Canal where she lived (Pollock, Ernst, De Chirico, and the garden with her tomb and those of her dogs), or **Punta della Dogana** and **Palazzo Grassi** for contemporary art. The **Basilica della Salute**, on the point, was built as an ex voto for the end of the plague of 1630 and rests on over a hundred thousand wooden piles driven into the mud.

At sunset we went to the **Zattere**, the long quay facing the Giudecca, where Venetians go to walk.

The evening of **cicchetti** among the bacari of Dorsoduro and San Polo, standing, moving from one counter to another. It's the right way to dine in Venice, and costs a fraction of a restaurant.

**In practice:** Accademia and Peggy Guggenheim with their own tickets, the Guggenheim is closed on Tuesdays · Frari with an entrance ticket, or with the Chorus Pass · Scuola Grande di San Rocco with a ticket, mirrors provided · all on foot between Dorsoduro and San Polo · cicchetti at the counter, a few euros each with an ombra.

### Day 3 — Cannaregio, the Ghetto and the Islands
The morning in **Cannaregio**, the most lived-in and least visited of the six sestieri. **The Venetian Ghetto is the first ghetto in the world**: in 1516 the Republic obliged Jews to reside in this area closed off by guarded gates, and the word itself comes from here, from the foundry ("geto") that stood there before. Since they couldn't build outward, **they built upward**: the buildings of the Campo di Ghetto Nuovo have **very low ceilings and more levels than any other Venetian building**, and you can see it with the naked eye. There are five historic synagogues, visitable with a guide through the **Jewish Museum**. **Hours and availability change** and depend on holidays: check them beforehand.

Then the **Madonna dell'Orto**, Tintoretto's church, where he is buried, and the **Fondamenta della Misericordia** with its straight canals, bacari and neighborhood life.

The afternoon is for the **northern lagoon**, by vaporetto:
- **Murano** for glass. The furnaces can be visited, **but must be chosen carefully**: the circuit of free demonstrations always ends in a showroom with pressure to buy. The **Glass Museum** is the honest visit.
- **Burano** for the houses painted in solid colors (a fishermen's tradition, who recognized them from the lagoon in the fog) and for **lace**, almost vanished as real craft; the museum tells its history. Beware: almost all the lace on sale in Burano is not made there.
- **Torcello**, which almost nobody does and is the reason to come to the lagoon. It is **the island from which Venice was born**: it had tens of thousands of inhabitants before malaria and silting emptied it, and today it has a few dozen. What remains is the **Basilica of Santa Maria Assunta**, founded in the 7th century, with a **mosaic Last Judgment** on the inner facade and a **Byzantine Madonna on a gold ground** in the apse, alone, on an empty golden field: one of the strongest images in all Venetian art. Outside is the grass field with the **Devil's Bridge**, without parapets.

**Recommended order:** Torcello first or last, and **don't leave it out** to go shopping in Murano.

**In practice:** Jewish Museum and synagogues on accompanied visits, hours tied to holidays and the Sabbath · day vaporetto pass for the northern lagoon · Murano, Burano and Torcello linked by lines from Fondamente Nove, with crossing times that add up · in Torcello the basilica and bell tower with a ticket · check the last vaporetto from Torcello, departures are sparse.

## What to see / what to do
- **St. Mark's Basilica, booked, at opening**, with the Loggia dei Cavalli.
- **The Doge's Palace**, and the Secret Itineraries if you book.
- **The San Giorgio Maggiore bell tower** instead of St. Mark's.
- **The Scuola Grande di San Rocco**, with the mirrors for the ceilings.
- **Titian's Assumption at the Frari**, seen on axis from the back of the nave.
- **The Ghetto in Cannaregio**, with the buildings built upward.
- **Torcello**, the basilica and the mosaic of the Madonna on a gold ground.
- **Cicchetti in the bacari**, standing, changing counters.
- **The gondola ferry** to cross the Grand Canal standing up, for about 2 euros.
- **Getting up at dawn** and walking alone on Rialto.

## Possible extensions
- **Biennale (+1 day):** **art in odd years, architecture in even years**, at the Giardini and the Arsenale, between May and November. The **Arsenale** is worth a visit even just as a space: it was the Republic's shipyard and for centuries the largest industrial complex in Europe.
- **Minor islands (+1 day):** **San Lazzaro degli Armeni**, a monastery and library visitable with a guide at fixed times; **the Lido**, with the beach and Liberty-style architecture; **San Francesco del Deserto**, almost impossible to reach and therefore silent.
- **Padua (+1 day):** under half an hour by train, with Giotto's **Scrovegni Chapel**: **entry only by reservation, in groups, with a wait in an acclimatization chamber before entering** and a stay of a few minutes. It's one of the most restricted visits in Italy and has to be organized accordingly.
- **Verona and the Prosecco hills (+2 days):** it links naturally to the itinerary on the great wine routes.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **In the city, not in Mestre.** It's the choice the trip depends on: sleeping in Mestre means seeing Venice only in the hours when it is full.
- **Cannaregio** and **Castello** to be in the center at human prices and in lived-in neighborhoods.
- **Dorsoduro** for the best balance of beauty, quiet and location.
- **San Marco** costs a lot and in the evening empties of Venetians.
- **Practical note:** check **how many bridges** separate the lodging from the vaporetto stop, because suitcases are carried by hand up the steps and no service does it for you.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **Cicchetti at the counter** in the bacari, with an ombra, changing places every two or three bites.
- **Sarde in saor**, **baccalà mantecato**, **folpetti**, **liver Venetian-style**.
- **Moeche**, if you happen to be there in the brief molting season.
- **Risotto di gò**, made with a lagoon fish you don't find elsewhere.
- **To drink:** the **Venetian spritz**, more bitter; the whites of Friuli and the Veneto; Prosecco from the real area, the hills between Conegliano and Valdobbiadene.
- **General rule:** places with a fixed-price tourist menu near San Marco and Rialto are to be avoided, without exception. **Rock-bottom-priced fish in a city where fish arrives by boat doesn't exist.** You just need to move two bridges away.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Sleep in the city and not in Mestre.** Everything else follows.
- **Check the access fee and registration** before leaving: the rules have changed several times.
- **Book St. Mark's Basilica** and leave the big backpack at the hotel or the cloakroom.
- **Don't go up the St. Mark's bell tower:** San Giorgio Maggiore has the best view and no line.
- **Buy a vaporetto pass** only if you do the islands, and walk for everything else.
- **Check the tide forecasts** in autumn and winter.
- **Get around at dawn and after seven in the evening**, when the city empties.
- **Don't take a gondola without checking the official rate in force.**
- **Travel light.** Venice is a city of bridges and steps, and there's no way to avoid them.
- **Don't plan Venice in July and August** if there is an alternative.

## Chiusura personale
What stays with you from Venice doesn't happen at San Marco. It happens at Torcello, which is forty minutes by vaporetto and where almost nobody goes: you step off onto an island of grass and canals, walk ten minutes along a canal without meeting anyone, and enter a seventh-century basilica that is almost empty: no Baroque gold, no side chapels, only brick. At the far end, in the apse's semi-dome, is a Byzantine Madonna in mosaic, very tall and slender, with the child in her arms, on a completely empty gold ground. There's nothing else around: no angels, no saints, no architecture. Only gold.

And then you understand the order of things: that there was a city here of tens of thousands of inhabitants when Venice didn't yet exist, that when the lagoon silted up and malaria arrived those people moved to a sandbank a little farther on and built the thing we know, and that this mosaic was already there watching. Venice is not a city on the water: it is **a city that chose the water as a defense**, and built a commercial empire on top of it for a thousand years. Torcello is the place where you see where it started from, and where you understand that it could go back there too.
