# On the Routes of the Great Wines: Langhe, Valpolicella and Tuscany in 10 Days

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 in progress (itinerary and practical information verified; the first-person passages are written as a draft to be reread and personalized — the names of the wineries actually visited are missing, and here they matter more than elsewhere)
**Periodo del viaggio:**
**Durata:** 10 days, 9 nights
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independent, with a rental car and wineries booked
**Categoria:** Europa · Italia · vino · enogastronomia · paesaggi

---

## Apertura personale
Italy is the world's largest wine producer and has over five hundred catalogued native varieties: more than any other country, and not by a little. Three wine landscapes are UNESCO heritage. But the reason this theme works as a trip isn't the wine itself: it's that wine is the best pretext for crossing agricultural Italy instead of monumental Italy, and for talking with the people who make things instead of the ones who tell the story. In an Italian winery, unlike many French regions, the person who receives you is almost always the one who makes the wine, and that completely changes the kind of conversation. On one condition, which is also the only real rule of this itinerary: **you book, you don't just show up.**

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** **September and October**, during the harvest, when the wineries are at work and you see the process instead of the story. April-June is the second window. **In August many wineries close** or are preparing; from November to March visits are reduced.
- **Rule number one, book:** a winery is a farm business with its own work cycle, **not a shop**. Those who show up unannounced in most cases find the gate closed; those who write an email three days earlier are received by the producer, who has an hour for you. Between these two days there is an enormous difference.
- **Two or three wineries a day at most.** After the third you no longer taste anything attentively and the day becomes a collection of stamps. Better two done well, with time to walk among the rows.
- **Who drives, the point to settle before leaving:** you visit wineries in hilly areas and you drink. There are two serious solutions. Either **taste and spit**, as professionals do (wine is judged in the mouth, not in the stomach, and every winery has the dedicated container on the table), or **pay a driver**. Several areas offer dedicated shuttle services. In Italy the penalties for drunk driving are severe, and in the wine regions there are checks.
- **Costs:** tastings generally **€15-40 per person** depending on the wines and the level of hospitality, often **credited against purchases**. In the best-known areas (Barolo, Montalcino) the historic wineries cost more; in emerging areas you pay much less or nothing.
- **Buying:** at the winery the price is the production price and you talk with whoever made the wine. **Don't buy in a wine shop what you can buy at the winery.** It's worth bringing something to protect the bottles during the trip.
- **At the tasting:** no strong perfumes, which interfere. And there's no need to pretend to know: producers explain far more willingly to someone curious than to someone acting.
- **Where to eat:** the **osterie affiliated with the consortia** have the complete local wine lists at prices close to winery prices. It's the right place.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Arrival in the Langhe
We arrived in Alba and settled into an agriturismo among the hills. The Langhe are a **UNESCO heritage site** and the landscape is understood already from the road: marl hills cultivated with vines to the horizon, with villages perched on the ridges. It's worth keeping the first day light, with no wineries: you arrive, drop the bags and look at the map to see where the next days' areas are, because Barolo, Barbaresco and Roero are close as the crow flies but the hill roads stretch the times. It's also the moment to confirm the booked visits by email and to decide once and for all who drives.

We spent the afternoon in **Alba** to get our bearings, and the first dinner was Piedmontese cuisine: **raw meat hand-chopped with a knife**, vitello tonnato, tajarin with thirty egg yolks. It's among the best in Italy and accompanies the wine instead of fighting it. The centre can be walked in a couple of hours, along **via Vittorio Emanuele**, the main street, up to the **Cathedral of San Lorenzo**, with the medieval towers rising above the roofs. *If it's autumn, the **white truffle fair** is on the calendar: it's the moment when the Langhe are at their best, but also the one when prices double and it's hard to find a place.*

**In practice:** International White Truffle Fair on weekends from early October to early December (in 2026 from 10 October to 6 December), at the Cortile della Maddalena · in those weeks agriturismi and restaurants must be booked well ahead · from Alba to the Barolo villages about 20-30 minutes by car.

### Day 2 — Barolo
Two wineries booked in the **Barolo** area, one in the morning and one in the early afternoon, which is the right pace to taste with attention. Nebbiolo is among the world's great grape varieties, and here the municipalities of the denomination are eleven, with soil differences that over a few hundred meters change the wine perceptibly. It's worth choosing **one historic winery and one small one**, to see two opposite models: in the first the welcome is organized and there are the big casks, in the second the owner opens the door and you talk about the harvest. *Barolo is released after at least 38 months of ageing: asking to taste two different vintages helps you understand how much it changes with time.*

The afternoon we spent in **Barolo village** and at the **Grinzane Cavour Castle**, with the view over the hills. In Barolo the Falletti castle houses the **WiMu**, the wine museum, and in its cellars is the **Enoteca Regionale del Barolo**. The Grinzane castle, in the village where Cavour was mayor for almost twenty years, houses the Langhe Museum and the **Enoteca Regionale Piemontese Cavour**, and from its windows the geography of the ridges is easy to read. To close the day, the **La Morra belvedere** at sunset, with the whole Barolo basin below.

**In practice:** Grinzane Castle open every day from 10am, until 7pm from April to October and until 6pm the rest of the year, ticket about 8 euros · WiMu about 12 euros, hours change with the season · winery tastings about 20-40 euros, to be booked by email a few days ahead · in Barolo and La Morra park outside the centre.

### Day 3 — Barbaresco and Roero
The morning in **Barbaresco**, the other face of Nebbiolo: same grape, different soils and microclimate, a wine more ready and less austere than Barolo. Comparing the two on the same trip is the most instructive thing you can do here. The area is smaller and more compact, with three main villages, **Barbaresco**, **Neive** and **Treiso**, a few minutes from each other. One winery in the morning, then a walk in the village of Barbaresco, with its medieval tower overlooking the Tanaro, and in Neive, one of the prettiest villages in the area. *After two days of Nebbiolo the palate gets tired: it helps to alternate with a Dolcetto or a Barbera.*

The afternoon in the **Roero**, on the other bank of the Tanaro: sands instead of marls, Arneis for the whites, lower prices and fewer people. The landscape changes as soon as you cross the river, with the **rocche**, sandy gullies covered in woods, and a more fragmented viticulture mixed with orchards. You can drive up to **Guarene** or **Canale** to see the Langhe from the other side of the valley. Dinner in a consortium osteria, with the plin, the Piedmontese plin ravioli, also served "al tovagliolo" (in a napkin), without sauce.

**In practice:** Barbaresco-Neive about 10 minutes by car, Barbaresco-Canale about 25 · tastings in Barbaresco usually cost a little less than in Barolo, in the Roero often even less · in the villages many trattorias close one day a week: check and book dinner.

### Day 4 — Transfer to Valpolicella
We moved toward **Verona** and **Valpolicella**, about three and a half hours. It's best to leave after breakfast, without hurry, to arrive around lunchtime and have the afternoon free. The easiest route goes through Asti, follows the A21 motorway to Brescia and then the A4 eastward; those who want to break the journey stop for an hour on **Lake Garda**, at Sirmione or Lazise, which are almost on the way. **Valpolicella Classica** lies northwest of Verona and is made of five municipalities, Negrar, Marano, Fumane, Sant'Ambrogio and San Pietro in Cariano: small enough to cover in two days.

In the afternoon we saw the thing that makes this area different from all the others: the **fruttaie** (drying lofts). After the harvest the Amarone grapes don't go to the cellar. They are laid to dry on racks for three or four months, lose up to forty percent of their weight, and only then are vinified. You walk into rooms full of laid-out bunches, and you can smell it already from the courtyard. *If it's between October and January, the drying lofts are full and it's the right time.* Before dinner there's time for the **Pieve di San Giorgio**, a Romanesque parish church above Sant'Ambrogio, with its cloister and the view over the valley.

**In practice:** Alba-Valpolicella about 3 and a half hours, toll motorway · the visit to the fruttaia must be requested when booking, because not all wineries show it · to sleep, Fumane, Negrar or San Pietro in Cariano work well, about 20-30 minutes from Verona.

### Day 5 — Amarone, Valpolicella and Soave
One winery in **Valpolicella Classica**, the historic and small area, in the morning: it's the moment to taste the valley's range in a row, from young Valpolicella to **Ripasso** and on to Amarone, and see how the same blend of Corvina, Corvinone and Rondinella changes with drying. Then one winery in **Soave**, about forty minutes away: a white from Garganega grapes on volcanic soils, something else entirely, and very underrated. The village of **Soave** is enclosed by medieval walls, with the Scaliger castle on top of the hill and vineyards reaching right under the towers. *After an Amarone any white seems thin: if the wineries' hours allow it, do Soave first.*

Afternoon in **Verona** or among the **Venetian villas** of the area. In Verona three hours are enough for the **Arena**, Piazza delle Erbe, Piazza dei Signori and **Castelvecchio** with its crenellated bridge over the Adige; those who prefer to stay in the hills will find the Valpolicella villas, such as the sixteenth-century **Villa della Torre** in Fumane. Dinner with **risotto all'Amarone** and boiled meats, the house pairing.

**In practice:** Valpolicella-Soave about 40 minutes by car · Soave castle closed on Mondays, open morning and afternoon with hours to check · in Verona leave the car in a paid car park outside the limited traffic zone · tastings with Ripasso and Amarone cost a bit more than the local average.

### Day 6 — The Prosecco hills
A day on the **Conegliano and Valdobbiadene hills**, a UNESCO heritage site: slopes cultivated by hand where no vehicle can climb, with **Cartizze** in the steepest and priciest subzone, just over a hundred hectares above Valdobbiadene. From Valpolicella it takes about two hours, so leave early. The wine roads link Valdobbiadene and Conegliano across the hills, and the finest stretch is the hogback hills between **San Pietro di Barbozza**, **Santo Stefano** and **Col San Martino**, where the rows follow very steep slopes. Driving up above Cartizze you understand the shape of the landscape better than from any photo.

It's the area that divides opinion. The terraced landscape is spectacular, but the viticulture is very intensive and in some stretches it shows. It's worth visiting **a small producer** instead of a large winery, to understand the difference between industrial Prosecco and hillside Prosecco; asking to taste a **col fondo**, refermented in the bottle, gives an idea of how it was drunk here before the autoclave. Evening transfer toward Tuscany, or a night in the area and departure the next morning. *With almost four hours of driving ahead, whoever drives that day doesn't taste.*

**In practice:** Valpolicella-Valdobbiadene about 2 hours · Valdobbiadene-Chianti about 3 and a half to 4 hours, via Padua and Bologna · small producers often have no fixed visiting hours: write first · those who sleep in the area find agriturismi between Valdobbiadene and Conegliano, and leaving the next morning is safer.

### Day 7 — Chianti Classico
We arrived in **Chianti Classico** and settled between Florence and Siena. The road to take is the **Chiantigiana**, the SR 222, which crosses the area from north to south through **Greve in Chianti**, **Panzano** and **Castellina**: slow, all bends, but it's the right way to see vineyards, woods and farmhouses. Two wineries booked. **Sangiovese** is Italy's most widely grown grape and here it gives its historic version. The **Black Rooster** (Gallo Nero) on the bottle neck is the consortium's mark and the quickest way to tell Chianti Classico from generic Chianti, which is another denomination and another thing.

The afternoon among the walled villages (Castellina, Radda, Volpaia) and dinner with the **bistecca**: a specific cut, three fingers thick, rare, served by the kilo, which you don't order well done. **Radda** keeps the Palazzo del Podestà with coats of arms on its façade, **Volpaia** is a tiny fortified village, with the winery set inside the old houses. In **Greve** the funnel-shaped arcaded square is the heart of village life, among butchers and wine shops. *At night the Chianti roads are narrow and dark: for dinner, better a place close to where you sleep.*

**In practice:** Florence-Greve about 40 minutes, Greve-Castellina about 25 on the Chiantigiana · tastings about 20-40 euros, more at the historic estates · the bistecca is priced by weight and usually exceeds a kilo for two people: ask the price per 100 grams · on weekends dinner must be booked.

### Day 8 — Montalcino
A day in **Montalcino** for the **Brunello**: same grape as Chianti, a warmer and drier climate, minimum aging of five years. It's one of Italy's priciest and most celebrated wines, and the wineries here have a hospitality of international level. From Chianti it's a little over an hour south, via Siena. The village sits on a hill, with the fourteenth-century **Fortezza** at the top: inside there's a wine bar where Brunello and Rosso di Montalcino are poured by the glass, useful to get your bearings before the visits. *Rosso di Montalcino, same grape with much shorter ageing, is the cheap way to understand a producer's style.*

In the afternoon the **Abbey of Sant'Antimo**, Romanesque, among the olive trees: you get in free and there's hardly anyone. It lies about ten kilometres south of the village, below **Castelnuovo dell'Abate**, and late afternoon is the quietest time. *Alternative choice for those who want to spend less: **Montepulciano**, less than an hour away, with Vino Nobile. Same Sangiovese, far more human prices and a beautiful historic center.*

**In practice:** Castellina-Montalcino about 1 hour 15 minutes · Sant'Antimo church free entry, paid audio guide, hours change with the season · Brunello tastings are the most expensive of the itinerary and must always be booked · in Montalcino leave the car in the paid car parks below the centre.

### Day 9 — The Val d'Orcia
The day without wineries, to rest the palate and see the landscape around the wine: the **Val d'Orcia**, a UNESCO heritage site, with the cypresses, Pienza, San Quirico and the Sienese clay hills. It's the most photographed landscape in Italy, and it's an agricultural landscape built by people: those hills have that shape because for centuries they've been cultivated in a certain way. Leave early, while the haze still lies in the valleys, toward **San Quirico d'Orcia**, with the **Horti Leonini** garden, and just outside the village the cypresses around the **chapel of the Madonna di Vitaleta**.

In late morning **Pienza**, the ideal city that Pope Pius II had rebuilt in the fifteenth century, with the square, the cathedral and the walk along the walls overlooking the valley; it's also the town of **pecorino**. In the early afternoon a stop at **Bagno Vignoni**, where instead of a square there's a pool of thermal water. In the late afternoon, for those who still feel like it, a last winery or the **consortium enoteca**, where you taste many producers in one place without moving. *The white gravel roads are beautiful but hard on rental cars: better to leave the car and walk a stretch.*

**In practice:** Montalcino-San Quirico about 30 minutes, San Quirico-Pienza about 15 · early morning and sunset are the best hours for the light · in Pienza paid car parks outside the walls · the Bagno Vignoni pool is not for bathing: for a soak there are the village spas.

### Day 10 — Return
Last morning, last purchases directly at the winery, and return from Florence. It's worth setting the last visit or the bottle pickup early, to leave margin on the road. Bottles must be packed well: many wineries have cartons with dividers, and those who bought a lot can ask the winery to ship directly home, often easier than filling the suitcase. *On a plane wine travels only in checked luggage, never in hand luggage.*

From the Val d'Orcia to Florence it's a little under two hours, along the Via Cassia to Siena and then on the toll-free Siena-Florence highway. Those with time stop for an hour in **Siena**, in Piazza del Campo, before returning the car. In Florence the rental car is returned at the airport or near the station, without entering the limited traffic zone of the centre, where fines arrive even months later.

**In practice:** Val d'Orcia-Florence about 1 hour 45 minutes without traffic · never drive into Florence's ZTL · for shipping bottles, ask the winery about costs and times when you buy.

## What to see / what to do
- **Book the wineries three days ahead**, with an email. It's the rule on which everything depends.
- **Two or three at most per day**, or you stop tasting seriously.
- **Settle the question of who drives before leaving.**
- **One historic winery and one small one** in the same area, to see two models.
- **Barolo and Barbaresco on the same trip**, the most instructive comparison.
- **The Amarone drying lofts**, a technique you only see there.
- **A small producer in hillside Prosecco**, to understand the difference.
- **Buy at the winery, not in a wine shop.**
- **The consortium osterie**, for wine lists at winery prices.

## Possible extensions
- **Etna (+3-4 days):** the contrade on the volcano's slopes, with **ungrafted vines**: on that lava soil phylloxera never arrived, and there are pre-phylloxera vines over a hundred years old. It's like no other Italian area.
- **Collio and Colli Orientali (+3 days):** the best whites in Italy, straddling the Slovenian border, where the school of **orange wine** was born, whites macerated on the skins. Far more human prices than the Langhe at equal level.
- **Irpinia and Vulture (+4 days):** Aglianico, which stands up to Nebbiolo and Sangiovese of equal level at a third of the price, in areas almost devoid of tourism. Little organized hospitality, lots of producers in the cellar.
- **Valtellina (+2 days):** mountain Nebbiolo on terraces that drop away, with hundreds of kilometers of dry-stone walls built by hand. True heroic viticulture.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **Langhe:** agriturismi among the hills, often run by wine estates. High prices in season, especially during the truffle fair.
- **Valpolicella:** estates with hospitality in the classic area, which is small and can be toured in half an hour.
- **Chianti and Montalcino:** wine resorts and agriturismi. It's the priciest and most touristy area of the itinerary.
- **General note:** in Irpinia, Vulture and Valtellina you spend a fraction for similar quality, and that's why those extensions are worth it.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **Langhe:** tajarin, hand-chopped raw meat, vitello tonnato, plin, and white truffle in season. It's the richest cuisine of the itinerary.
- **Valpolicella:** risotto all'Amarone, boiled meats, pastissada de caval for the brave.
- **Chianti and Montalcino:** bistecca, pici, ribollita, chicken liver crostini. Simple cooking built on raw ingredients.
- **Everywhere:** the **osterie affiliated with the consortia**, which have the complete local wine list at prices close to winery prices.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Don't show up at a winery without an appointment.** It's mistake number one and costs the day.
- **Don't schedule more than two or three wineries a day.**
- **Don't put off the question of who drives.** You taste and spit, or you pay a driver.
- **Don't go in August**, when many wineries close.
- **Don't stop only at the famous denominations:** Langhe and Montalcino are pricey and crowded, while Irpinia, Vulture, Valtellina and Etna offer the same level with a fraction of the visitors.
- **Don't buy in a wine shop** what you can buy at the estate.
- **No strong perfumes** at the tasting.
- **Bring something to protect the bottles**, because they pile up.

## Chiusura personale
What you learn after a few days of wineries is that the relationship between fame and value, in Italy, is much looser than the market lets on. In the Langhe you drink very well and pay accordingly, and the level is objectively the highest in the country. But then you go down to Irpinia, walk into an estate where the owner is racking wine and stops to let you taste, and you drink an Aglianico that holds its own against reds that cost four times as much, in a place where there isn't a single tourist sign. Or you go up Etna and find vines over a hundred years old, ungrafted, because on that lava phylloxera never arrived: original roots, not grafted, something that has almost disappeared in Europe. In the end, the wine is the excuse. What remains is having spent ten days in an agricultural Italy that no monumental itinerary crosses, talking with people who do one thing and have done it for three generations.
