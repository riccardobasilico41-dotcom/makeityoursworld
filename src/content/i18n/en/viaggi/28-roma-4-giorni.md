# Rome in 4 Days (and How to Do It in 2 Without Ruining It)

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 in progress (itinerary and practical information verified; the first-person passages are written as a draft to be reread and personalized)
**Periodo del viaggio:**
**Durata:** 4 days, 3 nights — with a compressed 2-day version
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independent, all on foot and by metro
**Categoria:** Europa · Italia · Roma · archeologia · arte · gastronomia

## Apertura personale
Rome is the city where the average tourist struggles most, and almost always for one reason: they try to see all of it. You can't. The historic center is a continuous stratification in which every block has something that elsewhere would be the city's main attraction, and trying to tick off a list produces ten hours of walking a day and no memories.

Four days is the minimum to do it without rushing: one for the ancient city, one for the Vatican, one for the baroque center, one for the Rome that doesn't end up on postcards. Two days can be done (and below there's the version), but they must be done with an explicit sacrifice, decided before leaving and not improvised in the middle of the street with aching feet.

What really changes the experience isn't which monuments you choose: it's **booking everything a month ahead**. In Rome the two entries that truly sell out (the Borghese Gallery and the Colosseum's special areas) cannot be recovered on the same day by any means.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** **April, May, early June, October and November.** July and August are scorching and the center has no shade; in August many historic neighborhood businesses close for vacation. Winter is cold but manageable and not crowded, though the short days limit visits.
- **Essential reservations, in order:**
  - **Borghese Gallery:** entry **only by reservation** with **capped time slots and a visit of limited duration**. It's the only museum in Rome where without a ticket bought in advance you don't get in. It should be bought weeks ahead.
  - **Colosseum:** **named ticket with a time slot**. The basic ticket includes the **Roman Forum and Palatine** and is valid over a defined period: the package in force at the time of purchase should be checked. **The Arena (the arena floor) and underground chambers (hypogeum) are separate tickets, in very limited numbers, and vanish immediately.**
  - **Vatican Museums:** online reservation on paper isn't mandatory, but in practice it is. Without it, the outside line lasts hours.
  - **Domus Aurea:** guided visit with a hard hat, on limited days and by reservation.
- **Roma Pass:** exists in 48- and 72-hour versions and includes transport and admissions. **It does not include the Vatican Museums**, which are in another State. It only pays off if you visit enough state museums: before buying it by reflex, it's worth doing the math.
- **Vatican, dress code:** **shoulders and knees covered** in St. Peter's and the Vatican Museums, men included. The check is real and those not in compliance are stopped. A light scarf in your backpack is enough.
- **St. Peter's:** **the basilica is free to enter**, but in the middle hours the security check in the square has a very long line. **The dome is paid** and is climbed with a partial elevator or on foot: **the last steps are on foot anyway, on a narrow, sloping staircase that follows the curve of the dome**. Anyone claustrophobic really suffers there.
- **Pantheon:** **it is no longer free.** Admission has been paid since 2023. It's still one of the most extraordinary buildings in the world and should be seen.
- **The free Caravaggios:** three churches in the center have Caravaggios visible without a ticket: **San Luigi dei Francesi** (the St. Matthew cycle), **Santa Maria del Popolo** (Conversion of St. Paul and Crucifixion of St. Peter) and **Sant'Agostino** (Madonna di Loreto, or of the Pilgrims). You need **coins for the coin-operated lighting**, and you have to respect the hours, because they close at lunchtime.
- **Car:** useless and harmful. The center is a **ZTL** with electronic gates, parking is a problem and everything is done on foot. Metro lines A and B cover little: in Rome digging means finding ruins, which is why a city so large has so little metro. The two lines that exist, though, serve the right points.
- **From the airport to the centre:** from **Fiumicino (FCO)** the Leonardo Express reaches Termini in 32 minutes, every 15 minutes until about 23:30, €14, ticket from the machines or the Trenitalia app; the FL1 regional train costs about €8 and goes to Trastevere, Ostiense and Tiburtina; buses to Termini cost about €6-8 and take about an hour · white city taxi at a fixed fare of €55 within the Aurelian Walls, only from the official rank · from **Ciampino (CIA)** bus to Termini in about 40 minutes, about €6, or a taxi at a fixed fare of about €40 · Uber works with chauffeur cars, which cost more · avoid anyone offering a ride in the arrivals hall.
- **Pickpocketing:** it is real on metro line A, on bus 64 and in the areas around Termini and the main monuments. It's sleight of hand, not physical danger: backpack in front on the metro and nothing in back pockets.
- **Water:** the **nasoni**, the cast-iron fountains, give good, cold drinking water throughout the city. In summer they change the day. Always carry a bottle.
- **Food:** the Roman pastas are four and come from the same stock: **gricia** (guanciale and pecorino), **cacio e pepe**, **amatriciana** (gricia plus tomato), **carbonara** (gricia plus egg). **Guanciale, not pancetta. Pecorino, not parmigiano. No cream.** Then **supplì**, **pizza al taglio sold by weight**, **artichoke alla romana and alla giudia** (seasonal, generally **February to April**), the **quinto quarto** in Testaccio and the **maritozzo** with cream for breakfast.
- **Hours:** Roman dinner cuisine rarely starts before 8 pm. State museums have **one weekly closing day**, different from one to another: check beforehand, because fitting them together badly is the most common planning mistake.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Ancient Rome: Colosseum, Forum, Palatine
We began with the one thing in Rome you can't skip, and we began early: **first available time slot at the Colosseum**, when the light is low and the groups haven't arrived yet.

From inside, the **Colosseum** surprises those who expect it intact. Below you see **the hypogeum**, the machine of corridors, lifts and cages that lay under the arena floor, left exposed because the floor is gone. If you manage to get the ticket with **arena or underground chambers**, that's where it's worth spending your time: walking on the arena floor and looking up changes the scale of everything.

Then, with the same ticket, we moved on to the **Roman Forum and Palatine**. The classic mistake is to rush through them after the Colosseum, when you're already tired: they're the hardest part to read and the most interesting. Without a key to interpret it the Forum is a field of stones; with a guide, a serious audio guide or half an hour of preparation the night before, it becomes the administrative center of an empire.

Almost everyone dismisses the **Palatine** in twenty minutes, and yet it deserves more. It's the green, high hill where the imperial houses stood, and it's **the only point from which you look down on the Forum** with few others around.

In the afternoon we went to **San Clemente**: a twelfth-century basilica built on a fourth-century early Christian church, itself built on first-century Roman buildings with a **Mithraeum** inside. We went down three levels and a thousand years, and at the bottom we heard the water of a still-active Roman conduit flowing. It's the place that explains Rome better than any other, and it has a fraction of the visitors of the Colosseum, which is three hundred meters away.

In the evening we were in **Monti**, the neighborhood behind the Forums: very close, lived-in, without the postcard effect.

**In practice:** the Colosseum ticket is valid 24 hours and includes the Roman Forum and Palatine; buy it only on the official Archaeological Park website, days ahead · slots with the arena or underground sell out first · metro B Colosseo or Cavour · San Clemente: the basilica is free, the underground excavations need a separate ticket · in Monti, book dinner at weekends.

### Day 2 — Vatican: Museums, Sistine, St. Peter's
This is the most tiring day and should be organized accordingly.

We entered the **Vatican Museums** with a reservation, **as early as possible**. The route is kilometers long and one-way. You pass through the Pigna courtyard, the Pio-Clementino Museum with the **Laocoön** and the **Apollo Belvedere**, the Gallery of the Candelabra, the Tapestries Gallery and finally the **Gallery of Maps**: forty maps of Italy frescoed in the sixteenth century on a gilded ceiling. It's the piece almost nobody expects and that many remember more than the Sistine.

Then the **Raphael Rooms**, with the School of Athens, and finally the **Sistine Chapel**. In the Sistine **you can't take photographs and you can't talk**, and the guards repeat it out loud every few minutes, which, it must be said, doesn't help contemplation. The practical advice: **sit on the side benches**, look **at the ceiling first** (Genesis, from the prophets to the sibyls to the panel with the Creation of Adam) and only afterward at the **Last Judgment** on the end wall, painted thirty years later by a much gloomier Michelangelo. Standing in the middle of the crowd you see nothing; sitting you see everything.

On the way out we entered **St. Peter's Basilica**. Inside, scale is what counts: the letters of the frieze at the base of the dome are almost two meters tall and from the ground look normal, and it's all built to achieve this effect. Michelangelo's **Pietà** is immediately on the right, **behind glass** since 1972.

If your legs hold up, there's the **dome**: elevator to the terrace and then **the final climb on foot up a staircase that narrows and tilts** following the curve of the dome. It's not a figure of speech: at the end you walk crooked. At the top is the best view of Rome and the Vatican Gardens, which otherwise can't be seen.

For the evening, **Prati**, which is right there and is a real neighborhood, or **Trastevere**, across the river.

**In practice:** book the Vatican Museums on the official website; closed on Sundays except the last of the month (free entry and very long lines) · allow at least three or four hours for the route · metro A Ottaviano or Cipro · St. Peter's is free after the security check, with shoulders and knees covered · the dome is paid separately, a few euros more with the lift.

### Day 3 — The baroque center and the Borghese Gallery
In the morning we toured the center on foot, on the most beautiful loop in the city. **Piazza Navona** has the shape of Domitian's stadium because **a Roman stadium lies beneath it**, parts of which can be visited, and it hosts Bernini's **Fountain of the Four Rivers**. The **Pantheon** has **the largest unreinforced concrete dome ever built** and has held it up since the second century, with an oculus open to the sky through which, when it rains, it rains inside. Then the **Trevi Fountain** (access rules have changed several times in recent years: check them beforehand) and **Piazza di Spagna** with the Spanish Steps.

In between, the **free Caravaggios**: first San Luigi dei Francesi, because it's on the route between Navona and the Pantheon, then Sant'Agostino, a couple of steps away. Coins for the light.

The afternoon was for the **Borghese Gallery**, with **mandatory reservation** and **visit by slot**. It's the most beautiful museum in Rome and perhaps the best-proportioned in Italy. There are Bernini's marbles (the **Rape of Proserpina**, with fingers sinking into the thigh as if the marble were flesh, and **Apollo and Daphne**) and six Caravaggios, in a villa of human dimensions that can be toured entirely in the time of the slot. The time limit seems a restriction and is exactly the reason the visit works: it's never crowded.

Afterward we walked to **Villa Borghese** and the **Pincio at sunset**, with the terrace above Piazza del Popolo.

Evening in the **Ghetto**, with **carciofi alla giudia** if it's the right season.

**In practice:** Pantheon ticket about 5 euros, best booked at weekends · Borghese Gallery closed on Mondays, mandatory booking on the official website and two-hour slots · San Luigi dei Francesi and Sant'Agostino are free but close in the middle of the day: check the hours · carciofi alla giudia are in season roughly from February to April.

### Day 4 — The Rome that doesn't end up on postcards
This is the day that separates those who have seen Rome from those who have seen its monuments, and it should be chosen according to taste. There are three options, all full.

**Option A: Appian Way and catacombs.** **On Sundays the Appian Way is closed to traffic** and it's the right day: you rent a bike and pedal on original Roman paving stones among pines, tombs and mausoleums, with the **Mausoleum of Cecilia Metella** and the **Circus of Maxentius**. Below are the **catacombs** (San Callisto, San Sebastiano, Domitilla): kilometers of galleries dug into the tufo, visited only with a guide. A possible addition: the **Parco degli Acquedotti**, where the arches cross an open meadow on the city's outskirts.

**Option B: Ostia Antica.** With half an hour by train you reach **an entire Roman city**, with streets, baths, multi-story insulae, the firefighters' barracks and the theater. Anyone who has seen Pompeii finds Ostia less dramatic but **far more walkable and almost empty**. It's the best way to understand what an average Roman city was like.

**Option C: Testaccio, Aventine, Domus Aurea.** **Testaccio** with the market and the tradition of the **quinto quarto**, the **Non-Catholic Cemetery** with the tombs of Keats and Shelley and **Monte Testaccio**, an artificial hill made of **fifty million broken olive-oil amphorae** accumulated over three centuries: the dump of ancient Rome, turned into a landform. Then the **Aventine** with the **Orange Garden** and the famous **keyhole of the Priory of the Knights of Malta**, through which the dome of St. Peter's is framed at the end of an avenue of hedges. If you managed to book, the **Domus Aurea** closes the day: it's Nero's villa, visited with a hard hat in a permanent restoration site, and has a virtual-reality reconstruction of the octagonal hall.

Evening in **Trastevere**, but on the side streets, not the central one.

**In practice:** bus 118 goes to the Appia Antica, and bikes can be rented at the Park's information points · each catacomb has a different weekly closing day: pick them by the calendar · Ostia Antica by the Roma-Lido train from Porta San Paolo station, next to Piramide metro, on a normal city ticket · Ostia excavations closed on Mondays · Domus Aurea by reservation only and on limited days.

## The Two-Day Version
Two days in Rome work only if you accept one rule: **you give up the Vatican or you give up the rest.** They don't fit together. There are two sensible combinations.

**Two days, ancient + center version (the recommended choice for a short first visit)**
- **Day 1:** Colosseum in the first time slot, Forum and Palatine, quick lunch, **San Clemente** in the afternoon, evening in Monti.
- **Day 2:** baroque center on foot (Navona, Pantheon, Trevi, Spagna) with the Caravaggios in San Luigi dei Francesi, afternoon in the **Borghese Gallery** with the reserved slot and sunset from the Pincio.
- **What you give up:** Vatican Museums and St. Peter's. It seems heresy, but it's the choice that saves the trip: the Vatican alone is a full day, and forcing it into two days means doing everything in a rush and badly.

**Two days, Vatican + ancient version (for those who want the Vatican at all costs)**
- **Day 1:** Vatican Museums as soon as they open, Sistine, St. Peter's, a slow afternoon in Prati or along the Tiber. Nothing else: the day is done.
- **Day 2:** Colosseum, Forum and Palatine in the morning, Pantheon and Navona in the afternoon on foot.
- **What you give up:** Borghese Gallery, San Clemente, all of side-street Rome.

**In both cases:** stay **inside or right next to the historic center**, even if you pay more. Over two days, forty minutes of transport each way is a tenth of the usable time.

## What to see / what to do
- **The Colosseum in the first time slot**, with arena or underground chambers if you manage to book them.
- **The Palatine at an unhurried pace**: it's the only point from which you look down on the Forum.
- **San Clemente**, three levels and a thousand years under the same church.
- **The Gallery of Maps** at the Vatican Museums, which many remember more than the Sistine.
- **The Sistine sitting on the benches**, looking first at the ceiling and then at the Judgment.
- **The dome of St. Peter's**, with the warning about the final staircase.
- **The Borghese Gallery**, booked weeks ahead.
- **The three free Caravaggios in the churches of the center**, with coins for the light.
- **The Appian Way by bike on a Sunday**, when it's closed to traffic.
- **Monte Testaccio**, a hill made of broken amphorae.

## Possible extensions
- **Tivoli (+1 day):** **Hadrian's Villa**, Emperor Hadrian's enormous residence-city, and **Villa d'Este**, with the garden of fountains powered **entirely by gravity**, without pumps, since the sixteenth century. It's reached by train or bus in about an hour.
- **Ostia Antica (+half a day):** half an hour by train for an entire, almost empty Roman city.
- **Castelli Romani (+1 day):** the volcanic lakes of Albano and Nemi, the fraschette, the white wine of the hills. Outside the international tourist circuit.
- **Naples (+2-3 days):** a little over an hour by high-speed train. It's the natural link with the itinerary of the coast and the islands of the gulf.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **Monti** is the best choice for those who want to be in the center without being in the shop window: it's within walking distance of the Forums and remains a lived-in neighborhood.
- **Trastevere** is beautiful and noisy, and in the evening the central streets are a fair.
- **Prati** is convenient for the Vatican, orderly and quieter, with somewhat more human prices.
- **Termini area** costs less and is very convenient for trains, but it's the least pleasant part of the center.
- On short stays, location matters more than the category of the property.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **The four pastas** (gricia, cacio e pepe, amatriciana, carbonara), with **guanciale and pecorino** and never with cream.
- **Supplì** as a snack and **pizza al taglio by weight**, which is a Roman institution different from round pizza.
- **The artichoke**, alla romana or alla giudia, in its season.
- **The quinto quarto in Testaccio**, the cooking of a neighborhood born around the slaughterhouse.
- **The maritozzo with cream** for breakfast, at least once.
- **To drink:** the whites of the Castelli and Cesanese, the Lazio red almost nobody knows.
- **La Gattabuia**, in Trastevere, in an old tavern that was once a prison. *My memory: I like La Gattabuia, it's a former jail. But there are lots of places, in lots of different areas, where you eat well.*
- **General rule:** moving **three blocks** away from the main monuments changes the quality and halves the bill. Restaurants with an English menu, photos of the dishes on display and a tout at the door aren't necessarily bad, but they're definitely touristy and overpriced. *My memory: the ones with English menus or a tout are usually meh. The idea is to move a little out of the center to eat more authentically.*

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Book the Borghese Gallery and Colosseum weeks ahead**, not days.
- **Don't try to put the Vatican and ancient Rome on the same day.**
- **Check the weekly closing day** of every museum before building the schedule: they don't coincide.
- **Bring covered shoulders and knees** on Vatican day, men included.
- **Don't take a car.** ZTL, gates, no parking.
- **Real shoes.** Roman paving stones and sampietrini destroy thin soles, and in Rome you walk far more than you expect.
- **Coins in your pocket** for the lighting of the Caravaggios in the churches.
- **Water bottle and nasoni**, which in summer change the day.
- **Don't eat in monumental squares**, and be wary of the tourist menu in ten languages.
- **Watch out for pickpockets** on metro line A, bus 64 and in monumental areas.
