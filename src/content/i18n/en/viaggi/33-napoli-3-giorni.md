# Naples in 3 Days, with Pompeii and Herculaneum

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 in progress (itinerary and practical information verified; the first-person passages are written as a draft to be reread and personalized)
**Periodo del viaggio:**
**Durata:** 3 days, 2 nights — with the archaeology day outside the city
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independent, all on foot, metro and local trains
**Categoria:** Europa · Italia · Napoli · archeologia · arte · gastronomia

---

## Apertura personale
Naples is the Italian city you arrive in with the most prejudices and leave with the fewest. It has a density of art that stands up to Rome and Florence (the difference being that here the masterpieces sit inside neighborhood churches, in narrow alleys, with no barriers and no line) and it has a street life that in the rest of western Europe simply no longer exists.

The rule is the same as for Rome: you don't see all of it. Three days work if two are spent in the city and one is devoted entirely to Vesuvian archaeology, which is the reason half the world comes down here.

The thing to know before leaving is that **Naples has to be walked**, not crossed by taxi. The historic center is a Greco-Roman layout still legible to the naked eye (three parallel **decumani** cut by the cardines) and the modern city sits on top of it without ever having erased it.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** **April, May, early June, September and October.** July and August are scorching, and Pompeii at noon in August is dangerous as well as unpleasant: **there's no shade over a vast area**. Winter is mild, rainy at times and very uncrowded, excellent for the city (less so for the excavations).
- **Essential reservations:**
  - **Sansevero Chapel:** entry **only by timed reservation**, in a small chapel with limited capacity. **It sells out days ahead.** **You can't take photographs**, and the ban is enforced.
  - **Vesuvius (Gran Cono):** **access requires booking a timed ticket online**. If you go up without one you don't get in.
  - **Pompeii and Herculaneum:** online tickets with slots, strongly recommended in high season.
- **The MANN before or after Pompeii:** the **National Archaeological Museum** houses **almost the best of what was found at Pompeii and Herculaneum**: the mosaics, the detached frescoes, the silverware, the bronzes. **In the excavations, in many cases, there are copies.** Anyone who goes to Pompeii without having seen the MANN sees walls; anyone who visits it first knows what's missing and where it is.
- **Transport:** the **Line 1 metro** is very convenient and some stations (**Toledo** in particular) are real works of contemporary art, part of the "Art Stations" circuit. Then there are **four funiculars** that go up to the Vomero. **A car in the city is out of the question**: traffic, ZTL, no parking, and driving that follows unwritten rules.
- **For the excavations:** the **Circumvesuviana** connects Naples with Herculaneum, Pompeii and Sorrento. It's the means everyone uses, it's **slow, often crowded and not always on time**, and **pickpocketing on the tourist routes is a known fact**. There is a faster and pricier service dedicated to tourists, with guaranteed seats, that in high season is worth the difference: **schedules and seasonality change, so check beforehand.**
- **From the airport to the centre:** **Capodichino (NAP)** is about 7 km from the centre. · **Alibus**: about €5, ticket at the newsstand, the airport machines or in the ANM app (it costs more on board), to the Central Station in 15-20 minutes and to Molo Beverello in about 35, every 15-20 minutes until late evening · **official white taxis** from the rank outside arrivals, with a **fixed fare** set by the city, about €21-28.50 depending on the zone (about €21 to the Central Station and the old town, about €24 to Molo Beverello, about €28.50 to the Via Partenope seafront): ask for it before getting in, otherwise the meter runs · avoid anyone offering rides inside the terminal. **Napoli Centrale** then puts the city a little over an hour from Rome by high-speed train.
- **Safety, without alarmism and without denial:** the real risk is **snatching and pickpocketing**, not assault. Practical rules: **phone not in hand while walking distracted**, backpack in front on crowded transport, no watches or chains in view, caution around the station area in the evening. The rest of the historic center, in the evening, is full of people and you can walk around calmly. **Crossing the street** is the skill that really matters: you cross at a steady, predictable pace, without dashing and without hesitating, and the scooters calculate the trajectory.
- **Food:** **Neapolitan pizza** is a regulated standard: high-hydration dough, very brief cooking in a wood-fired oven at very high temperature, a tall crust and a **soft center that doesn't stand up**. It's eaten with a knife and fork, or folded "wallet-style" if it's from the street. The two classics are **marinara** (without mozzarella) and **margherita**. Then **pizza fritta**, the Sunday **ragù** cooked for hours, **genovese** (which despite the name is thoroughly Neapolitan: onions and meat cooked down for hours), **friarielli** with sausage, **sfogliatella** riccia or frolla, **babà**, **taralli 'nzogna e pepe**.
- **Coffee:** you drink it **at the bar**, short and already sweetened: if you want it unsweetened you have to say so first. The **caffè sospeso**, paid for someone who comes later, is a real custom.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — The decumani, the Veiled Christ and underground Naples
We started with the historic center, walking the decumani.

**Spaccanapoli**, the popular name of the lower decumanus, cuts the city in a straight line for kilometers, and from above it looks like a crack between the buildings. We slipped into it and headed east: **Piazza del Gesù Nuovo**, with the church whose facade is covered in **diamond-point piperno** (it was a palace, and the rusticated facade stayed), and the **Cloister of Santa Chiara**, covered in **eighteenth-century majolica** on pillars and benches, with its pergolas and the silence amid the noise.

Then the **Sansevero Chapel**, in the reserved time slot. It's a single room, and it's one of the most powerful experiences in Italian art. At the center is Giuseppe Sanmartino's **Veiled Christ**, a marble sculpture in which the veil covering the body is **carved from the same block**: you see the weave of the fabric, the folds stretching across the knees, the wounds showing through. The nineteenth-century legend of a veil "petrified" by an alchemical process of the Prince of Sansevero is fascinating and false: it's marble, worked by a man. Around it are the **Disillusion**, a wholly perforated net of marble wrapped around a figure, and, in the basement, the two **Anatomical Machines**: two skeletons with the circulatory system reconstructed in detail, an unsettling thing still debated.

We spent the afternoon on **Via dei Tribunali**, the greater decumanus, the street of pizza and nativity scenes. Here is the **Pio Monte della Misericordia**, the kind of surprise that explains Naples. It's a charitable institution active since 1602, and above the altar of its small octagonal church are **Caravaggio's Seven Works of Mercy**, painted for that spot and still in that spot. You get in for very little and there's hardly anyone.

Then we went **below**, because Naples has a city under the city: **Greek tufo quarries that became Roman cisterns, then air-raid shelters during the war**. **Napoli Sotterranea** is visited with a guide and includes **very narrow passages, walked by candlelight**, to be skipped if you're claustrophobic. Alternatively there's the **Galleria Borbonica**, a nineteenth-century tunnel commissioned by Ferdinand II as an escape route from the palace, never completed, then a wartime shelter and a depot for seized cars, which are still there.

The evening, again on Via dei Tribunali, with pizza.

**In practice:** Sansevero Chapel only with an online timed booking, ticket around €12, usually closed on Tuesdays, no photos · Santa Chiara cloister with a ticket of a few euros, Pio Monte della Misericordia about €10 · Napoli Sotterranea and Galleria Borbonica only on accompanied visits at set times, to be booked; the first has very narrow tunnels · in the evening the best-known pizzerias on Via dei Tribunali have queues: arrive early.

### Day 2 — MANN, Sanità and the hill
In the morning we were at the **National Archaeological Museum**, together with those in Rome the most important Roman archaeology museum in the world. It should be done **before Pompeii**.

Inside is the **Farnese collection**, with the **Farnese Bull**, the largest sculptural group of antiquity to have come down to us, carved from a single block, and the **Farnese Hercules**. There are the **Pompeian mosaics**, including the **Battle of Issus** with Alexander and Darius, **almost two million tesserae** on a wall of the House of the Faun, and the frescoes detached from the Vesuvian villas. And there's the **Secret Cabinet**, the room with the erotic finds that the Bourbons kept locked, opened to the public on a stable basis only in recent times: beyond the curiosity, it's a document of how different the Roman mentality was from ours.

In the afternoon we went to the **Rione Sanità**, the neighborhood that more than any other has changed its face in the last twenty years. There are the **baroque palaces with open staircases**, like the **Palazzo dello Spagnolo** with its double-ramp "hawk-wing" staircase, and above all the **Catacombs of San Gennaro**, the largest early Christian complex in southern Italy, on two levels, with frescoes from the second century. **They are run by a cooperative of young people from the neighborhood**, founded to give work in a district that had none, and the ticket funds that project: it's one of the visits where you best understand what a tourism that serves some purpose means. Not far away, for those who can handle the theme, is the **Fontanelle Cemetery**, an ossuary in a tufo quarry where the cult of the "anime pezzentelle" (poor souls) was practiced: you adopted an anonymous skull, cared for it, and in exchange asked for protection.

At the end of the day we went up to the **Vomero** by **funicular**. **Castel Sant'Elmo** has the terrace with the most complete view over the gulf, and next to it is the **Certosa di San Martino**, with the cloister, the baroque church and the **nativity scene section**. There is the **Cuciniello Nativity**, an enormous eighteenth-century nativity scene in which the Nativity is a detail amid a scene of Neapolitan life with markets, taverns and hundreds of figures.

Sunset from the viewpoint and then back down. In the evening, **Chiaia** or the **Caracciolo waterfront**, which is pedestrian, with **Castel dell'Ovo** in the background.

**In practice:** MANN closed on Tuesdays, open 9am to 7.30pm with last entry at 6.30pm, tickets also online · Catacombs of San Gennaro only on guided visits, at set departure times · Fontanelle Cemetery open about 10am-6pm, last entry 5.15pm, closed on Wednesdays · funicular to the Vomero with an ordinary city transport ticket · Castel Sant'Elmo and Certosa di San Martino have separate tickets, about €5 and about €6: the Certosa is closed on Tuesdays, Castel Sant'Elmo is open every day.

### Day 3 — Vesuvius, Herculaneum and Pompeii
This is the day outside the city, and it must be decided in advance because **doing all three is too much**.

**The recommended combination is Herculaneum in the morning and Vesuvius in the afternoon.** Or **Pompeii for the whole day**. Anyone who insists on doing Pompeii and Herculaneum on the same day ends up doing both badly.

*My memory: Pompeii is more beautiful, but Herculaneum has fewer people. Decide for yourselves which you'd enjoy more.*

**Herculaneum** is smaller, can be toured in two or three hours and is **much better preserved than Pompeii**. Pompeii was buried by lapilli and ash; Herculaneum was hit by **pyroclastic flows that sealed it under twenty meters of material**, carbonizing rather than burning. That's why **there are still beams, doors, beds, wooden shelves**, and **the upper floors of the houses are standing**, which at Pompeii almost never happens. In the **boat chambers on the ancient beach** over three hundred skeletons were found: they were the people waiting to be taken aboard.

**Vesuvius** is reached by the buses that climb from the Herculaneum square to the parking area at altitude; from there **you walk on an uphill dirt track to the rim of the Gran Cono**, with the wind and the gravel. **Online booking is mandatory.** From the rim you look into the crater, fumaroles included, and behind it the whole gulf, with towns built right up the slopes. The real vertigo comes from **the six hundred thousand inhabitants of the red zone** that you can see from up there, more than from the crater. **Closed shoes are in practice mandatory, a windbreaker even in summer.**

**Pompeii** is something else: it's **an entire city**, and you walk for hours. The priorities, so as not to scatter: the **Villa of the Mysteries** with the cycle of frescoes in Pompeian red, the **Forum** with Vesuvius at the end, the **House of the Faun**, the **Stabian Baths**, the **Lupanar**, the **Amphitheater** (the oldest known Roman stone amphitheater) and the **casts**: the voids left by bodies in the ash, filled with plaster by Giuseppe Fiorelli in the nineteenth century, which give back the exact positions in which people died. **You need a hat, water and real shoes**, because you walk on uneven paving and shade is almost absent.

Then we returned to Naples for the last evening in the city.

**In practice:** Circumvesuviana from Napoli Porta Nolana or Garibaldi, just under half an hour to Ercolano Scavi and about forty minutes to Pompei Scavi · Pompeii tickets are personal, with date and time slot, around €20, bought only on pompeiisites.org or from official resellers · Vesuvius: tickets only online at vesuviopark.vivaticket.it, around €12, no ticket office at the top · in strong wind or weather alerts the Gran Cono path closes: check the day before.

## What to see / what to do
- **The Veiled Christ** at the Sansevero Chapel, booked in good time.
- **Caravaggio's Seven Works of Mercy**, still in the place they were painted for.
- **The MANN before Pompeii**, not after.
- **The majolica cloister of Santa Chiara.**
- **The Catacombs of San Gennaro**, run by the young people of the Rione Sanità.
- **The Cuciniello Nativity** at the Certosa di San Martino.
- **The view from Castel Sant'Elmo**, the best over the gulf.
- **Herculaneum**, better preserved than Pompeii and far less crowded.
- **The rim of Vesuvius's Gran Cono**, looking at the towns below.
- **The Toledo metro station**, which is a work of art more than a stop.

## Possible extensions
- **Amalfi Coast and the islands of the gulf (+4-6 days):** it's the natural continuation, and has a dedicated itinerary.
- **Phlegraean Fields (+1 day):** **Pozzuoli** with the Flavian amphitheater and its intact underground chambers, the **Solfatara** (**access was closed after a 2017 accident: check beforehand whether it has reopened**), **submerged Baia**, visitable by glass-bottom boat or diving, and **Cumae** with the Sibyl's Cave. It's the active volcanic area almost nobody visits, and it's worth as much as Pompeii.
- **Caserta (+half a day):** the **Royal Palace** with its grand staircase, the very long park with the waterfall and the **English Garden**. The park is huge: tour it by shuttle or by bike.
- **Paestum (+1 day):** three Doric temples from the fifth century BC among the best preserved in the world, with the museum and the **Tomb of the Diver**.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **Historic center (decumani)** to be inside the real city, with the noise that follows: in Naples night noise is the rule.
- **Chiaia** for the most elegant and quiet area, near the waterfront.
- **Vomero** for the quiet, the air and the better prices, with funiculars that go down to the center in a few minutes.
- **Central Station area**: it costs less and is convenient for the excavations, but it's the least pleasant part of the city, especially in the evening.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **Pizza margherita and marinara**, with the soft center, eaten with knife and fork or folded wallet-style.
- **Pizza fritta**, which is a dish in its own right and not a fallback.
- **Ragù** and **genovese**, the two sauces of Neapolitan Sundays.
- **Friarielli and sausage**, and the **parmigiana**.
- **Sfogliatella** riccia or frolla at breakfast, **babà** afterward, **taralli 'nzogna e pepe** on the waterfront.
- **Coffee at the bar**, short, specifying beforehand if you want it unsweetened.
- **To drink:** Falanghina, Greco di Tufo, Fiano di Avellino among the whites; **Lacryma Christi del Vesuvio**, which grows on volcanic soil, and Taurasi among the reds.
- **Nennella**, for a touristy, kitschy experience. *My memory: Nennella is touristy-kitsch, but fun.*
- **General rule:** the historic pizzerias of the center have long lines and often a number system; the neighborhood ones make the same pizza without the wait. Value for money in Naples is the best in Italy, and it's hard to eat badly.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Book the Sansevero Chapel as soon as you fix your dates.**
- **Do the MANN before Pompeii**, otherwise you look at walls without knowing what was on them.
- **Don't do Pompeii and Herculaneum on the same day.**
- **Book Vesuvius online**: without a ticket you can't go up.
- **Hat, water and real shoes** for the excavations: there's no shade and the paving is uneven.
- **Watch out for pickpockets on the Circumvesuviana**, and consider the fast guaranteed-seat service in high season.
- **Don't take a car in the city.**
- **Cross the street at a steady pace**, without dashing and without stopping halfway.
- **Don't skip the Phlegraean Fields** if you have an extra day: they're the area almost nobody visits and are worth as much as the main circuit.
- **Check the access status of the Solfatara**, closed after the 2017 accident.

## Chiusura personale
From Naples, the advice I give is about Pompeii and Herculaneum: Pompeii is more beautiful, but Herculaneum has fewer people, so decide for yourselves which you'd enjoy more. And for a different kind of evening, Nennella is a bit touristy-kitsch, but fun.
