# Indonesia in 35 Days: From Sumatra to Sulawesi, Island After Island

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 in progress (itinerary and practical information complete and verified)
**Periodo del viaggio:** April to October, the dry season that covers the whole archipelago on this route. Raja Ampat, whose best window is the opposite one (October to April), is left out for exactly that reason
**Durata:** 35 days, 34 nights
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independent, with domestic flights, ferries, buses and mandatory local guides where required (Rinjani trek, orangutans at Bukit Lawang, Komodo dragons)
**Categoria:** Asia · Indonesia · vulcani · giungla · trekking · isole · cultura

## Apertura personale
Over the years, six trips to Indonesia have left me with eight different islands (and eight different Indonesias) that I never told as a single route. This itinerary condenses the best of those six trips into one thirty-five-day story, written for people who really do have a month and more to spare. It isn't the account of one trip followed start to finish. It's an honest, granular lining-up of what those six trips actually showed. You start in Sumatra, where you walk through mud looking for wild orangutans and decide whether to do it by scooter or with a driver. You cross Java through Jakarta, thousand-year-old temples at dawn and at sunset, and volcanoes that spit sulfur. You stop in Bali just long enough to understand that it isn't one island but at least six (the beach-club south, Uluwatu for surfing, Canggu for partying, Ubud as a base for the Sidemen rice paddies, the Munduk waterfalls, the sea at Amed and the dolphins of Lovina). Then you head east, to Lombok, the Gilis, Komodo, Flores and Sulawesi, where the postcard Indonesia ends and the slower, less beaten one begins. Raja Ampat isn't here, and that's not an oversight: its season is the opposite of the rest of the archipelago, and mixing it in would have meant telling it badly. It will get its own trip.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** this itinerary follows the dry season, **April to October**, which covers almost the whole route: Sumatra, Java, Bali, Lombok, Komodo, Flores and Sulawesi. **Raja Ampat is the exception**, with its best window flipped (October to April). It's the most common planning mistake for people who try to combine it with the rest of the country in a single trip, and it's the reason it doesn't appear in this itinerary: it deserves its own trip, and will get one.
- **Documents:** passport valid for at least 6 months. For tourism you need a visa on arrival: it's better to apply online for the **e-VOA** at evisa.imigrasi.go.id at least a week before (about 500,000 IDR, roughly €35, with autogate access) rather than doing it at the airport. Since October 2025 the **All Indonesia Arrival Card** is also mandatory, an electronic form to fill in before arrival. It has to be redone at every entry by air, so also on domestic connections to different areas if the flight goes through an international hub.
- **Currency:** Indonesian rupiah (IDR), roughly €1 = 17,000-18,000 IDR. You handle numbers with lots of zeros: be careful not to mix up the 10,000 and 100,000 notes.
- **Payments:** cards are accepted in Bali, in the cities of Java and in resorts. Outside those, which means a good part of this itinerary (Sumatra, inland Flores, the villages of Sulawesi), you need cash. ATMs are common in Bali and Java, rare elsewhere: arrive in Lombok, Komodo and Flores already stocked up.
- **Connectivity:** Telkomsel has the best coverage on the most remote islands; XL Axiata and Indosat are good alternatives in populated areas. The SIM has to be registered with your passport. In some parts of Flores and on the Gilis the signal is weak even with the best carrier.
- **Health:** no mandatory vaccinations, except yellow fever for those arriving from at-risk countries. Hepatitis A and B and typhoid are recommended; malaria prophylaxis for the eastern parts of the itinerary (Flores, Komodo) should be discussed with your travel doctor before leaving. Always drink bottled water, and expect "Bali belly" in the first few days.
- **Safety:** overall the country is calm for travelers. The real risks are elsewhere. Scooter accidents are the number one cause of trouble for tourists, in Bali and Lombok but also anywhere on this itinerary where you happen to rent one on your own: in Sumatra between Bukit Lawang and Lake Toba, in Yogyakarta, on day trips from Ubud. The sea currents on some beaches are treacherous, and active volcanoes (several of which this itinerary touches directly) can close airports with little notice. Watch out too for scooter rental contracts with damage disputed at return.
- **Getting around:** with an archipelago this size, domestic flights are the backbone of getting around (Garuda, Lion Air, Citilink, Wings Air), along with ferries, boats and intercity buses. For the overland stretches of Sumatra and the day trips from Ubud in Bali, the same choice always comes back: **a rented scooter**, cheap and flexible but only for people with real riding experience on often narrow, busy roads, or **a private driver by the day**, more expensive but without the risk of an accident or a breakdown far from anything (roughly 300,000-900,000 IDR per leg depending on distance, with details stage by stage in the relevant days). In Bali and Java, Grab and Gojek are very handy. On the Gilis there are no motor vehicles: only cidomo and bicycles.
- **Electricity:** 230V, type C and F sockets, the same as in Italy, so you don't need an adapter.
- **Time zone:** the itinerary crosses **two different time zones**: WIB (UTC+7) for Sumatra and Java, WITA (UTC+8) for Bali, Lombok, Komodo, Flores and Sulawesi. Keep it in mind when fitting domestic flights together, especially on the Java-to-Bali leg between Day 11 and Day 12.
- **Holidays:** Nyepi, the Balinese New Year (March, date varies), is the "day of silence": all activity in Bali stops for 24 hours, the airport closes and you can't leave your accommodation. If your travel dates fall near Nyepi, you need to know in time and adapt the itinerary for that stretch.
- **Two-part structure:** this itinerary is written as a single thirty-five-day story, but it also works split in half: details are in the note between Day 20 and Day 21.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Arrival in Medan and transfer to Bukit Lawang
**Part 1 — Western Indonesia: Sumatra, Java and Bali (20 days).** We landed at Kualanamu International (KNO), Medan's airport and the first gateway of this trip. From here you go by private car or bus to **Bukit Lawang**, a good three hours on roads that get worse as you near the park. We settled into one of the guesthouses along the **Bohorok River**, handy for an early start the next day. It's the only logistical detail that matters here, because the trek begins at dawn to avoid the worst of the heat. We spent the first evening going over the next day's trekking rules: certified guide, no food used to lure animals, no city shoes.

### Day 2 — Trekking among the orangutans of Gunung Leuser
Trekking in the jungle of **Gunung Leuser National Park**, one of the last ecosystems in the world where orangutans, tigers, rhinos and elephants still live side by side. It's not a stroll. You climb through mud clinging to roots, the humid heat takes your breath away, and after a couple of hours without seeing anything we started to wonder if we'd got the plan wrong. Then a **Sumatran orangutan** (a different species from the Bornean one, critically endangered) came down onto a branch a few meters away, looked at us for a few seconds and went back to eating. *The uncomfortable part has to be said right away: some guides lure the animals with food to guarantee a sighting, and it's a practice to refuse explicitly when you book. Only certified guides registered at the park visitor center, not the cheaper ones offered on the street.* We were back in Bukit Lawang in the afternoon.

### Day 3 — Berastagi, the Sibayak and Sinabung volcanoes
Transfer to **Berastagi**, the volcanic plateau north of Lake Toba, dominated by the cones of **Sibayak** and **Sinabung**. It's the first day you face the question that comes back all along the overland stretch of Sumatra: **rented scooter or private driver?** The roughly 120-130 km from Bukit Lawang to Berastagi take 5-8 hours depending on stops, because the mountain roads are narrow, winding and often in bad shape. On a scooter it's an experience for people who already ride confidently, not for those trying it for the first time in Indonesia. If you want zero worries, book a driver with a car, usually 800,000-900,000 IDR (€45-50) for the leg: more expensive, but without the risk of an accident or breakdown on an isolated stretch. If you like, add a shorter trek up Sibayak, where you can see the still-active crater smoking up close. For everyone the reward is the same, a soak in the **hot springs** at the foot of the volcano, which is just what you need after two days of jungle and mud. Sumatran coffee, drunk in the warungs along the road, is among the best in the country and costs a few cents.

### Day 4 — To Lake Toba and Samosir Island
Transfer to **Lake Toba**, the largest volcanic lake in the world, born from an eruption so huge it affected the Earth's climate for years. The same choice as the day before applies here: the roughly 3 hours of road to **Parapat**, the boarding port, usually cost 700,000-800,000 IDR (€40-45) with a driver, or you do it by scooter if you arrived on one. From Parapat the ferry takes you to **Samosir Island**, which sits in the middle of the crater and is as big as a province. We settled into a homestay on the island, and from here the pace of the trip slowed down on its own.

### Day 5 — Samosir and the Batak villages
The day was for **Batak** culture, the largely Christian people (an oddity in majority-Muslim Indonesia) who have lived on Samosir for centuries. We visited the **traditional villages** with their houses with the characteristic **saddle roof**, sloping and curved like the prow of a ship, decorated with carved motifs. It's an architecture of its own, not to be confused with the Toraja boat-roof houses you see at the end of the trip in Sulawesi: different cultures, both with sloping-roofed houses, but with their own shapes and symbols. Things to look for include the **stone tombs** of the ancestors, still objects of worship, and, if you happen to be there at the right time, the **Sigale-gale** dances with life-size wooden puppets. Pork also shows up on the menus here, absent elsewhere because of the Islamic context in the rest of the country. To get around the villages scattered across the island, a rented scooter is the most practical solution: the roads are quieter than on the mainland, and half a day's rental costs a few euros.

### Day 6 — Jakarta and the flight to Yogyakarta
Last day in Sumatra: from Samosir we went back to **Medan**, and from there a flight took us to **Jakarta**, the capital and the country's air hub. It's worth stopping for a full day and not just passing through, with one clear limit: **one day, no more**. Jakarta isn't the reason you come to Indonesia, but thirteen million people in the central area alone deserve at least a look. With limited time you focus on **Kota Tua**, the old Dutch colonial Batavia with its arcaded squares and museums in former administrative buildings, and on **Monas**, the national monument with the golden obelisk that dominates the center and from which, on haze-free days, you can see the city stretching to the horizon. In the late afternoon, a domestic flight to **Yogyakarta**, the cultural heart of Java: barely an hour and a half that closes out the day. The recommended base is the Prawirotaman area, quieter than Malioboro but still within walking distance of everything.

### Day 7 — Sunrise at Borobudur, sunset at Prambanan
Wake-up in the dark for **sunrise at Borobudur**, the largest Buddhist temple in the world. The ticket to climb the structure is limited and sells out well in advance: book it before leaving. It's one of the few things on this trip that lives up to its fame. In the late afternoon, **Prambanan**, the largest Hindu temple complex in Indonesia, for **sunset**: in the last minutes of sun the black stone spires light up with golden light, a counterpoint to the horizontal mass seen in the morning. One temple at dawn, one at sunset.

### Day 8 — Yogyakarta: kraton, batik and Malioboro
A day in the city: the **kraton**, the sultan's palace still in use, a traditional **batik** workshop to see wax and colors applied by hand, and an evening walk along **Malioboro**, the street of shopping and street food. It's also the most convenient day to rent a **scooter** and get around on your own. In Yogyakarta it's nice and practical and rentals cost a few euros a day, but let's be clear: you have to really know how to ride, because Indonesian traffic is dense, unpredictable and lacks the implicit rules you're used to in Europe. If you don't feel sure, there are Grab and Gojek, which cover the city just as well. Try the **gudeg**, the sweet jackfruit stew that is the city's signature dish and divides everyone who tastes it: you should try it at least once.

### Day 9 — By train to Surabaya and transfer to Cemoro Lawang
A transfer day toward **Bromo**, best broken into two legs instead of squeezed into one. First the **train** from Yogyakarta to **Surabaya Gubeng**: about 4-5 hours, with regular departures during the day (there are also night trains for those who prefer to save time). From Surabaya, a shorter train, about 2 hours, to **Probolinggo**, the station closest to Bromo. *This is where you need to be on guard.* Among independent travelers Probolinggo is known for touts and unofficial agencies who staff the exit of the station and the bus terminal and offer minivans to Cemoro Lawang at inflated prices, even triple what you'd pay on a public minivan, which usually costs around 35,000 IDR per person. The two most reported tactics are saying the public minivans have "already left" or are "full" to push you toward a pricier private option, or claiming that certain routes are "closed" when they aren't. The defense is simple: ignore anyone who approaches you even before you step off the train, look for the public minivan or an agency with verifiable reviews, and don't let yourself be rushed, because no offer vanishes in five minutes. We arrived at **Cemoro Lawang**, on the rim of the Bromo crater, in the late afternoon: a light dinner and early to bed, because the next day's alarm was in the dead of night.

### Day 10 — Sunrise at Gunung Bromo and the Madakaripura waterfall
We left around 3 a.m. for the Mount Penanjakan viewpoint, in a shared **4x4 jeep**. The same caution about price from the day before applies here. The shared jeep has a reference fare of around 700,000-900,000 IDR **per vehicle** (split among the passengers, usually 4-6 people), but at dawn, among the drivers crowding the pickup points, you may hear figures two or three times higher passed off as the "official" price: the rule is the same, never let yourself be rushed. The sunrise, with Bromo smoking in the foreground and Semeru in the background, is spectacular, but you should know beforehand: it's also a parking lot with hundreds of jeeps and rows of people holding up their phones. It's still worth it. After sunrise we went down to the foot of the volcano and walked up to the crater rim across the **Sea of Sand**. *You need heavy layers: nighttime temperatures at altitude are much colder than you'd expect at the equator.* For the rest of the day, a detour to the **Madakaripura** waterfall, about 45 minutes by car from the Bromo area; the combination is so natural that almost all tours include it in the same loop. It's the tallest waterfall in Java, hidden at the end of a narrow canyon. You walk about a kilometer along the river, then another five hundred meters wading under four smaller waterfalls, until you reach the main jet, which falls from over 200 meters. With a private driver it's the simplest way to get there, because on this stretch there's almost no public transport. In the late afternoon, transfer to **Banyuwangi** or **Licin**, the base for the next day's night trek to Kawah Ijen.

### Day 11 — The blue fire of Kawah Ijen
We set off around one in the morning, climbed in the dark with the **gas mask** (mandatory, because the sulfur fumes are seriously dangerous) and went down into the crater for the **blue fire**: in person the flames are smaller than they look in photos. It's everything else that's huge. Along the same trail pass the **sulfur miners** with baskets of eighty kilos on their shoulders, nearly barefoot, and it's that detail that stays with you more than the flames. On the way back, in full daylight, the view of the turquoise sulfuric acid lake, and the rest of the day free to rest before the leg to Bali.

### Day 12 — The ferry to Bali and arrival in Ubud
From Banyuwangi or Licin, a short transfer takes you to the port of **Ketapang**, where you board the ferry to **Gilimanuk**, on the western tip of Bali: barely a 30-60 minute crossing (a bit more counting the wait for loading and unloading), with departures every fifteen minutes, twenty-four hours a day. From Gilimanuk, a couple and a half hours of road took us to **Ubud**, which on this trip serves as the base for the whole central part of the island: for its own day and for the following days' excursions. The first impact of the best-known island in the archipelago is best met with the right expectations: just a few kilometers from the port are enough to leave the coastal traffic behind and find the flower offerings in front of the houses every morning. *One thing has to be said right away, for anyone arriving in Bali imagining only beaches: the generic south, the beach club and shopping mall package around Seminyak, is pure tourism, flat, the same anywhere in the world, and on this itinerary it doesn't deserve a dedicated day. The real Bali is elsewhere, and it's the one in the days that follow.*

### Day 13 — Ubud and the Tegalalang rice terraces
A day in Ubud: the **Tegalalang terraced rice fields**, which roll down the hillside in green waves, and, for those who want an extra physical challenge, a **sunrise trek up Gunung Batur**, leaving in the middle of the night to be at the summit before dawn. Alternatively, a slower day among the temples and craft markets of the center.

### Day 14 — Sidemen, the peace of the rice fields
Day trip to **Sidemen**, in the valley at the foot of Gunung Agung: rice terraces less touristy than Tegalalang's, almost no tour buses, and a rhythm that makes many consider it the "real" Bali of the rice paddies. There isn't a single monument to tick off, and that's the point. We walked among the terraces, passed through the **songket** weaving villages (the traditional fabric with gold and silver threads) and stopped at a warung with a view over the valley and the volcano. **How to get there:** by scooter from Ubud it's about 1h15-1h30 of road, doable for those who've already got used to Balinese traffic in the previous days; or a private driver for the round trip, usually 350,000 IDR (about €20) just for the leg, a figure that goes up if you keep him on hand for the whole day.

### Day 15 — Amed and the sea to the east
Day trip to **Amed**, on the northeastern coast, for the sea: black volcanic sand beaches, the wreck of the **USAT Liberty** reachable by snorkeling even from the beach alone, and a fishing-village rhythm rather than a resort one. *To be honest: for those who on this same trip will reach the Gilis and Komodo later, the sea here is a pleasant extra, not the high point of the trip. The other islands on this itinerary do better, with clearer water and more life underwater. Amed should be taken for what it is, a day of decompression with the sea as a backdrop, not the marine stop of the trip.* **How to get there:** about 2 hours by scooter or car from Ubud; with a private driver, usually 500,000 IDR (about €28-30) round trip.

### Day 16 — Munduk and the waterfalls, toward Lovina
Trip to **Munduk**, on the volcanic highlands of the north, land of coffee and cocoa plantations and some of the most beautiful waterfalls in Bali: **Munduk Waterfall** and the nearby **Melanting**, both reachable with a short walk in the forest. The road climbing from Ubud passes Lake Beratan and its floating temple, an excellent stop along the way. From Munduk you descend toward **Lovina**, on the north coast, where we slept: the next day the plan changes tone. **How to get there:** with a private driver the whole Ubud-Munduk-Lovina loop is the most comfortable solution, usually 500,000-600,000 IDR (€28-35) for the main leg; by scooter it's doable, but it's hours of mountain road, to be considered only with solid riding experience.

### Day 17 — Lovina and the dolphins at dawn
A very early wake-up, before dawn, for the boat trip off **Lovina** to look for the **dolphins** that come close to the coast every morning. *The same caveat as Amed applies: it's a tourist activity through and through, not a purist wildlife experience. Every morning dozens of motorboats head out into the same patch of sea, often chasing the same groups of dolphins, and it's fair to ask how spontaneous the scene still is or whether it has gotten used to human presence. That said, seeing dolphins leap in the dawn light, with the sea still flat, remains a moment worth the early alarm.* For the rest of the morning we headed back toward **Ubud**, closing the loop begun the day before.

### Day 18 — Uluwatu: surf, beaches and the Kecak dance at sunset
Transfer to the south of the island, toward **Uluwatu**: here Bali changes face again, and for the better. The coast is all sheer cliffs and beaches hidden at the bottom of staircases carved into the rock (**Padang Padang**, **Suluban**, with its bar inside the cave, **Bingin**), each with a different atmosphere. It's also one of the best-known **surf** destinations on the island, with reef-break waves better suited to people with experience than to first-timers, though the local schools cover both levels. The **Uluwatu** temple, perched on the cliff, is visited in the late afternoon, and at sunset there's the **Kecak dance**: seventy men sitting in a circle making all the music with their voices, without instruments. It's the thing that stays with you most from all of Bali, more than any rice field or beach.

### Day 19 — Canggu and Bali's nightlife
Transfer to **Canggu**, on the coast north of Seminyak. It's party Bali: beach clubs, live music venues, an international scene of surfers and digital nomads that lives as much at night as by day. It's the opposite of the peace of Sidemen or Munduk, and that's deliberate: one last evening of energy before the slower days that close this part of the trip. And it's something different from the generic, anonymous beach-club south of Seminyak: Canggu has an identity of its own, the generic south doesn't. If you have time, also stop at **Tanah Lot**, the temple on a rock surrounded by the tide, half an hour's drive away: one of the most photographed images of Bali, best at sunset.

### Day 20 — A day on Nusa Penida
A full day on **Nusa Penida**, leaving early by fast boat from Sanur. The loop covers **Kelingking Beach** (the T-Rex-shaped bay, with a steep descent to the beach), **Angel's Billabong**, **Broken Beach** and, for those who add it, the snorkeling spots with **manta rays** at Manta Point. *Better to be on the first boat in the morning: by midday Kelingking is a queue of people on a narrow path, a victim of its own success.*

**If you have three weeks, you stop here, and it's a complete trip: twenty days that run from Sumatra to Bali, from wild orangutans to the Kecak dance at sunset at Uluwatu, from the peace of Sidemen to the dolphins of Lovina, without the feeling of having skipped something.** Whoever continues leaves the best-known, most touristy Indonesia behind and enters the less beaten one: fewer facilities, more slowness, and in many ways the most memorable part of the trip. **The following two weeks, from Lombok to Sulawesi, work just as well as a trip in their own right**, for those who prefer to do only the second half or reverse the order.

### Day 21 — From Bali to Lombok, toward Senaru
**Part 2 — Eastern Indonesia: Lombok, Komodo, Flores and Sulawesi (15 days).** From here the trip changes tone: fewer facilities, more time on boats and roads, an even slower rhythm. You cross to **Lombok** by fast ferry from Padang Bai or Sanur, or by plane to Lombok International Airport (LOP). From Lombok, transfer toward **Senaru** or **Sembalun**, the two mountain villages from which the Rinjani treks start: the choice between the two depends on the ascent route. The evening is for getting ready: light backpack, thermal layer for the nights at altitude.

### Day 22 — Start of the Gunung Rinjani trek
We began the trek up **Gunung Rinjani**, the second-highest volcano in Indonesia (3,726 m). A guide is **mandatory by law**, and also needed in practice: the route is long, steep in places and at high altitude. The first day is all uphill, through forest and then high-altitude savanna, to the camp on the crater rim, where the wind gives no respite and we slept little and badly that night. *Comparing it with the other treks on the trip doesn't hold up: this is the hardest of all.*

### Day 23 — Segara Anak lake and the descent from Rinjani
Waking up above the **Segara Anak crater lake**, with its warm sulfurous waters, with the clouds below instead of above, repays all of the previous day's effort. We went down to the lake, where you can take a dip in the hot springs on the shore, then climbed back up and returned toward Senaru or Sembalun along the reverse route. Legs finished, but it's a tiredness you gladly take home.

### Day 24 — To the Gili Islands
Transfer to the **Gili Islands**, by fast boat from the port on Lombok. The pace changes abruptly from the two previous days: you arrive on islands where there are no motor vehicles, only **cidomo** (horse-drawn carts) and bicycles, and you can walk from one end to the other in under an hour.

### Day 25 — Gili Trawangan and Gili Air, without engines
A day on the Gilis: **snorkeling with sea turtles**, whom you meet starting right from the beach, without a boat or a guide. It's one of the simplest and loveliest surprises of the whole trip. **Gili Trawangan** is the most party-oriented of the three, with a nightlife above expectations; **Gili Air** strikes a better balance between services and calm, and it's good to know that before choosing where to sleep. A slow day, well earned after Rinjani.

### Day 26 — Labuan Bajo and start of the Komodo Park tour
Transfer to **Labuan Bajo**, on the western tip of Flores, the starting point of all tours to **Komodo National Park**. We boarded for a multi-day tour (the most common format is 2-4 nights, often sleeping on board), which is also the only way to be on Padar Island at dawn the next day. The first evening we went to the night market on the Labuan Bajo waterfront, where you eat the day's catch grilled on the spot, at a fraction of the price of the restaurants with a harbor view a bit further along.

### Day 27 — Padar Island at dawn and Pink Beach
Early wake-up for the climb up **Padar Island**, short and steep, onto a ridge with one of the most photographed views in Indonesia: three bays with three different sands, all under a single gaze. *It must be done very early: from eight o'clock the line on the trail is constant and the heat gets serious.* For the rest of the day, a stop at **Pink Beach**, with sand tinted pink by coral fragments, for swimming or snorkeling along the coast.

### Day 28 — Komodo dragons and snorkeling at Kanawa
You land on **Komodo island** or on **Rinca** (the latter is wilder and less crowded, and you have to ask the operator for it explicitly) for the ranger-guided trek to the **Komodo dragons**, the last great land predator remaining on Earth. They almost always stay still, and what makes the impression is the size more than the movement: an enormous animal in the shade, with the ranger holding only a forked stick. *Approaching without a guide is never an option: they are dangerous predators.* In the afternoon, snorkeling at the **Kanawa** spot, before the boat ride back to Labuan Bajo.

### Day 29 — Trans-Flores to Bajawa and the village of Bena
First leg of the **Trans-Flores**, the road that crosses the island from west to east: scenic, winding and slower than it looks on the map, so much so that two hundred kilometers can take a whole day. We arrived in **Bajawa**, the base for the traditional villages of the area, with a visit to the village of **Bena**, at the foot of the Inerie volcano: conical-roofed houses arranged in a circle around a ceremonial center, one of the most intact traditional villages on Flores.

### Day 30 — From Bajawa to Moni
Second leg on the Trans-Flores, from Bajawa to **Moni**, the village from which you set off at night for Kelimutu. It's a transfer day with free stops along the way, on a stretch where the landscape alone justifies the hours in the car, among rice terraces and villages that still live off subsistence farming.

### Day 31 — Sunrise at Kelimutu
We set off around 4 in the morning for the climb up **Kelimutu**, with its three **crater lakes** of different colors: turquoise, dark green and almost black in the most common configurations. The colors change over time due to chemical phenomena, though, and there's no way to know in advance what you'll find: that's the point of the place, not a flaw. For the rest of the day, transfer toward **Ende** or **Maumere**, on the east coast of Flores, the departure point for the flight to Sulawesi.

### Day 32 — To Makassar and Tana Toraja
Flight to **Makassar**, the main hub of **Sulawesi**, and from there a bus or domestic flight to **Rantepao**, in the heart of **Tana Toraja**. A long day of transfers, which also marks the passage to the last cultural stop of the trip: the largely Christian Toraja region, with funeral traditions unlike anything seen so far.

### Day 33 — Tana Toraja: tongkonan villages and rock tombs
A day among the **tongkonan villages**, with the traditional boat-roof houses (different, in form and culture, from the saddle-roof Batak houses seen on Samosir at the start of the trip), and the **tombs carved into the rock**, with the **tau tau**, the wooden effigies of the dead, keeping watch from high on the cliff walls. If you happen to be there at the right time and with a guide who explains the etiquette, you can witness a fragment of a **Toraja funeral ceremony**: an event that lasts days, involves hundreds of people and includes the sacrifice of buffalo in front of everyone. It's a celebration, extremely costly, for which families save for years. Attending as outsiders requires respect: a small gift, usually sugar or cigarettes, and staying where the guide indicates.

For organizing Sulawesi I recommend [Wale Adventure](https://wale-adventure.com): they're kind and good at what they do, they run private tours all over the island (Toraja, Tomohon, Tangkoko, Bunaken), and they also have a vanilla farm you can visit. In the interest of transparency: I built their website.

### Day 34 — Trekking among the mountain villages around Rantepao
A one-day trek among the **mountain villages** around Rantepao, through terraced rice fields and isolated tongkonan houses: a slower way of seeing the same culture as the day before, without the setting of an organized ceremony. In the late afternoon we headed back toward **Makassar** for the last night of the trip.

### Day 35 — Makassar and departure
Last morning in **Makassar**, with time for a visit to **Fort Rotterdam**, the Dutch fort on the waterfront, and one last meal: **coto makassar**, a beef soup traditionally eaten here for breakfast, is the right way to end. Departure the same day. Thirty-five days, seven islands, two time zones and an eighth island, Raja Ampat, deliberately left for another time.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **Sumatra:** guesthouses along the Bohorok River in Bukit Lawang, handy for an early start on the trek; homestays on Samosir Island for Lake Toba.
- **Java:** Jakarta is visited in a day, with no overnight stay, between two flights; Yogyakarta, Prawirotaman area, quieter than Malioboro; Cemoro Lawang on the rim of the Bromo crater; Banyuwangi or the guesthouses of Licin for Ijen.
- **Bali:** Ubud as a base for much of the stay (the rice fields, Sidemen, Amed and the starting point for the loop to Munduk, with a night in Lovina along the way); then Uluwatu for the cliffs and the sunset at the temple, Canggu for the last night of nightlife. Sleeping a night on Nusa Penida avoids the double crossing in a day, though in this itinerary it's organized as a day trip.
- **Lombok and the Gilis:** Senaru or Sembalun as bases for Rinjani (plus a night in a tent camp during the trek); Gili Trawangan for the nightlife or Gili Air for the balance between services and calm.
- **Komodo:** Labuan Bajo as the base, with an overnight on board during the boat tour in the park.
- **Flores:** Bajawa for the traditional villages, Moni for Kelimutu.
- **Sulawesi:** Rantepao, the natural base for all of Tana Toraja, with simple guesthouses in the center.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **Sumatra:** nasi padang, born right here: display cases with ten or fifteen ready-made dishes, you point at what you want and pay only for what you touch. Then Sumatran coffee along the road to Berastagi; pork appears only here, in the Lake Toba area, because of the Christian Batak culture.
- **Java:** in Jakarta, the nasi uduk and soto carts around Kota Tua; gudeg in Yogyakarta, nasi goreng and mie goreng from the evening carts, sate ayam, tempeh (invented right here in Java). Around Bromo and Ijen the offer is minimal and you eat at the guesthouse.
- **Bali:** babi guling (spit-roasted suckling pig) and bebek betutu (duck cooked for hours in spices) at specialized places around Ubud; in Canggu the scene of restaurants and healthy food for the international crowd; in Uluwatu the warungs perched on the cliff above the surf beaches; in the neighborhood warungs, everywhere, you eat for a few euros.
- **Lombok and the Gilis:** ayam taliwang, grilled chicken in chili sauce; on the Gilis, the Trawangan night market with fish displayed on ice, chosen and grilled on the spot.
- **Komodo:** the night market on the Labuan Bajo waterfront for fish; on the boats, the crew's food (rice, fish, vegetables), simple and surprisingly good.
- **Flores:** minimal offer outside Labuan Bajo, almost always rice, fish or chicken in village warungs; se'i, the smoked meat typical of the eastern islands, and Flores coffee roasted at home.
- **Sulawesi:** in Tana Toraja pork and buffalo appear, because the area is Christian; pa'piong, meat cooked inside a bamboo tube, has to be ordered hours ahead. In Makassar, coto makassar for breakfast.

## Practical tips
- **Certified guides, always.** For the orangutans at Bukit Lawang, for Rinjani (mandatory by law) and for the Komodo dragons: it's a matter of safety for travelers and animals, not a formality.
- **Explicitly refuse the baiting of orangutans with food** when booking the trek at Bukit Lawang.
- **Scooter or private driver, the recurring choice.** In Sumatra between Bukit Lawang, Berastagi and Lake Toba, in Yogyakarta, and in Bali for the day trips from Ubud: the scooter is cheap and flexible but requires real riding experience; the private driver costs more (usually 300,000-900,000 IDR per leg depending on distance) but eliminates the risk.
- **The minivan scams toward Bromo:** at Probolinggo, ignore the touts who declare the public vehicles "full" or "gone" and be wary of anyone offering a shared jeep well above the 700,000-900,000 IDR reference.
- **The generic south of Bali (the Seminyak area) can safely be skipped:** Uluwatu and Canggu offer the same coast with more character.
- **Gas mask for Kawah Ijen:** the sulfur fumes are seriously dangerous.
- **e-VOA and All Indonesia Arrival Card** have to be completed before every entry into the country, on the official portals.
- **Three time zones in the archipelago** (WIB, WITA, WIT): the itinerary crosses two of them, WIB and WITA, and you need to account for that when fitting domestic flights together.
- **Cash outside Bali and Java:** ATMs are rare in Lombok, Komodo, Flores and inland Sulawesi.
- **Raja Ampat is left out on purpose:** its best season (October to April) is the opposite of that of the rest of the archipelago covered here. It should be done as a trip of its own.
- **Nyepi in Bali:** if your dates coincide with the Balinese New Year, the island stops for 24 hours and the airport closes: you need to know in time.
- **Two-part structure:** this itinerary also works split in half, see the note at Day 20.

## Chiusura personale
What's left of this trip isn't a single image, which is unusual to say after thirty-five days. It's more the contrast between the two halves: the first, from Sumatra to Bali, is the Indonesia most travelers know or imagine (temples at dawn and at sunset, volcanoes, rice fields, a dance at sunset, and an island, Bali, that turns out to be made of wildly different areas rather than one postcard). The second, from Lombok to Sulawesi, is the Indonesia that gets left behind in the more general guidebooks, and it's the one that left the sharpest memories: the wind on the rim of the Rinjani crater, a Komodo dragon that doesn't move for a whole hour, the colors of Kelimutu that nobody can predict, a Toraja funeral that lasts for days. Lining up six different trips into a single story meant making choices (Raja Ampat out, Bunaken barely touched), but that was exactly the point: to show how varied a single nation made of islands can be, and how little the first three weeks resemble the last two.
