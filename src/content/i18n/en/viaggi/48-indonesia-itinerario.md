# Indonesia in 35 Days: From Sumatra to Sulawesi, Island After Island

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 in progress (itinerary and practical information complete and verified)
**Periodo del viaggio:** six trips to Indonesia over the years
**Durata:** 35 days, 34 nights
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independent, with domestic flights, ferries, buses and mandatory local guides where required (Rinjani trek, orangutans at Bukit Lawang, Komodo dragons)
**Categoria:** Asia · Indonesia · vulcani · giungla · trekking · isole · cultura

---

## Apertura personale
Over the years, six trips to Indonesia have left me with eight different islands (and eight different Indonesias) that I never told as a single route. This itinerary condenses the best of those six trips into one thirty-five-day story, written for people who really do have a month and more to spare. You start in Sumatra, where you walk through mud looking for wild orangutans and decide whether to do it by scooter or with a driver. You cross Java through Jakarta, thousand-year-old temples at dawn and at sunset, and volcanoes that spit sulfur. You stop in Bali just long enough to understand that it isn't one island but at least six (the beach-club south, Uluwatu for surfing, Canggu for partying, Ubud as a base for the Sidemen rice paddies, the Munduk waterfalls, the sea at Amed and the dolphins of Lovina). Then you head east, to Lombok, the Gilis, Komodo, Flores and Sulawesi, where the postcard Indonesia ends and the slower, less beaten one begins. Raja Ampat isn't here, and that's not an oversight: its season is the opposite of the rest of the archipelago, and mixing it in would have meant telling it badly. It will get its own trip.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** this itinerary follows the dry season, **April to October**, which covers almost the whole route: Sumatra, Java, Bali, Lombok, Komodo, Flores and Sulawesi. **Raja Ampat is the exception**, with its best window flipped (October to April). It's the most common planning mistake for people who try to combine it with the rest of the country in a single trip, and it's the reason it doesn't appear in this itinerary: it deserves its own trip, and will get one.
- **Documents:** passport valid for at least 6 months. For tourism you need a visa on arrival: it's better to apply online for the **e-VOA** at evisa.imigrasi.go.id at least a week before (about 500,000 IDR, roughly €35, with autogate access) rather than doing it at the airport. Since October 2025 the **All Indonesia Arrival Card** is also mandatory, an electronic form to fill in before arrival. It has to be redone at every entry by air, so also on domestic connections to different areas if the flight goes through an international hub.
- **Currency:** Indonesian rupiah (IDR), roughly €1 = 17,000-18,000 IDR. You handle numbers with lots of zeros: be careful not to mix up the 10,000 and 100,000 notes.
- **Payments:** cards are accepted in Bali, in the cities of Java and in resorts. Outside those, which means a good part of this itinerary (Sumatra, inland Flores, the villages of Sulawesi), you need cash. ATMs are common in Bali and Java, rare elsewhere: arrive in Lombok, Komodo and Flores already stocked up.
- **Connectivity:** Telkomsel has the best coverage on the most remote islands; XL Axiata and Indosat are good alternatives in populated areas. The SIM has to be registered with your passport. In some parts of Flores and on the Gilis the signal is weak even with the best carrier.
- **Health:** no mandatory vaccinations, except yellow fever for those arriving from at-risk countries. Hepatitis A and B and typhoid are recommended; malaria prophylaxis for the eastern parts of the itinerary (Flores, Komodo) should be discussed with your travel doctor before leaving. Always drink bottled water, and expect "Bali belly" in the first few days.
- **Safety:** overall the country is calm for travelers. The real risks are elsewhere. Scooter accidents are the number one cause of trouble for tourists, in Bali and Lombok but also anywhere on this itinerary where you happen to rent one on your own: in Sumatra between Bukit Lawang and Lake Toba, in Yogyakarta, on day trips from Ubud. The sea currents on some beaches are treacherous, and active volcanoes (several of which this itinerary touches directly) can close airports with little notice. Watch out too for scooter rental contracts with damage disputed at return.
- **Getting around:** with an archipelago this size, domestic flights are the backbone of getting around (Garuda, Lion Air, Citilink, Wings Air), along with ferries, boats and intercity buses. For the overland stretches of Sumatra and the day trips from Ubud in Bali, the same choice always comes back: **a rented scooter**, cheap and flexible but only for people with real riding experience on often narrow, busy roads, or **a private driver by the day**, more expensive but without the risk of an accident or a breakdown far from anything (roughly 300,000-900,000 IDR per leg depending on distance, with details stage by stage in the relevant days). In Bali and Java, Grab and Gojek are very handy. On the Gilis there are no motor vehicles: only cidomo and bicycles.
- **From the airport to the centre:** from **Medan Kualanamu (KNO)** you need a private car for Bukit Lawang (see Day 1); for Medan there's the Railink train, about 35-45 minutes, or a taxi from the official desk. At **Jakarta Soekarno-Hatta (CGK)** the airport train reaches BNI City and Manggarai in 45-55 minutes, about 70,000 IDR, tickets from the machines or the Access by KAI app; Blue Bird taxis from the official rank, Grab and Gojek from the pickup points. At **Bali Ngurah Rai (DPS)** there are no trains: fixed zone-fare taxis from the official desk (Ubud about 300,000-450,000 IDR, 1-2 hours) or Grab and Gojek, about 200,000-350,000 IDR. Never the drivers touting in arrivals away from the desks.
- **Electricity:** 230V, type C and F sockets, the same as in Italy, so you don't need an adapter.
- **Time zone:** the itinerary crosses **two different time zones**: WIB (UTC+7) for Sumatra and Java, WITA (UTC+8) for Bali, Lombok, Komodo, Flores and Sulawesi. Keep it in mind when fitting domestic flights together, especially on the Java-to-Bali leg between Day 11 and Day 12.
- **Holidays:** Nyepi, the Balinese New Year (March, date varies), is the "day of silence": all activity in Bali stops for 24 hours, the airport closes and you can't leave your accommodation. If your travel dates fall near Nyepi, you need to know in time and adapt the itinerary for that stretch.
- **Two-part structure:** this itinerary is written as a single thirty-five-day story, but it also works split in half: details are in the note between Day 20 and Day 21.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Arrival in Medan and transfer to Bukit Lawang
**Part 1 — Western Indonesia: Sumatra, Java and Bali (20 days).** We landed at Kualanamu International (KNO), Medan's airport and the first gateway of this trip. From here you go by private car or bus to **Bukit Lawang**, a good three hours on roads that get worse as you near the park. We settled into one of the guesthouses along the **Bohorok River**, handy for an early start the next day. It's the only logistical detail that matters here, because the trek begins at dawn to avoid the worst of the heat. We spent the first evening going over the next day's trekking rules: certified guide, no food used to lure animals, no city shoes.

It's worth choosing flights that land by early afternoon: the road to Bukit Lawang, with potholes, trucks and little lighting, is best avoided at night. There is no direct public transport from the airport, because the cheap buses leave from the terminals in Medan city, so almost everyone has the guesthouse send a car, and the guesthouse often organizes the next day's trek too. Many guesthouses can only be reached on foot along the river, with the last stretch on paths and small bridges: *a backpack is far more practical than a wheeled suitcase*. The village is small, you eat at the warungs overlooking the Bohorok and go to bed early.

**In practice:** Kualanamu airport-Bukit Lawang about 3-4 hours by private car, roughly 650,000-700,000 IDR · minibuses and public buses only from the Medan terminals, and slower · withdraw cash in Medan, because ATMs are scarce in Bukit Lawang · book accommodation and the trek with a certified guide together.

### Day 2 — Trekking among the orangutans of Gunung Leuser
Trekking in the jungle of **Gunung Leuser National Park**, one of the last ecosystems in the world where orangutans, tigers, rhinos and elephants still live side by side. It's not a stroll. You climb through mud clinging to roots, the humid heat takes your breath away, and after a couple of hours without seeing anything we started to wonder if we'd got the plan wrong. Then a **Sumatran orangutan** (a different species from the Bornean one, critically endangered) came down onto a branch a few meters away, looked at us for a few seconds and went back to eating. *The uncomfortable part has to be said right away: some guides lure the animals with food to guarantee a sighting, and it's a practice to refuse explicitly when you book. Only certified guides registered at the park visitor center, not the cheaper ones offered on the street.* We were back in Bukit Lawang in the afternoon.

**In practice:** half-day trek or 1-2 days with a night in the jungle, prices set by local guides and worth comparing beforehand · park permit usually included · long trousers, long sleeves, repellent and shoes with a good sole · many guides offer a return by tubing down the river, to ask about when booking.

### Day 3 — Berastagi, the Sibayak and Sinabung volcanoes
Transfer to **Berastagi**, the volcanic plateau north of Lake Toba, dominated by the cones of **Sibayak** and **Sinabung**. It's the first day you face the question that comes back all along the overland stretch of Sumatra: **rented scooter or private driver?** The roughly 120-130 km from Bukit Lawang to Berastagi take 5-8 hours depending on stops, because the mountain roads are narrow, winding and often in bad shape. On a scooter it's an experience for people who already ride confidently, not for those trying it for the first time in Indonesia. If you want zero worries, book a driver with a car, usually 800,000-900,000 IDR (€45-50) for the leg: more expensive, but without the risk of an accident or breakdown on an isolated stretch. If you like, add a shorter trek up Sibayak, where you can see the still-active crater smoking up close. For everyone the reward is the same, a soak in the **hot springs** at the foot of the volcano, which is just what you need after two days of jungle and mud. Sumatran coffee, drunk in the warungs along the road, is among the best in the country and costs a few cents.

**In practice:** Bukit Lawang-Berastagi 5-8 hours, driver roughly 800,000-900,000 IDR · Sibayak with a guide recommended and a dawn start, before clouds and rain roll in · hot springs at the foot of Sibayak with an entry fee of a few euros · Sinabung has been active for years: the exclusion zones must be respected.

### Day 4 — To Lake Toba and Samosir Island
Transfer to **Lake Toba**, the largest volcanic lake in the world, born from an eruption so huge it affected the Earth's climate for years. The same choice as the day before applies here: the roughly 3 hours of road to **Parapat**, the boarding port, usually cost 700,000-800,000 IDR (€40-45) with a driver, or you do it by scooter if you arrived on one. From Parapat the ferry takes you to **Samosir Island**, which sits in the middle of the crater and is almost as big as Singapore. We settled into a homestay on the island, and from here the pace of the trip slowed down on its own.

It's best to leave Berastagi in the morning, to reach Parapat with time to spare before the last afternoon ferries. Many drivers suggest a stop at the **Sipiso-piso** waterfall, which drops more than a hundred meters from the plateau at the northern end of the lake. The passenger ferry leaves from Parapat and docks at **Tuktuk**, the small Samosir peninsula where homestays and restaurants are concentrated; the car ferry instead leaves from Ajibata and arrives in Tomok. Many guesthouses have their own jetty and the boat will stop there if you ask when boarding. The afternoon is free on the lake, and it's the first real rest of the trip.

**In practice:** Berastagi-Parapat about 3 hours, driver roughly 700,000-800,000 IDR · passenger ferry Parapat-Tuktuk about 45 minutes, frequent departures by day, last time to check locally · car ferry from Ajibata for those keeping their vehicle · ATMs are scarce on the island: better to arrive with cash.

### Day 5 — Samosir and the Batak villages
The day was for **Batak** culture, the largely Christian people (an oddity in majority-Muslim Indonesia) who have lived on Samosir for centuries. We visited the **traditional villages** with their houses with the characteristic **saddle roof**, sloping and curved like the prow of a ship, decorated with carved motifs. It's an architecture of its own, not to be confused with the Toraja boat-roof houses you see at the end of the trip in Sulawesi: different cultures, both with sloping-roofed houses, but with their own shapes and symbols. Things to look for include the **stone tombs** of the ancestors, still objects of worship, and, if you happen to be there at the right time, the **Sigale-gale** dances with life-size wooden puppets. Pork also shows up on the menus here, absent elsewhere because of the Islamic context in the rest of the country. To get around the villages scattered across the island, a rented scooter is the most practical solution: the roads are quieter than on the mainland, and half a day's rental costs a few euros.

**In practice:** the most visited villages are Tomok, Ambarita with King Siallagan's stone chairs, and Simanindo · scooter rental about 70,000-100,000 IDR a day, also for half a day, quiet roads but with potholes · the Sigale-gale dances are held at set times for visitors, ask at your homestay · tomorrow is an early start: between ferry and road it takes many hours.

### Day 6 — Jakarta and the flight to Yogyakarta
Last day in Sumatra: from Samosir we went back to **Medan**, and from there a flight took us to **Jakarta**, the capital and the country's air hub. It's worth stopping for a full day and not just passing through, with one clear limit: **one day, no more**. Jakarta isn't the reason you come to Indonesia, but more than ten million people in the city proper alone deserve at least a look. With limited time you focus on **Kota Tua**, the old Dutch colonial Batavia with its arcaded squares and museums in former administrative buildings, and on **Monas**, the national monument with the golden obelisk that dominates the center and from which, on haze-free days, you can see the city stretching to the horizon. In the late afternoon, a domestic flight to **Yogyakarta**, the cultural heart of Java: barely an hour and a half that closes out the day. The recommended base is the Prawirotaman area, quieter than Malioboro but still within walking distance of everything.

**In practice:** Parapat-Kualanamu airport about 4-5 hours, leave at dawn · alternatively Silangit airport, about an hour and a half from Parapat, has direct flights to Jakarta · Kota Tua and Monas are a few minutes apart by Grab, and the climb to the top of Monas has a separate ticket and limited places · the flight to Yogyakarta lands at the new YIA airport, about an hour from the city.

### Day 7 — Sunrise at Borobudur, sunset at Prambanan
Wake-up in the dark for **sunrise at Borobudur**, the largest Buddhist temple in the world. The ticket to climb the structure is limited and sells out well in advance: book it before leaving. It's one of the few things on this trip that lives up to its fame. In the late afternoon, **Prambanan**, the largest Hindu temple complex in Indonesia, for **sunset**: in the last minutes of sun the black stone spires light up with golden light, a counterpoint to the horizontal mass seen in the morning. One temple at dawn, one at sunset.

From Yogyakarta to Borobudur it takes about an hour and a half by car, so you leave around 3:30-4. *The rules change often: today the climb onto the structure is done in time slots with a guide, with sandals provided at the entrance and a quota of about 1,200 people a day, while sunrise on top of the temple depends on special packages that aren't always available.* If there's no place, you watch the sunrise from **Punthuk Setumbu** hill, with the temple emerging from the mist, and then go in with the first slot. The middle hours are best spent in the shade, because the heat on the stones gets heavy. **Prambanan** is about 40 minutes from the city, on the opposite side; in the same park there's the **Sewu** complex, almost always empty. Better to go in a couple of hours before closing.

**In practice:** tickets on the temples' official portal, about 25 dollars per person for foreigners for each site, the Borobudur structure climb extra and with limited slots · there's a combined ticket for the two temples · a car with driver for the day is the simplest solution · Prambanan closes in the late afternoon, last entry about an hour before.

### Day 8 — Yogyakarta: kraton, batik and Malioboro
A day in the city: the **kraton**, the sultan's palace still in use, a traditional **batik** workshop to see wax and colors applied by hand, and an evening walk along **Malioboro**, the street of shopping and street food. It's also the most convenient day to rent a **scooter** and get around on your own. In Yogyakarta it's nice and practical and rentals cost a few euros a day, but let's be clear: you have to really know how to ride, because Indonesian traffic is dense, unpredictable and lacks the implicit rules you're used to in Europe. If you don't feel sure, there are Grab and Gojek, which cover the city just as well. Try the **gudeg**, the sweet jackfruit stew that is the city's signature dish and divides everyone who tastes it: you should try it at least once.

**In practice:** the kraton is open only in the morning, Tuesday to Sunday from 9 am to 2 pm (closed on Mondays), ticket about 25,000 IDR for foreigners · **Taman Sari**, the sultan's water castle, is a few minutes' walk away · scooter rental about 70,000-100,000 IDR a day, with an international licence valid for motorbikes · gudeg is eaten in the places on **Wijilan** street, the gudeg street near the kraton.

### Day 9 — By train to Surabaya and transfer to Cemoro Lawang
A transfer day toward **Bromo**, best broken into two legs instead of squeezed into one. First the **train** from Yogyakarta to **Surabaya Gubeng**: about 4-5 hours, with regular departures during the day (there are also night trains for those who prefer to save time). From Surabaya, a shorter train, about 2 hours, to **Probolinggo**, the station closest to Bromo. *This is where you need to be on guard.* Among independent travelers Probolinggo is known for touts and unofficial agencies who staff the exit of the station and the bus terminal and offer minivans to Cemoro Lawang at inflated prices, even triple what you'd pay on a public minivan, which usually costs around 35,000 IDR per person. The two most reported tactics are saying the public minivans have "already left" or are "full" to push you toward a pricier private option, or claiming that certain routes are "closed" when they aren't. The defense is simple: ignore anyone who approaches you even before you step off the train, look for the public minivan or an agency with verifiable reviews, and don't let yourself be rushed, because no offer vanishes in five minutes. We arrived at **Cemoro Lawang**, on the rim of the Bromo crater, in the late afternoon: a light dinner and early to bed, because the next day's alarm was in the dead of night.

**In practice:** Yogyakarta-Surabaya trains about 4-5 hours, tickets on the KAI railway app or website, to buy a few days ahead · Surabaya-Probolinggo about 2 hours · public minivan to Cemoro Lawang roughly 35,000 IDR, leaves when full, about an hour and a half of climbing · simple guesthouses in Cemoro Lawang, hot water is not a given.

### Day 10 — Sunrise at Gunung Bromo and the Madakaripura waterfall
We left around 3 a.m. for the Mount Penanjakan viewpoint, in a shared **4x4 jeep**. The same caution about price from the day before applies here. The shared jeep has a reference fare of around 700,000-900,000 IDR **per vehicle** (split among the passengers, usually 4-6 people), but at dawn, among the drivers crowding the pickup points, you may hear figures two or three times higher passed off as the "official" price: the rule is the same, never let yourself be rushed. The sunrise, with Bromo smoking in the foreground and Semeru in the background, is spectacular, but you should know beforehand: it's also a parking lot with hundreds of jeeps and rows of people holding up their phones. It's still worth it. After sunrise we went down to the foot of the volcano and walked up to the crater rim across the **Sea of Sand**. *You need heavy layers: nighttime temperatures at altitude are much colder than you'd expect at the equator.* For the rest of the day, a detour to the **Madakaripura** waterfall, about 45 minutes by car from the Bromo area; the combination is so natural that almost all tours include it in the same loop. It's the tallest waterfall in Java, hidden at the end of a narrow canyon. You walk about a kilometer along the river, then another five hundred meters wading under four smaller waterfalls, until you reach the main jet, which falls from over 200 meters. With a private driver it's the simplest way to get there, because on this stretch there's almost no public transport. In the late afternoon, transfer to **Banyuwangi** or **Licin**, the base for the next day's night trek to Kawah Ijen.

**In practice:** shared jeep roughly 700,000-900,000 IDR per vehicle · entry to Bromo Tengger Semeru park paid separately, more expensive at weekends · at Madakaripura you get wet: a dry bag for phone and documents · Bromo-Licin or Banyuwangi about 5-6 hours by car.

### Day 11 — The blue fire of Kawah Ijen
We set off around one in the morning, climbed in the dark with the **gas mask** (mandatory, because the sulfur fumes are seriously dangerous) and went down into the crater for the **blue fire**: in person the flames are smaller than they look in photos. It's everything else that's huge. Along the same trail pass the **sulfur miners** with baskets of eighty kilos on their shoulders, nearly barefoot, and it's that detail that stays with you more than the flames. On the way back, in full daylight, the view of the turquoise sulfuric acid lake, and the rest of the day free to rest before the leg to Bali.

The walk starts from the **Paltuding** car park: about 3 km of steady climbing to the crater rim, then the descent among the rocks to the bottom, where the blue fire burns. The gate opens around 2 a.m., so from Licin or Banyuwangi you leave around one with the driver or guide. *The blue fire can only be seen in the dark, and on some nights the wind pushes the fumes onto the descent path: the guides decide on the spot whether to go down, and their decision must be respected.* You need shoes with a good sole, a headlamp and a warm layer for the wait at the top, where it's cold before dawn.

**In practice:** tickets on the official parks platform (AyoKeTamanNasional), non-refundable · gate open roughly from 2 a.m. to noon · park closed for maintenance on the first Friday of every month and sometimes for fumes, bad weather or fires · mask and headlamp often included in tours, otherwise for rent at the car park · climb to the rim about 1h30-2 hours.

### Day 12 — The ferry to Bali and arrival in Ubud
From Banyuwangi or Licin, a short transfer takes you to the port of **Ketapang**, where you board the ferry to **Gilimanuk**, on the western tip of Bali: barely a 30-60 minute crossing (a bit more counting the wait for loading and unloading), with departures every fifteen minutes, twenty-four hours a day. From Gilimanuk, about three and a half to four hours of road took us to **Ubud**, which on this trip serves as the base for the whole central part of the island: for its own day and for the following days' excursions. The first impact of the best-known island in the archipelago is best met with the right expectations: just a few kilometers from the port are enough to leave the coastal traffic behind and find the flower offerings in front of the houses every morning. *One thing has to be said right away, for anyone arriving in Bali imagining only beaches: the generic south, the beach club and shopping mall package around Seminyak, is pure tourism, flat, the same anywhere in the world, and on this itinerary it doesn't deserve a dedicated day. The real Bali is elsewhere, and it's the one in the days that follow.*

**In practice:** Ketapang-Gilimanuk ferry around the clock, foot-passenger ticket about 10,600 IDR, bought online with ASDP's Ferizy app · Gilimanuk-Ubud about 3h30-4 hours, driver to book in advance · on entering Bali you pay the island's tourist levy, about 150,000 IDR, online or on arrival · in Ubud it's best to sleep in the lanes toward the rice fields, away from the main street.

### Day 13 — Ubud and the Tegalalang rice terraces
A day in Ubud: the **Tegalalang terraced rice fields**, which roll down the hillside in green waves, and, for those who want an extra physical challenge, a **sunrise trek up Gunung Batur**, leaving in the middle of the night to be at the summit before dawn. Alternatively, a slower day among the temples and craft markets of the center.

Batur is climbed in about two hours in the dark, with a headlamp; tours pick you up at the hotel between 2 and 3, and on clear mornings from the top you see the sun rise beside Lombok's Rinjani. On the way back, around 9, Tegalalang is on the road: stop straight away, before the mid-morning coaches. Those who choose the slow day start early at the **Ubud Market**, then the **Ubud Palace** (Puri Saren) and the **Saraswati** temple with its lotus pond. In the afternoon the **Monkey Forest**, the sacred forest of the macaques: *glasses, hats and food must be zipped in your backpack, because the monkeys steal everything*. At the end of the day the **Campuhan Ridge Walk**, a grassy ridge at the edge of town, and in the evening a Balinese dance performance.

**In practice:** Batur trek with an organized tour, transfer and guide included, roughly 70-75 dollars private, less in a group · Tegalalang about 25,000 IDR, plus small donations along the paths · Monkey Forest ticket for foreigners around 100,000-120,000 IDR · tickets for the dance at the palace on sale the same day in town.

### Day 14 — Sidemen, the peace of the rice fields
Day trip to **Sidemen**, in the valley at the foot of Gunung Agung: rice terraces less touristy than Tegalalang's, almost no tour buses, and a rhythm that makes many consider it the "real" Bali of the rice paddies. There isn't a single monument to tick off, and that's the point. We walked among the terraces, passed through the **songket** weaving villages (the traditional fabric with gold and silver threads) and stopped at a warung with a view over the valley and the volcano. **How to get there:** by scooter from Ubud it's about 1h15-1h30 of road, doable for those who've already got used to Balinese traffic in the previous days; or a private driver for the round trip, usually 350,000 IDR (about €20) just for the leg, a figure that goes up if you keep him on hand for the whole day.

**In practice:** Ubud-Sidemen about 1h15-1h30 · walks among the rice fields are free, but a local guide knows the best paths · the songket workshops can be visited with no obligation to buy · those who want to stay a night will find guesthouses overlooking the valley.

### Day 15 — Amed and the sea to the east
Day trip to **Amed**, on the northeastern coast, for the sea: black volcanic sand beaches, the wreck of the **USAT Liberty**, at **Tulamben**, about ten kilometers further north, reachable by snorkeling even from the beach alone, and a fishing-village rhythm rather than a resort one. *To be honest: for those who on this same trip will reach the Gilis and Komodo later, the sea here is a pleasant extra, not the high point of the trip. The other islands on this itinerary do better, with clearer water and more life underwater. Amed should be taken for what it is, a day of decompression with the sea as a backdrop, not the marine stop of the trip.* **How to get there:** about 2 hours by scooter or car from Ubud; with a private driver, usually 500,000 IDR (about €28-30) round trip.

It's best to leave Ubud early, for calm sea and morning light. The wreck, beached in 1942 and dragged into the water by the 1963 eruption of Agung, lies a few meters off the pebble beach of Tulamben: in the morning there's more light and fewer divers. The afternoon is spent in one of the bays of **Jemeluk** or **Lipah**, with coral close to the shore. Those who don't want to drive two hours back in the evening can spend a night in Amed.

**In practice:** Ubud-Amed about 2 hours, driver roughly 500,000 IDR return · the Liberty wreck has free access from the beach, guides and mask rental on site · snorkeling gear for rent at the warungs in the bays for a few euros · reef shoes useful on the pebbles and black sand.

### Day 16 — Munduk and the waterfalls, toward Lovina
Trip to **Munduk**, on the volcanic highlands of the north, land of coffee and cocoa plantations and some of the most beautiful waterfalls in Bali: **Munduk Waterfall** and the nearby **Melanting**, both reachable with a short walk in the forest. The road climbing from Ubud passes Lake Beratan and its floating temple, an excellent stop along the way. From Munduk you descend toward **Lovina**, on the north coast, where we slept: the next day the plan changes tone. **How to get there:** with a private driver the whole Ubud-Munduk-Lovina loop is the most comfortable solution, usually 500,000-600,000 IDR (€28-35) for the main leg; by scooter it's doable, but it's hours of mountain road, to be considered only with solid riding experience.

The best order is this: leave Ubud in the morning, stop at the **Pura Ulun Danu Beratan** temple before 10, when the groups arrive, then the scenic road above lakes **Buyan** and **Tamblingan** to Munduk for lunch. The waterfalls are visited in the early afternoon: the paths are damp and slippery, and *you need shoes with a sole, not flip-flops*. Up here it's cool and fog often rolls in during the afternoon, so it's best to head down to the coast while it's still light.

**In practice:** Ulun Danu Beratan with a foreigners' ticket of about 100,000 IDR from 1 July 2026 · Munduk waterfalls with small cash tickets at the start of the paths · Ubud-Munduk about 2h30, Munduk-Lovina about 1 hour · in Lovina it's worth booking the dolphin boat the same evening.

### Day 17 — Lovina and the dolphins at dawn
A very early wake-up, before dawn, for the boat trip off **Lovina** to look for the **dolphins** that come close to the coast every morning. *The same caveat as Amed applies: it's a tourist activity through and through, not a purist wildlife experience. Every morning dozens of motorboats head out into the same patch of sea, often chasing the same groups of dolphins, and it's fair to ask how spontaneous the scene still is or whether it has gotten used to human presence. That said, seeing dolphins leap in the dawn light, with the sea still flat, remains a moment worth the early alarm.* For the rest of the morning we headed back toward **Ubud**, closing the loop begun the day before.

The boats leave from the beach around 6 and stay out a couple of hours; you book the evening before with the boatmen or through your accommodation, and the price is agreed before boarding. Back on shore there's time for breakfast and a swim. On the road toward Ubud you can stop at the **Banjar hot springs**, about ten kilometers from Lovina, or at the **Gitgit** waterfall, on the mountain road climbing toward the center of the island.

**In practice:** shared boat trip with the price agreed beforehand, usually a few hundred thousand rupiah per boat · departure around 6, back around 8 · Lovina-Ubud about 2h30-3 hours with a driver · Banjar hot springs with an entry fee of about 45,000 IDR for foreigners, open daily from about 8 am to 6 pm.

### Day 18 — Uluwatu: surf, beaches and the Kecak dance at sunset
Transfer to the south of the island, toward **Uluwatu**: here Bali changes face again, and for the better. The coast is all sheer cliffs and beaches hidden at the bottom of staircases carved into the rock (**Padang Padang**, **Suluban**, with its bar inside the cave, **Bingin**), each with a different atmosphere. It's also one of the best-known **surf** destinations on the island, with reef-break waves better suited to people with experience than to first-timers, though the local schools cover both levels. The **Uluwatu** temple, perched on the cliff, is visited in the late afternoon, and at sunset there's the **Kecak dance**: seventy men sitting in a circle making all the music with their voices, without instruments. It's the thing that stays with you most from all of Bali, more than any rice field or beach.

**In practice:** Ubud-Uluwatu about 1h30-2 hours depending on traffic · the temple has an entry ticket, the Kecak is paid separately, about 150,000 IDR, with shows at 6 and 7 p.m. · arrive 30-45 minutes early for the best seats · keep glasses and hats safe, because the temple monkeys steal them · the beaches are reached by long stairways, better at low tide.

### Day 19 — Canggu and Bali's nightlife
Transfer to **Canggu**, on the coast north of Seminyak. It's party Bali: beach clubs, live music venues, an international scene of surfers and digital nomads that lives as much at night as by day. It's the opposite of the peace of Sidemen or Munduk, and that's deliberate: one last evening of energy before the slower days that close this part of the trip. And it's something different from the generic, anonymous beach-club south of Seminyak: Canggu has an identity of its own, the generic south doesn't. If you have time, also stop at **Tanah Lot**, the temple on a rock surrounded by the tide, half an hour's drive away: one of the most photographed images of Bali, best at sunset.

Canggu works in two halves. By day the beaches of **Batu Bolong** and **Echo Beach**, to watch the surfers or take a lesson on gentler waves than Uluwatu's, and the cafés of the Berawa area; in the late afternoon the detour to Tanah Lot, where it's worth arriving an hour before sunset because the car park and paths fill up. In the evening you go back to Canggu for dinner and the bars. *Traffic in the lanes between Canggu and Seminyak is among the worst on the island: in the evening Grab and Gojek are more practical than a scooter, and after drinking the scooter is out of the question.*

**In practice:** Uluwatu-Canggu about 1-1h30 depending on traffic · Tanah Lot 30-40 minutes from Canggu, foreigners' ticket of about 75,000 IDR · beginner surf lessons at Batu Bolong, with board rental on site · for tomorrow: Nusa Penida boat booked and a transfer to Sanur at dawn.

### Day 20 — A day on Nusa Penida
A full day on **Nusa Penida**, leaving early by fast boat from Sanur. The loop covers **Kelingking Beach** (the T-Rex-shaped bay, with a steep descent to the beach), **Angel's Billabong**, **Broken Beach** and, for those who add it, the snorkeling spots with **manta rays** at Manta Point. *Better to be on the first boat in the morning: by midday Kelingking is a queue of people on a narrow path, a victim of its own success.*

The crossing takes 30-45 minutes and the first boat leaves Sanur around 6:30; at **Banjar Nyuh** harbor a driver with a car for the day is waiting, to book in advance. The island's roads are narrow and partly broken, and between one spot and the next it can take an hour: the most sensible loop stays on the west coast, with Kelingking in the morning, Broken Beach and Angel's Billabong in the afternoon. *The descent to Kelingking is steep with minimal protection: anyone unsure on their feet stays at the viewpoint at the top, which already gives you the view.* Angel's Billabong should be seen with a calm sea, because waves can suddenly surge into the pool.

**In practice:** Sanur-Nusa Penida fast boat about 30-45 minutes, first departure around 6:30, last around 5 p.m. · car with driver on the island to book in advance · small entry or parking fees at the viewpoints · manta snorkeling on an organized trip, only with good sea conditions.

**If you have three weeks, you stop here, and it's a complete trip: twenty days that run from Sumatra to Bali, from wild orangutans to the Kecak dance at sunset at Uluwatu, from the peace of Sidemen to the dolphins of Lovina, without the feeling of having skipped something.** Whoever continues leaves the best-known, most touristy Indonesia behind and enters the less beaten one: fewer facilities, more slowness, and in many ways the most memorable part of the trip. **The following two weeks, from Lombok to Sulawesi, work just as well as a trip in their own right**, for those who prefer to do only the second half or reverse the order.

### Day 21 — From Bali to Lombok, toward Senaru
**Part 2 — Eastern Indonesia: Lombok, Komodo, Flores and Sulawesi (15 days).** From here the trip changes tone: fewer facilities, more time on boats and roads, an even slower rhythm. You cross to **Lombok** by fast ferry from Padang Bai or Sanur, or by plane to Lombok International Airport (LOP). From Lombok, transfer toward **Senaru** or **Sembalun**, the two mountain villages from which the Rinjani treks start: the choice between the two depends on the ascent route. The evening is for getting ready: light backpack, thermal layer for the nights at altitude.

The two options are only partly equivalent. The fast boat from Padang Bai reaches Lombok or the Gilis in a couple of hours, but in rough seas the crossing is hard going; the flight from Denpasar takes less than an hour but requires transfers to and from the airports. *The slow public ferry from Padang Bai to Lembar is cheap but takes four to five hours or more, with uncertain timetables.* From Lombok airport to Senaru or Sembalun it's about three hours by road, usually with the trekking agency's transfer, which in the evening also gives the briefing and checks your gear.

**In practice:** Padang Bai-Lombok or Gili fast boat about 1h30-2h30 · Denpasar-Lombok flight just under an hour · airport-Senaru about 3 hours by car · trek to book with a licensed operator, registration mandatory on the eRinjani system · Rinjani closes every year from January to the end of March, and sometimes at other times after accidents or bad weather.

### Day 22 — Start of the Gunung Rinjani trek
We began the trek up **Gunung Rinjani**, the second-highest volcano in Indonesia (3,726 m). A guide is **mandatory by law**, and also needed in practice: the route is long, steep in places and at high altitude. The first day is all uphill, through forest and then high-altitude savanna, to the camp on the crater rim, where the wind gives no respite and we slept little and badly that night. *Comparing it with the other treks on the trip doesn't hold up: this is the hardest of all.*

You set off early in the morning, after breakfast, and the climb takes up almost the whole day: from the village to the crater rim, at around 2,600-2,700 meters, there are between 1,500 and 2,000 meters of elevation gain depending on the starting point, with a stop for a lunch cooked by the porters. The porters carry tents, food and water; your own pack holds clothes, jacket, headlamp and a day's supply of water. *At the camp the temperature drops close to zero at night: check the sleeping bag supplied by the agency before leaving.* The sunset on the rim, with the lake below and the small cone of **Barujari** smoking in the middle, repays a good part of the effort.

**In practice:** 2-day/1-night or 3-day/2-night packages with guide, porters, tents and meals, prices vary widely and are worth comparing · park permit for foreigners paid per day, often included in the package · insurance required by the park's most recent rules · poles, gloves and a thermal layer essential.

### Day 23 — Segara Anak lake and the descent from Rinjani
Waking up above the **Segara Anak crater lake**, with its warm sulfurous waters, with the clouds below instead of above, repays all of the previous day's effort. We went down to the lake, where you can take a dip in the hot springs on the shore, then climbed back up and returned toward Senaru or Sembalun along the reverse route. Legs finished, but it's a tiredness you gladly take home.

The descent from the rim to the lake takes a couple of hours on rock and gravel, with a few passages where you use your hands, and the climb back up is just as demanding. The hot springs, a few minutes from the shore, are where you most gladly stop, and the warm water loosens the muscles after the cold night. *The final stretch toward the village, in the forest, is where people slip the most: dust or mud on already tired legs, to take without hurrying.* At the base a transfer usually takes you to your accommodation in Senaru or straight to the coast.

**In practice:** a long day, with many hours of walking · tips for guide and porters at the end of the trek, customary and expected · for the evening, accommodation with hot water in Senaru or a transfer to the coast already arranged · keep a spare day in the plan in case of bad weather.

### Day 24 — To the Gili Islands
Transfer to the **Gili Islands**, by fast boat from the port on Lombok. The pace changes abruptly from the two previous days: you arrive on islands where there are no motor vehicles, only **cidomo** (horse-drawn carts) and bicycles, and you can walk from one end to the other in under an hour.

From Senaru to the northwest coast it takes about an hour and a half to two hours by road to **Bangsal**, the port where the public boats to the three islands leave, with a crossing of about half an hour. *Bangsal has a certain reputation for touts offering tickets, carts and porters at inflated prices: tickets for the public boats are bought at the official counter, and you can carry your own luggage.* The public boats leave when they fill up, so it's best to arrive by late morning. On the island the pier is a few steps from the center: you reach your accommodation on foot, by bike or by cidomo, and the afternoon is already free for the first swim.

**In practice:** Senaru-Bangsal about 1h30-2 hours by car · public boat Bangsal-Gili about 30 minutes, cheap ticket at the counter · fast boats from nearby ports, more expensive but more comfortable · bring enough cash, because ATMs on the Gilis are few and not always reliable.

### Day 25 — Gili Trawangan and Gili Air, without engines
A day on the Gilis: **snorkeling with sea turtles**, whom you meet starting right from the beach, without a boat or a guide. It's one of the simplest and loveliest surprises of the whole trip. **Gili Trawangan** is the most party-oriented of the three, with a nightlife above expectations; **Gili Air** strikes a better balance between services and calm, and it's good to know that before choosing where to sleep. A slow day, well earned after Rinjani.

The turtles are seen mostly in the morning, with calmer water and better light, swimming along the reef a few meters from the shore. *Watch out for currents in the channels between the islands: they can be strong and carry you out, so stay close to the beach and don't swim across.* Those who want to see more join a snorkeling boat trip around the three islands, with stops at the turtle spots and at the underwater statues off **Gili Meno**. At sunset the west coast of Trawangan fills with people sitting on the beach, with the outline of Bali's Gunung Agung in the background.

**In practice:** bicycles for rent for about 50,000 IDR a day, the best way to go around the island · shared snorkeling trip around the three islands of about half a day, at a modest price · boats between the islands with few departures a day, check times at the pier · Gili Air or Gili Meno to sleep in peace, Trawangan for the evenings.

### Day 26 — Labuan Bajo and start of the Komodo Park tour
Transfer to **Labuan Bajo**, on the western tip of Flores, the starting point of all tours to **Komodo National Park**. We boarded for a multi-day tour (the most common format is 2-4 nights, often sleeping on board), which is also the only way to be on Padar Island at dawn the next day. The first evening we went to the night market on the Labuan Bajo waterfront, where you eat the day's catch grilled on the spot, at a fraction of the price of the restaurants with a harbor view a bit further along.

Labuan Bajo is reached by flying from Bali or Lombok, with just over an hour in the air; from the Gilis you first have to go back to Lombok or Bali, so it's a day that starts early. There are also multi-day cruises from Lombok to Labuan Bajo, slow and basic. *Choosing the boat is the most important decision of this part of the trip: options range from simple shared boats, with mattresses on deck, to private phinisi with cabins, and it's worth checking recent reviews, life jackets and whether the park ticket is included.* Before dinner there's time to climb one of the hills above the port, such as **Bukit Sylvia**, for sunset over the islands.

**In practice:** direct flights to Labuan Bajo (LBJ) from Bali, less frequent from Lombok · 2-4 night tours to book well ahead in high season, asking in writing what's included · park fees have changed several times in recent years and there are daily visitor quotas: check the current amount with the operator · the **Kampung Ujung** night market is on the waterfront.

### Day 27 — Padar Island at dawn and Pink Beach
Early wake-up for the climb up **Padar Island**, short and steep, onto a ridge with one of the most photographed views in Indonesia: three bays with three different sands, all under a single gaze. *It must be done very early: from eight o'clock the line on the trail is constant and the heat gets serious.* For the rest of the day, a stop at **Pink Beach**, with sand tinted pink by coral fragments, for swimming or snorkeling along the coast.

The boat drops anchor off Padar while it's still dark, and you go ashore around 5 to be at the top for sunrise: the climb takes about thirty minutes on steps and trail, with a steep final stretch. You come down when the sun starts to warm up and have breakfast on board. After Pink Beach many tours add **Manta Point**, where with some luck you swim above the mantas passing in the current, and **Taka Makassar**, a white sandbank in the middle of the sea. *At Manta Point the currents can be strong: get in the water only with a life jacket or next to the guide, letting yourself drift while the boat follows.*

**In practice:** Padar climb about 30-40 minutes, closed shoes and water · Padar and Pink Beach are inside the park, with tickets according to the rules of the moment · the mantas are never guaranteed · sunscreen and a T-shirt for snorkeling, because there's little shade on deck.

### Day 28 — Komodo dragons and snorkeling at Kanawa
You land on **Komodo island** or on **Rinca** (the latter is wilder and less crowded, and you have to ask the operator for it explicitly) for the ranger-guided trek to the **Komodo dragons**, the largest lizard in the world. They almost always stay still, and what makes the impression is the size more than the movement: an enormous animal in the shade, with the ranger holding only a forked stick. *Approaching without a guide is never an option: they are dangerous predators.* In the afternoon, snorkeling at the **Kanawa** spot, before the boat ride back to Labuan Bajo.

Dragon visits take place in the morning, when the animals are more active and the heat is still bearable; short trails take about an hour, long ones a few hours, always with the ranger. *Anyone on their period or with open wounds must tell the ranger before setting off, because the dragons can smell blood from far away.* **Kanawa** is an islet with a white beach and a reef starting a few meters from the shore, good for the last snorkel before port. In Labuan Bajo you sleep in town, ideally near the road out, because the next day you leave early overland.

**In practice:** entry to Komodo or Rinca only with a ranger, fees to check with the operator · closed shoes and light trousers for the trek · tips for ranger and crew at the end of the tour, appreciated · it's worth booking the car with driver for the Trans-Flores while still in Labuan Bajo.

### Day 29 — Trans-Flores to Bajawa and the village of Bena
First leg of the **Trans-Flores**, the road that crosses the island from west to east: scenic, winding and slower than it looks on the map, so much so that two hundred kilometers can take a whole day. We arrived in **Bajawa**, the base for the traditional villages of the area, with a visit to the village of **Bena**, at the foot of the Inerie volcano: conical-roofed houses arranged in a circle around a ceremonial center, one of the most intact traditional villages on Flores.

From Labuan Bajo to Bajawa it takes about nine to ten hours with stops, so you leave at dawn. Along the way a stop near **Ruteng**, in the highlands, is worthwhile for the **Cancar spiderweb rice fields**, fields divided into wedges that you look down on from a small viewpoint. You reach **Bena**, about twenty kilometers beyond Bajawa, in the afternoon: you sign the register, leave a donation and walk around the village, which is inhabited and should be visited discreetly, asking permission before photographing people. *Flores roads at night are dark and full of bends: better to reach Bajawa before sunset.*

**In practice:** car with driver for the Trans-Flores with a daily price agreed beforehand, better value if hired for several days · Labuan Bajo-Bajawa about 9-10 hours with stops · Bena with a registered donation as entry · Bajawa is cool in the evening: a sweater comes in handy.

### Day 30 — From Bajawa to Moni
Second leg on the Trans-Flores, from Bajawa to **Moni**, the village from which you set off at night for Kelimutu. It's a transfer day with free stops along the way, on a stretch where the landscape alone justifies the hours in the car, among rice terraces and villages that still live off subsistence farming.

The road descends from Bajawa toward the south coast and reaches **Ende** after about five hours with stops; from there to Moni it's about another hour and a half of climbing through the valleys. Before Ende you pass **Penggajawa** beach, covered with blue and green pebbles that locals gather and sell; in Ende you can see the house where **Sukarno** was exiled in the 1930s, before becoming the country's first president. Moni is a small, cool village with a few guesthouses along the road: you have dinner early, agree on the night departure time with the driver and go to sleep.

**In practice:** Bajawa-Moni about 6-7 hours in total with stops · Sukarno's house in Ende open during office hours, Monday to Friday from 9 am, closed at weekends, with a donation or a small ticket · simple accommodation in Moni, to book in high season · Moni has no ATMs, Ende does.

### Day 31 — Sunrise at Kelimutu
We set off around 4 in the morning for the climb up **Kelimutu**, with its three **crater lakes** of different colors: turquoise, dark green and almost black in the most common configurations. The colors change over time due to chemical phenomena, though, and there's no way to know in advance what you'll find: that's the point of the place, not a flaw. For the rest of the day, transfer toward **Ende** or **Maumere**, on the east coast of Flores, the departure point for the flight to Sulawesi.

From the village to the park car park it's about 12 km, half an hour by car or motorbike, and from the car park you walk about twenty minutes on an easy path with steps to the highest viewpoint, from which you see the lakes. Before dawn it's cold: you need a jacket and something on your head. Clouds can cover everything for the first hour and then suddenly open, so don't be in a hurry to go down. Back in Moni there's time for a swim at the **Murundao** waterfall, just outside the village, before setting off again.

**In practice:** park entry for foreigners about 150,000 IDR on weekdays and more at weekends, in cash · Moni-car park about 30 minutes · Moni-Ende about 1h30-2 hours, Moni-Maumere about 3-4 hours · flights from Ende and Maumere are few and their schedules change: book them in advance.

### Day 32 — To Makassar and Tana Toraja
Flight to **Makassar**, the main hub of **Sulawesi**, and from there a bus or domestic flight to **Rantepao**, in the heart of **Tana Toraja**. A long day of transfers, which also marks the passage to the last cultural stop of the trip: the largely Christian Toraja region, with funeral traditions unlike anything seen so far.

There are two combinations. The night bus leaves in the evening from Makassar's **Daya** terminal and reaches Rantepao early in the morning, after about nine to ten hours: the best companies have reclining seats and strong air conditioning, so you need a sweater. The flight to Toraja airport takes less than an hour but has limited schedules, and has to fit with the arrival from Flores. Those arriving by plane have the evening for a walk in Rantepao and to arrange a local guide; those taking the bus arrive at dawn the next day and should rest a few hours before going out.

**In practice:** flights from Flores to Makassar often with a stopover, to check in advance · Makassar-Rantepao night bus about 9-10 hours, roughly 200,000-350,000 IDR depending on class · Makassar-Toraja flight about 55 minutes, few flights · a local guide for Tana Toraja recommended, especially for ceremonies.

### Day 33 — Tana Toraja: tongkonan villages and rock tombs
A day among the **tongkonan villages**, with the traditional boat-roof houses (different, in form and culture, from the saddle-roof Batak houses seen on Samosir at the start of the trip), and the **tombs carved into the rock**, with the **tau tau**, the wooden effigies of the dead, keeping watch from high on the cliff walls. If you happen to be there at the right time and with a guide who explains the etiquette, you can witness a fragment of a **Toraja funeral ceremony**: an event that lasts days, involves hundreds of people and includes the sacrifice of buffalo in front of everyone. It's a celebration, extremely costly, for which families save for years. Attending as outsiders requires respect: a small gift, usually sugar or cigarettes, and staying where the guide indicates.

**In practice:** local guide to arrange in Rantepao · **Ke'te Kesu**, **Londa** and **Lemo** are the most visited sites, with small entry fees · funeral ceremonies are more frequent in the dry season, especially in summer: ask your guide · scooter or car with driver to get around the villages.

For organizing Sulawesi I recommend [Wale Adventure](https://wale-adventure.com): they're kind and good at what they do, they run private tours all over the island (Toraja, Tomohon, Tangkoko, Bunaken), and they also have a vanilla farm you can visit. In the interest of transparency: I built their website.

### Day 34 — Trekking among the mountain villages around Rantepao
A one-day trek among the **mountain villages** around Rantepao, through terraced rice fields and isolated tongkonan houses: a slower way of seeing the same culture as the day before, without the setting of an organized ceremony. In the late afternoon we headed back toward **Makassar** for the last night of the trip.

The most common route starts from the villages north of Rantepao, such as **Batutumonga**, on the slopes of Mount **Sesean**, with rice fields running down toward the valley and a view over the whole plain; from there you walk down for a few hours through fields and small villages, with a guide who knows the paths and the people who live along them. *The path runs along dykes and private land: walk where the guide says and ask before entering house courtyards.* The pace is slow, with stops for a Toraja coffee, and you get back to Rantepao in time for the journey to the coast.

**In practice:** guide to arrange in Rantepao, by the day · shoes with a sole, because after rain the dykes are muddy · return to Makassar by night bus, arriving in the morning, or by the flight from Toraja when there is one · check that the timings leave a margin for your departure flight.

### Day 35 — Makassar and departure
Last morning in **Makassar**, with time for a visit to **Fort Rotterdam**, the Dutch fort on the waterfront, and one last meal: **coto makassar**, a beef soup traditionally eaten here for breakfast, is the right way to end. Departure the same day. Thirty-five days, seven islands, two time zones and an eighth island, Raja Ampat, deliberately left for another time.

Those arriving on the night bus have the early morning for the waterfront: **Fort Rotterdam** takes an hour to walk around among bastions and colonial buildings, with the **La Galigo** museum inside. Then **Paotere** harbor, where the **pinisi** are moored, the large wooden boats of the Bugis that still link the islands. Coto makassar is eaten in the specialist places in the center, open from the morning; if you have time, also try **pisang epe**, flattened grilled banana with palm sugar syrup, sold on the **Losari** waterfront, which comes alive mostly in the evening.

**In practice:** Fort Rotterdam and the La Galigo Museum with a cheap ticket, daytime hours · Sultan Hasanuddin airport about 30-45 minutes from the center, more at rush hour · Grab and Gojek work well in the city · international flights almost all go via Jakarta, Bali, Kuala Lumpur or Singapore.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **Sumatra:** guesthouses along the Bohorok River in Bukit Lawang, handy for an early start on the trek; homestays on Samosir Island for Lake Toba.
- **Java:** Jakarta is visited in a day, with no overnight stay, between two flights; Yogyakarta, Prawirotaman area, quieter than Malioboro; Cemoro Lawang on the rim of the Bromo crater; Banyuwangi or the guesthouses of Licin for Ijen.
- **Bali:** Ubud as a base for much of the stay (the rice fields, Sidemen, Amed and the starting point for the loop to Munduk, with a night in Lovina along the way); then Uluwatu for the cliffs and the sunset at the temple, Canggu for the last night of nightlife. Sleeping a night on Nusa Penida avoids the double crossing in a day, though in this itinerary it's organized as a day trip.
- **Lombok and the Gilis:** Senaru or Sembalun as bases for Rinjani (plus a night in a tent camp during the trek); Gili Trawangan for the nightlife or Gili Air for the balance between services and calm.
- **Komodo:** Labuan Bajo as the base, with an overnight on board during the boat tour in the park.
- **Flores:** Bajawa for the traditional villages, Moni for Kelimutu.
- **Sulawesi:** Rantepao, the natural base for all of Tana Toraja, with simple guesthouses in the center.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **Sumatra:** nasi padang, born right here: display cases with ten or fifteen ready-made dishes, you point at what you want and pay only for what you touch. Then Sumatran coffee along the road to Berastagi; pork appears only here, in the Lake Toba area, because of the Christian Batak culture.
- **Java:** in Jakarta, the nasi uduk and soto carts around Kota Tua; gudeg in Yogyakarta, nasi goreng and mie goreng from the evening carts, sate ayam, tempeh (invented right here in Java). Around Bromo and Ijen the offer is minimal and you eat at the guesthouse.
- **Bali:** babi guling (spit-roasted suckling pig) and bebek betutu (duck cooked for hours in spices) at specialized places around Ubud; in Canggu the scene of restaurants and healthy food for the international crowd; in Uluwatu the warungs perched on the cliff above the surf beaches; in the neighborhood warungs, everywhere, you eat for a few euros.
- **Lombok and the Gilis:** ayam taliwang, grilled chicken in chili sauce; on the Gilis, the Trawangan night market with fish displayed on ice, chosen and grilled on the spot.
- **Komodo:** the night market on the Labuan Bajo waterfront for fish; on the boats, the crew's food (rice, fish, vegetables), simple and surprisingly good.
- **Flores:** minimal offer outside Labuan Bajo, almost always rice, fish or chicken in village warungs; se'i, the smoked meat typical of the eastern islands, and Flores coffee roasted at home.
- **Sulawesi:** in Tana Toraja pork and buffalo appear, because the area is Christian; pa'piong, meat cooked inside a bamboo tube, has to be ordered hours ahead. In Makassar, coto makassar for breakfast.
- **Durian:** you find it everywhere, and some people love it. I think it's absolutely disgusting, haha.

## Practical tips
- **Certified guides, always.** For the orangutans at Bukit Lawang, for Rinjani (mandatory by law) and for the Komodo dragons: it's a matter of safety for travelers and animals, not a formality.
- **Explicitly refuse the baiting of orangutans with food** when booking the trek at Bukit Lawang.
- **Scooter or private driver, the recurring choice.** In Sumatra between Bukit Lawang, Berastagi and Lake Toba, in Yogyakarta, and in Bali for the day trips from Ubud: the scooter is cheap and flexible but requires real riding experience; the private driver costs more (usually 300,000-900,000 IDR per leg depending on distance) but eliminates the risk.
- **The minivan scams toward Bromo:** at Probolinggo, ignore the touts who declare the public vehicles "full" or "gone" and be wary of anyone offering a shared jeep well above the 700,000-900,000 IDR reference.
- **The generic south of Bali (the Seminyak area) can safely be skipped:** Uluwatu and Canggu offer the same coast with more character.
- **Gas mask for Kawah Ijen:** the sulfur fumes are seriously dangerous.
- **e-VOA and All Indonesia Arrival Card** have to be completed before every entry into the country, on the official portals.
- **Three time zones in the archipelago** (WIB, WITA, WIT): the itinerary crosses two of them, WIB and WITA, and you need to account for that when fitting domestic flights together.
- **Cash outside Bali and Java:** ATMs are rare in Lombok, Komodo, Flores and inland Sulawesi.
- **Raja Ampat is left out on purpose:** its best season (October to April) is the opposite of that of the rest of the archipelago covered here. It should be done as a trip of its own.
- **Nyepi in Bali:** if your dates coincide with the Balinese New Year, the island stops for 24 hours and the airport closes: you need to know in time.
