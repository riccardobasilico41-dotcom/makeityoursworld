# The Aeolian Islands by Sail: Eight Days, Seven Islands

**Durata:** 8 days, 7 nights — the classic Saturday-to-Saturday week

**Categoria:** Europa · Italia · Sicilia · vela · vulcani · isole

## Apertura personale
The Aeolian Islands are seven volcanic islands in the middle of the Tyrrhenian Sea, a UNESCO site, and they're one of the few archipelagos in the Mediterranean where **how you get there completely changes what you see**. By ferry you see the ports, and the ports of the Aeolians are the least interesting part: you land at Marina Corta or Santa Marina among the hydrofoils, take a bus, go back for the last run. By boat you see the cliffs, the underwater fumaroles, the coves with no access from land, and above all **the Sciara del Fuoco of Stromboli from the sea, at night**, which is the reason this itinerary exists.

There's also a practical reason, and not a minor one: **on a boat you sleep aboard**, and accommodation in the Aeolians in season is expensive, scarce and often disappointing. The week under sail costs as much as a week of hotels, and it moves with you.

It's not a vacation for everyone. You're cramped, you depend on the weather, and the itinerary that follows **should be considered a track and not a program**: in the Aeolians the wind decides, and a serious skipper changes course without asking permission.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** **June and September**, no argument. July and August have a crowded sea, full anchorages, prices at their peak and saturated towns; **the second half of September is probably the best week of the year**, with the water still warm, few boats and low light. May and early October are excellent, with the risk of a few more days of wind.
- **Where to leave from:** the usual embarkation ports are **Milazzo**, **Portorosa** and **Capo d'Orlando** in Sicily, and **Tropea** or **Vibo Marina** in Calabria. Milazzo is the closest to the archipelago and the most convenient to reach: airport at **Catania**, then bus or train.
- **Options:** **boat with a skipper** (the right choice for those without experience: the skipper knows the anchorages and reads the local weather), **bareboat charter** for those with a sailing license and real miles, or a berth on a shared boat, the cheapest option and the least predictable as far as company. **Food for the boat is bought at the provisioning stop on the first day** and split.
- **The weather rules.** The Aeolians have strong local winds, and with a heavy sea certain anchorages become unusable: **Stromboli in particular has very few shelters**. **During the week the itinerary gets rewritten several times on the fly**, and that's normal. Anyone who needs to know for certain where they'll sleep on Thursday shouldn't book a boat.
- **Stromboli, access to the summit:** the volcano is in **permanent eruptive activity**, and after the paroxysmal events of recent years **the access rules have changed several times**. The climb is allowed **only with authorized guides** and **up to a maximum altitude set by ordinance**, modified several times: **it's best to check it at the time of departure**. The period of absolute ban and the altitude limits are not negotiable and don't depend on the guide. **The alternatives that always work: the climb to the Punta Labronzo Observatory at sunset and, above all, approaching the Sciara del Fuoco by boat after dark.**
- **Vulcano, the Gran Cratere:** **since the gas emission crisis of 2021 access to the crater and the summit area was closed and then reopened with limitations**, and **the mud pool also remained closed for a long time**. Before counting on it, check what the situation is.
- **Fresh water:** on several islands of the archipelago **water arrives by tanker ships** and is a scarce and expensive good. On the boat, tank water is used sparingly (it's the first rule you learn aboard) and showers are kept short.
- **What to bring:** **boat shoes with light soles** (black soles mark the deck), hiking shoes for the volcanoes, **a rain jacket and a heavy sweatshirt** even in September, because at night while sailing it's cold, **a soft bag and never a hard suitcase** (aboard there's nowhere to put it), sunscreen and a **headlamp** for Stromboli.
- **Seasickness:** the crossings between one island and another are short, almost always two to four hours, but between Panarea and Stromboli the Tyrrhenian can be rough. Anyone who suffers should bring remedies and take them **before** leaving, not after.
- **Food:** **pane cunzato** (bread, tomato, anchovies, capers, oregano, cheese) is the signature dish of the islands; **Salina capers** are a Slow Food presidium and have a different quality from ordinary ones; then **squid and potatoes** and **granita** for breakfast. The wine is **Malvasia delle Lipari**, dry and above all **passito**, golden and sweet, to drink at the end of a meal.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Boarding at Milazzo and transfer to Vulcano
We boarded in the afternoon: safety briefing, provisioning and settling in aboard. The bunks are narrow, and that's where you understand why the bag had to be soft.

In the late afternoon we left for **Vulcano**, about an hour and a half of crossing. It's the island closest to Sicily and the first impact is **olfactory** before visual: the smell of sulfur arrives before the island does, and it's strong.

Anchorage or mooring in the Porto di Levante area. First dinner aboard, and the first night at anchor with the boat moving.

### Day 2 — Vulcano and the Seven Wounds
Early in the morning you climb to the **Gran Cratere**, if access is allowed: about an hour on a path of volcanic sand and ash, with no shade, to be done **before eight** because after that the sun is unbearable. From the rim you look into the crater, with the **yellow sulfur fumaroles** coming out of the walls, and you see the whole archipelago in a line to the north, with Stromboli at the end. **Access depends on ordinances: better to check beforehand.**

The rest of Vulcano: the **thermal waters bubbling directly into the sea** near Porto di Levante, where you soak in an area of warm bubbles; the **black sand beach** of the Sabbie Nere; and by boat the trip to the **Grotte del Cavallo** and the **Venus Pool**, on the western side, reachable only from the sea.

In the afternoon we made the short sail to **Lipari**. The route passes in front of the **Seven Wounds**, the sheer cliffs of Vulcano's western coast.

Night in Lipari.

### Day 3 — Lipari: the castle, the museum and the pumice
**Lipari** is the largest and the most populated, the only one that looks like a real town, and almost everyone uses it only as a transport hub.

We spent the morning in the **fortified citadel**. On the rock is the **acropolis**, where excavations have uncovered a **continuous stratigraphy from the Neolithic through the Bronze Age to the Roman era**, overlapping and readable. Inside the walls is the **Aeolian Archaeological Museum**, one of the most important in the Mediterranean for prehistory: the collection of **terracotta Greek theater masks** found in the necropolis has no equivalent in the world, and it's the kind of place you enter for an hour and leave after three.

The Aeolians are explained there. These islands were inhabited and rich already five thousand years ago because they had **obsidian**, the volcanic glass from which the sharpest blades of the Neolithic were made, and they traded it all over the Mediterranean.

The afternoon by boat along the **northeast coast**, in front of the **former pumice quarries** between Canneto and Porticello. **Extraction has ceased** and on the white cliff the abandoned plants remain, with loading piers descending into the water. **The pumice seabed makes the sea a milky blue** that looks like nothing else, and we dove in there. **Access to the facilities from land is forbidden and dangerous**, but from the sea the spectacle is all there.

For those staying ashore there's the **Quattrocchi** viewpoint, from which you see the faraglioni and Vulcano together: it's the island's postcard.

### Day 4 — Salina: the green one
A short crossing to **Salina**, the anomaly of the archipelago: **it's the only island with spring water**, and that's why it's green, cultivated and full of trees while the others are barren. It has two twin extinct volcanoes, **Monte dei Porri** and **Fossa delle Felci**, which give it the two-humped profile recognizable from kilometers away.

In the morning we climbed the **Fossa delle Felci**, the highest point in the Aeolians, at **962 meters**. You walk in a forest of ferns and chestnuts, which on a volcanic island in the southern Tyrrhenian is disorienting, and from the summit you see the whole archipelago arranged in a circle.

Then **Pollara**, on the western side: a natural amphitheater that is **the half crater of a volcano collapsed into the sea**, with houses inside the basin and the cliff dropping sheer. The best-known scenes of *Il Postino* were shot here. **Access to the beach under the cliff has been limited or banned because of collapse risk**: better to find out beforehand. The sunset from Pollara, anyway, is watched from above and is the best in the Aeolians.

Salina is also the island of food: the **capers**, which here are a Slow Food presidium and are picked by hand before the flower opens, and the **Malvasia delle Lipari**, produced mostly here. The wineries are visited **by appointment**.

Night at anchor at Santa Marina or Rinella.

### Day 5 — Panarea and the islets
**Panarea** is the smallest of the major islands and the most fashionable. In August it's saturated and expensive; in June and September it goes back to being what it is, a white village without cars (**you get around on foot or with electric vehicles**), with bougainvillea and low houses.

By boat, though, you come for **the islets to the east**: **Basiluzzo**, **Dattilo**, **Lisca Bianca** and **Bottaro**, the remains of the rim of an ancient crater. Between Lisca Bianca and Bottaro **gas bubbles come out of the seabed**: you swim in water that seethes with warm bubbles, above active underwater fumaroles. It's one of the strangest things you can do swimming in the Mediterranean.

On land we walked to **Punta Milazzese**. On a sheer promontory there's a **prehistoric Bronze Age village**, with the foundations of twenty-three oval huts still visible, overlooking **Cala Junco**: a bay closed like an amphitheater by rock walls, with water of a color that seems retouched. You get there on foot in half an hour from the village, or by tender from the boat.

In the late afternoon we left for Stromboli. It's the longest crossing of the week and has to be done in daylight.

### Day 6 — Stromboli: the Sciara del Fuoco
It's the day you come to the Aeolians for.

**Stromboli is in permanent eruption**: rhythmic explosions from the summit craters, at intervals of a few minutes, **without interruption for at least two thousand years**. The ancient sources document it, calling it the lighthouse of the Mediterranean because sailors steered by its glow. The island has about five hundred inhabitants who live under it.

By day: the village, white and without cars, the jet-black sand beach and, on the other side, **Ginostra**, a hamlet of a few dozen inhabitants with **the smallest port in the world**, a slit in the rock into which one boat enters at a time, reachable only by sea and with no roads.

In the late afternoon, the climb, **within the limits and with the guides that the ordinance in force allows**. If the permitted altitude is low, the classic goal is the **Punta Labronzo Observatory**, about an hour's walk from the village, from which you look at the **Sciara del Fuoco**: the great black chute that descends from the craters to the sea, on which glowing lapilli roll down at every explosion. **Headlamp, closed shoes, windbreaker.**

But the real thing we did **after dark, from the boat**. You sail under the Sciara, switch off the deck lights and wait in silence. Every few minutes there's a dull boom, then the orange glow on the crater rim, then the glowing blocks that roll down the chute for seven hundred meters to the water and go out one after another. No filter, no barrier, no line: just the black sea, the noise and that thing that has been doing this for two thousand years, whoever watches it.

### Day 7 — Filicudi or Alicudi: the islands at the end
The crossing goes westward, to the two islands that almost nobody visits and that are the reason a boat itinerary beats the ferry: **they're poorly connected, have very few beds and in the low season are reached by only a few runs a week.**

**Filicudi:** the **Grotta del Bue Marino**, an enormous sea cave you enter by tender, named for the monk seal that lived there; the **Scoglio della Canna**, a lava obelisk over seventy meters tall rising straight from the sea; the prehistoric village of **Capo Graziano**; and the walk to **Zucco Grande**, an abandoned village reachable only on foot along a mule track, where almost no one lives anymore.

**Alicudi:** the most remote and extreme of the archipelago. **There are no roads and no cars**, and not by environmentalist choice: **there isn't a single flat surface**. The island is a volcanic cone rising out of the water, and everything is connected by **stone staircases**. Loads are carried **on the backs of mules**. A few dozen people live there, there's no nightlife, there's almost nothing. The experience is to climb the stairs to the upper part of the settlement, sit and look at the sea for an hour; anyone who doesn't understand the point shouldn't stop there.

Last night at anchor.

### Day 8 — Return and disembarkation
The sail back to the departure port is the longest crossing and generally the most beautiful: you leave the archipelago and watch it recede all together, with Stromboli disappearing last because it's the tallest.

We disembarked in the morning: cleaning the boat and goodbyes.

## If you don't go by sail
The archipelago can also be done very well by ferry, with a different logic: **you choose a base and do the other islands as day trips**, instead of moving every night.

- **Base in Lipari:** the most convenient choice, because it's the connections hub and has more lodging, more services and more runs than all the others. From there you reach Vulcano in ten minutes, Salina in half an hour, Panarea and Stromboli on excursions.
- **Base in Salina:** greener, quieter, more expensive, and well connected.
- **Stromboli must be slept on.** The day excursion from Lipari shows you the Sciara from the boat in the evening, and that's already a lot, but anyone who wants the village, Ginostra and the climb has to spend at least two nights there.
- **Filicudi and Alicudi in the low season have few runs a week**: they must be planned around the schedules, and whoever gets it wrong is stuck. This is where the boat makes the biggest difference.
- **Costs:** ferries and hydrofoils cost little; **lodging** in high season costs a lot, and seven nights ashore in August cost about as much as a week on a shared boat.

## What to see / what to do
- **The Sciara del Fuoco from the sea, at night.** It's the thing.
- **The fumaroles of the Gran Cratere of Vulcano**, if access is open.
- **The Aeolian Archaeological Museum** in Lipari, with the Greek theater masks.
- **The white sea under the pumice quarries**, from the sea side.
- **The Fossa delle Felci on Salina**, in a chestnut forest above the Tyrrhenian.
- **The sunset over Pollara**, inside the half crater.
- **The gas bubbles between Lisca Bianca and Bottaro**, swimming above the fumaroles.
- **Cala Junco and the prehistoric village of Punta Milazzese** on Panarea.
- **Ginostra**, with the smallest port in the world.
- **Alicudi**, where there are no roads and loads are carried on the backs of mules.

## Possible extensions
- **Eastern Sicily (+10 days):** you disembark at Milazzo and continue with the itinerary of Etna, Syracuse and the Val di Noto.
- **Western Sicily and the Egadi (+5-7 days):** the other side, with Palermo, Segesta and the islands of Favignana and Marettimo.
- **Tyrrhenian coast (+2 days):** Cefalù, Tindari and the Alcantara Gorges, convenient at embarkation or disembarkation.
- **Tropea and the Costa degli Dei (+2-3 days):** if you embark from the Calabrian side.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **Aboard**, for the whole week: it's the point of this formula.
- **One night ashore in Milazzo or nearby** before boarding, if the flight arrives in the evening: boarding is the afternoon of the next day and rushing isn't worth it.
- **In the ferry version:** Lipari as a base, with at least two nights in Stromboli for those who care about the climb.
- **Good to know:** beds on Filicudi and Alicudi are very few and in high season they sell out well in advance.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **Aboard**, almost every evening, with the provisions bought on the first day.
- **Pane cunzato** at stops ashore: it's the dish of the islands and is eaten standing.
- **Squid and potatoes**, and blue fish.
- **Salina capers** on everything, and the Aeolian **caponata**.
- **Granita** for breakfast, with the brioche.
- **To drink:** **Malvasia delle Lipari**, both dry and **passito**, golden and sweet, at the end of a meal.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Check the ordinances in force on Stromboli and Vulcano** before leaving: they change and can't be bypassed.
- **Don't book in July and August** if there's the alternative of June or September.
- **Soft bag, never a hard suitcase.**
- **Use boat water sparingly**: on the islands it's a scarce good.
- **Seasickness remedies taken beforehand**, not after.
- **Accept that the itinerary will change.** The weather rules, and a skipper who changes course is doing his job.
- **Don't treat Lipari as a stopover**: the archaeological museum is worth the trip by itself.
- **Don't skip Filicudi and Alicudi** because they're inconvenient. By boat they're within reach, and it's the only easy way to see them.
- **Headlamp and closed shoes** for the climb to Stromboli, even if you climb only a little.
- **Book the Salina wineries**, which are not shops.

## Chiusura personale
What stays with you isn't a panorama: it's a sound. You're anchored under the coast at Stromboli, at night, with the lights off and the engine stopped, and you can't see anything but the black profile of the volcano against a sky slightly less black. Then there's a dull thud, that you feel in the hull before your ears, and on the rim of the crater an orange light goes on. A few seconds later the glowing blocks start to come down the chute, rolling for hundreds of meters to the sea and going out one by one with a hiss.

Then the silence returns, and after a few minutes it starts again. And what gets to you, sitting in the cockpit watching it, is that this happens **like this, at that rhythm, since before there were Greeks**: the ancients called it the lighthouse of the Mediterranean and sailed by it, and there are five hundred people living at its feet, buying bread, sending their kids to school, and over their heads the volcano explodes every five minutes for two thousand years. Nobody ever decided anything about it. It's simply like that, and people have gotten used to it.
