## Apertura personale
There are two states on the same island. In the south, the Republic of Ireland, in the European Union, with the euro. In the north, Northern Ireland, which is part of the United Kingdom and uses the pound. The border between them is almost invisible: no barriers, no customs. You notice you've crossed it because the signs switch from kilometres to miles.

This trip is a twelve-day driving loop that starts and ends in Dublin. It goes south toward Glendalough, Kilkenny and Cork, follows the Atlantic coast from Kerry to Connemara and climbs north to Derry/Londonderry, the Antrim coast and Belfast. It's one of Rick's trips, and here the story makes room mostly for practical information, checked against official sources.

There is also a long section on the two Irelands: why the island is divided, what the Troubles were, what the Good Friday Agreement changed and where things stand today.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** May to September. The climate is oceanic: in July and August daily averages are 14-16 °C, and it rains more in the west than in the east. Always bring a waterproof jacket.
- **Documents:** in the Republic, EU citizens enter with an ID card valid for travel abroad, but ireland.com warns that many carriers ask for a passport. **In Northern Ireland an ID card is not enough:** you need a **passport** and the UK **ETA**, the electronic authorisation that costs £20 per person, children included, and is applied for online on gov.uk. You need it even if you enter by car from the Republic, where nobody checks at the border. Before you leave, read the Ireland and United Kingdom pages of your own government's travel advice (for Italians, viaggiaresicuri.it).
- **Two currencies:** euro in the Republic, pound in Northern Ireland. Cards work everywhere.
- **Driving:** on the left across the whole island. In the Republic limits are in km/h, in Northern Ireland in miles per hour (30 mph in town, 60 outside, 70 on motorways). If the car rented in Dublin is going to cross the border, tell the rental company when you book.
- **Phone:** EU roaming applies in the Republic. Not in Northern Ireland: it depends on your plan, so check beforehand.
- **Sockets:** type G with three pins, the same in both Irelands. You need an adapter.
- **Health:** the EHIC is valid in the Republic and, for treatment that cannot wait, in Northern Ireland too. Travel insurance is still recommended.
- **Book ahead:** Kilmainham Gaol in Dublin (entry by guided tour only), the Book of Kells ticket, the Giant's Causeway car park and Titanic Belfast.
- **From the airport to the centre:** from **Dublin (DUB)** the Aircoach (running 24 hours a day) and Dublin Express coaches reach O'Connell Street and the surrounding area in about 30 minutes without traffic, about €8-10 one way, ticket online, from the machines or by card on board · city buses 16 and 41 cost about €2-2.60 but take 40-60 minutes · metered taxis from the official rank, about €30-45 depending on the time; apps like Uber and FreeNow call regular taxis at the same fare · the typical mistake is picking up the rental car as soon as you land: you don't need it for the Dublin days.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Arrival in Dublin
You land in Dublin and head into the centre by bus or taxi. The car is picked up later: you don't need it in the city, and paying for a city-centre hotel car park for two nights without using it is money wasted. Express coaches run from the airport to the centre and reach O'Connell Street and the surrounding area in about half an hour; city buses cost less but take nearly twice as long. *At rush hour traffic stretches the journey, so if you arrive towards evening it's best not to plan anything straight after.*

The first afternoon is for a walk along the **Liffey**, with the **Ha'penny Bridge**, the cast-iron footbridge of 1816, and then an aimless wander between Temple Bar and Grafton Street to get the measure of the city. The evening is for a pub with traditional music: sessions usually start after dinner, and a pint costs more in the Temple Bar pubs. Everything else about Dublin is in the [city page](/destinazioni/irlanda/dublino).

**In practice:** airport-centre express coach about €8-10 one way, about 30 minutes without traffic · taxi about €30-45 depending on the time · for the first two nights it's best to stay in the centre, within walking distance of Trinity College · tomorrow's tickets should already be bought online.

### Day 2 — Dublin: the Book of Kells and Kilmainham Gaol
Early in the morning, **Trinity College** and the **Book of Kells**, an illuminated gospel book from around the year 800. The first slot of the morning is usually the quietest, and tickets are timed. The Long Room of the Old Library is being restored and some of the books have been removed: check what's on show at visittrinity.ie, the only site to buy tickets from. *No photos are allowed in the manuscript room.* After the visit you cross the campus on foot and in ten minutes you're on Grafton Street or at St Stephen's Green for lunch.

In the afternoon, **Kilmainham Gaol**, the prison opened in 1796 where the leaders of the 1916 Easter Rising were also held. Entry is by guided tour only, booked in advance. The gaol lies west of the centre, about half an hour's walk from Trinity or a few minutes by bus or taxi. The tour goes from the cells to the yard where the 1916 leaders were executed, and it helps make sense of the rest of the trip, including the part about the north.

**In practice:** Book of Kells timed tickets online, price varies by day and season, from about €25 (visittrinity.ie) · Kilmainham Gaol about €8, guided tour only, tickets only on the official site or heritageireland.ie, closed 24-27 December · Kilmainham tickets sell out fast in summer: book well ahead.

### Day 3 — Glendalough and Kilkenny
You pick up the car and leave the city heading south into the **Wicklow Mountains**. It's best to collect it early in the morning, at the airport or at an office outside the centre, so your first kilometres on the left are away from traffic. At **Glendalough**, just over an hour from Dublin, are the ruins of the monastery founded by Saint Kevin in the 6th century, with its round tower, and an easy path to the **Upper Lake**. *The Upper Lake car park fills up early on summer weekends: arrive by 10 and you'll find a space.*

In the afternoon you continue to **Kilkenny**, a medieval town on the River Nore, about an hour and a half's drive. You sleep there and in the evening walk around the centre: the Medieval Mile, from the castle to St Canice's Cathedral, is an easy stroll, and the town's pubs are a few steps from one another. The guides: [Wicklow and Glendalough](/destinazioni/irlanda/wicklow-glendalough), [Kilkenny and Cashel](/destinazioni/irlanda/kilkenny-cashel).

**In practice:** Dublin-Glendalough about 1 hour 15, Glendalough-Kilkenny about 1 hour 30 · national park free to enter, Upper Lake car park about €4-5 a day, visitor centre about €5 · in Kilkenny pick accommodation with parking, as the centre is narrow.

### Day 4 — Kilkenny Castle, the Rock of Cashel and Cork
In the morning, **Kilkenny Castle**, home of the Butler family for almost six hundred years. The indoor visit takes about an hour, and the park around it is open to everyone without a ticket. Then west to the **Rock of Cashel**, about an hour's drive, the stone hill that was the seat of the kings of Munster and, from 1101, of the Church: round tower, Romanesque chapel and Gothic cathedral. *The wind almost always blows on the hill, even in summer: bring a jacket.*

**Cormac's Chapel** can only be seen with a guide, and places are sold on site. So it's worth reaching Cashel by late morning and buying a slot straight away, then exploring the rest of the site while you wait. In the afternoon you arrive in **Cork**, just over an hour by motorway, in time for a walk in the centre, between St Patrick's Street and the quays of the River Lee.

**In practice:** Kilkenny Castle about €8 · Rock of Cashel about €8, Cormac's Chapel tour with limited places · Kilkenny-Cashel about 1 hour, Cashel-Cork about 1 hour 15 · in Cork stay in the centre and leave the car parked until tomorrow.

### Day 5 — The English Market, Kinsale and on to Killarney
In the morning, Cork's **English Market**, the covered market opened in 1788. You wander among stalls of fish, Irish cheeses, bread and cured meats, and there are places to sit for breakfast or a quick lunch. *The market is closed on Sundays and bank holidays: if day 5 falls on a Sunday, swap it with the previous evening.*

Then south to **Kinsale**, about half an hour away, a harbour of colourful houses and restaurants, with **Charles Fort** of 1677 at the mouth of the bay. The star-shaped fort can be reached by car or on a coastal walk from Summercove, and from the ramparts you see the whole inlet. Kinsale is also a good place for a seafood lunch. In the afternoon you drive west to **Killarney**, the base for the next two days. The guide: [Cork and Kinsale](/destinazioni/irlanda/cork-kinsale).

**In practice:** English Market Monday to Saturday, about 8am-6pm, closed Sundays and bank holidays · Charles Fort is an OPW site, open daily, about €5 (heritageireland.ie) · Kinsale-Killarney about 1 hour 45 · in Killarney book two nights in the same place.

### Day 6 — Ring of Kerry
The **Ring of Kerry** is a 179 km loop around the Iveragh Peninsula, from Killarney back to Killarney: Kenmare, Sneem, Waterville, Cahersiveen, Killorglin. Coaches all drive it anticlockwise, because they can't pass each other on the narrow roads; if you're in a car you can go the other way and not get stuck behind them. Without stops it takes about four hours, with stops the whole day. *Setting off early, before 9, you get the emptiest road and the best light on the bays.*

The best-known stops are **Ladies View**, **Moll's Gap** and, in Killarney National Park, **Muckross House** and **Torc** waterfall. Going clockwise, leave them for the end of the loop, when you head back to Killarney on the Kenmare road: Torc waterfall is a few minutes' walk from its car park, and the Muckross gardens can be visited without a ticket. Along the coast, Waterville and the area around Cahersiveen are the handiest places for lunch.

**In practice:** 179 km loop, about 4 hours of pure driving, a full day with stops · Muckross House tour of the house about €9 (muckross-house.ie), gardens and park free · fill up in Killarney before setting off · narrow roads: in practice coaches always have right of way.

### Day 7 — Dingle Peninsula and the Slea Head Drive
From Killarney to **Dingle**, about an hour's drive, and then onto the **Slea Head Drive**, the coastal loop at the tip of the peninsula: Ventry beach, a prehistoric fort, the beehive-shaped stone huts, the view of the **Blasket Islands** and the **Gallarus Oratory**, a small dry-stone church. The loop is about 50 km, but with stops and narrow roads it takes half a day. *As on the Ring of Kerry, you drive it clockwise, which here is the direction the signs show.*

It's a Gaeltacht area, where Irish is spoken and signs are often in Irish only: Dingle also appears as An Daingean. Download your maps to your phone, as there's no signal on some stretches. You sleep in Dingle, a harbour town with many pubs that play music in the evening, sometimes from late afternoon. The guide: [Ring of Kerry and Dingle](/destinazioni/irlanda/ring-of-kerry-dingle).

**In practice:** Killarney-Dingle about 1 hour · Slea Head Drive about 50 km, 3-4 hours with stops · some stops (the beehive huts) are on private land with a small cash fee · in Dingle book accommodation early in summer, there aren't many places.

### Day 8 — Cliffs of Moher, the Burren and Galway
A long day of driving north. From Dingle the shortest route to County Clare uses the ferry across the Shannon estuary, between Tarbert and Killimer, which saves the detour via Limerick. The **Cliffs of Moher** drop sheer into the Atlantic: arrive outside the peak hours, 11am to 4pm, and buy your ticket online. *In summer the visitor centre stays open until evening, and late afternoon is the time with the fewest people and the best light.*

Then the **Burren**, the plateau of bare limestone, with the **Poulnabrone** dolmen, a Neolithic tomb. The dolmen is a few steps from a small car park on the R480, and you view it from the marked path without climbing on the stones. Crossing the Burren northwards you come down towards Galway Bay. You reach **Galway** in the evening. The guide: [Cliffs of Moher and the Burren](/destinazioni/irlanda/cliffs-of-moher-burren).

**In practice:** Tarbert-Killimer ferry about 20 minutes, frequent sailings, about €20-25 per car · Cliffs of Moher ticket includes parking, about €15 at the desk and less online off-peak · Dingle-Cliffs about 3 hours with the ferry, Cliffs-Galway about 1 hour 30 · part of the coastal path south of the cliffs may be closed: check cliffsofmoher.ie.

### Day 9 — Connemara and Kylemore Abbey
A driving loop from Galway heading west, among bogs, lakes and the **Twelve Bens** mountains. The most scenic road runs from Oughterard towards Recess and Clifden, returning via Leenane and Killary fjord. In **Connemara National Park**, near Letterfrack, is the Diamond Hill trail. *The full loop to the top takes two to three hours and needs hiking shoes; in bad weather the lower path is enough.*

A little further on is **Kylemore Abbey**, the lakeside castle built between 1867 and 1871 and a Benedictine abbey since 1920, with its Victorian walled garden. Between the abbey, the small Gothic church and the garden, linked by a shuttle, you'll easily spend two hours. In the evening, back to Galway, the most musical city of the trip: the pubs of the Latin Quarter and Quay Street have live music almost every night. The guides: [Galway](/destinazioni/irlanda/galway), [Connemara](/destinazioni/irlanda/connemara).

**In practice:** Galway-Kylemore about 1 hour 30 by the most direct route, the full loop is a day · Connemara National Park free to enter · Kylemore Abbey single ticket for abbey and gardens, check the price on kylemoreabbey.com · in Galway pick accommodation with parking outside the pedestrian centre.

### Day 10 — From Galway to Derry/Londonderry
The longest drive: you head north and cross the border into Northern Ireland without any checks. From here signs are in miles and you pay in pounds. It's about four hours on the road, via Sligo and Donegal, and it's best to leave early with a single stop on the way. *Have your passport and ETA with you already: nobody asks for them at the border, but you still need them.*

In the afternoon you walk the **walls of Derry/Londonderry**, built between 1613 and 1619, and go down into the **Bogside**, the Bloody Sunday neighbourhood, with the **Museum of Free Derry**. The full circuit of the walls is about a kilometre and a half and takes less than an hour; from the walls you look down on the Bogside murals. At sunset, the **Peace Bridge** over the River Foyle. Why the city has two names is explained in the [Derry/Londonderry page](/destinazioni/irlanda-del-nord/derry-londonderry).

**In practice:** Galway-Derry about 4 hours · Derry walls free to walk · Museum of Free Derry weekdays from 9:30, weekends about 10am-4pm, last entry one hour before closing · if a car rented in the Republic isn't insured for the north you can't cross: check when you pick it up.

### Day 11 — Causeway Coastal Route: Giant's Causeway, Carrick-a-Rede and the Dark Hedges
The day of the Antrim coast. From Derry you reach the coast in just over an hour. You start with the ruins of **Dunluce Castle** on the cliff, then the **Giant's Causeway**, with more than 40,000 basalt columns: you can walk down to the rocks for free, the ticket is for the visitor centre and parking. *Arriving early in the morning, before the coaches from Belfast, you have the columns almost to yourself.*

Further east, the **Carrick-a-Rede** rope bridge, hanging 30 metres above the rocks, which closes in strong winds. You book a time slot, and if the bridge closes because of wind, tickets bought on the official site are refunded. In the afternoon, inland, the **Dark Hedges**, the 18th-century avenue of beech trees: the road has been closed to cars since 2017 and you walk it. In the evening you reach **Belfast**, about an hour away. The guide: [Causeway Coastal Route](/destinazioni/irlanda-del-nord/causeway-coastal-route).

**In practice:** Giant's Causeway Visitor Experience about £15, best price online, shorter hours in winter · Carrick-a-Rede with a time-slot booking on the National Trust site, check the price there · Dark Hedges free, parking nearby · from the coast to Belfast about 1 hour.

### Day 12 — Belfast: murals, peace walls and the Titanic, then Dublin
In the morning, a tour with a local guide among the **murals** of Falls Road and Shankill Road and the **peace walls**, the walls that still separate some neighbourhoods. There are black taxi tours, walking tours and tours with two guides, one from each community. The tour takes about an hour and a half and it's best to book the day before. *You can leave signatures and messages on the walls, but people and houses should only be photographed with permission.*

Then **Titanic Belfast**, opened in 2012 on the site of the former shipyard where the Titanic was built. The visit takes about two and a half hours, and next door is the SS Nomadic, the Titanic's tender ship, on the same ticket. In the afternoon you drive back towards Dublin to return the car and catch your flight, or you add a night. The guide: [Belfast](/destinazioni/irlanda-del-nord/belfast).

**In practice:** Titanic Belfast about £24.95, timed ticket to book online, last entry about 1 hour 40 before closing · Belfast-Dublin Airport about 2 hours by motorway · for a same-day flight pick an evening one: with the Titanic and the tour there's no slack.

## The two Irelands: why the island is divided
This is the part of the trip worth reading before you leave. With a few dates in your head, the murals of Belfast and the walls of Derry make much more sense.

**The 1921 partition.** The island was part of the United Kingdom. The Government of Ireland Act of 1920 divided it in two, and partition came into force on May 3, 1921. On December 6, 1921 the Anglo-Irish Treaty created the Irish Free State, born a year later, on December 6, 1922, with 26 counties. Six of the nine counties of Ulster, in the north, stayed in the United Kingdom: they are Northern Ireland. The south later became the Republic of Ireland: the law that gives it that name came into force on April 18, 1949.

**Not a religious war.** You often hear that the Troubles were a war between Catholics and Protestants. It is more accurate to say they were a political conflict over national identity, with a religious dimension as well. On one side the **unionists** and **loyalists**, mainly Protestant, who wanted to stay in the United Kingdom. On the other the **nationalists** and **republicans**, mainly Catholic, who wanted a united Ireland. Religion almost always marked which community you belonged to, but the question was which state to belong to.

**The Troubles, from the late 1960s to 1998.** There is no agreed start date; usually people start from the late 1960s, with the civil rights marches and the clashes of 1969. Those involved were republican paramilitary groups, such as the Provisional IRA, loyalist ones, such as the UVF and the UDA, and the forces of the British state, army and police. According to the figures gathered on Wikipedia, 3,532 people died, and more than half were civilians. Republican paramilitaries were responsible for about 60% of the deaths, loyalist ones for about 30%, the security forces for about 10%. On January 30, 1972, in Derry, British paratroopers killed 13 unarmed civilians during a civil rights march, and one man died later: this is Bloody Sunday. In 2010 the Saville Inquiry concluded that none of those shot posed a threat, and the British prime minister apologized.

**The Good Friday Agreement.** It was signed in Belfast on April 10, 1998 and approved in two referendums on May 22, 1998: 71% yes in Northern Ireland, 94% in the Republic. The main points: a government shared between the two communities, with an assembly in Belfast; the principle of consent, under which Northern Ireland stays in the United Kingdom until a majority, in the north and in the south, decides otherwise; the right to identify as Irish, British or both, with one or both citizenships; the Republic removed its claim on the north from its constitution. Then came the disarmament of the paramilitary groups, police reform and the early release of prisoners. Violence fell sharply, though there have been sporadic incidents since.

**The peace walls today.** The first walls between Catholic and Protestant neighborhoods in Belfast date from 1969 and were meant to be temporary. They stayed. In 2013 the Northern Ireland government set itself the goal of removing them all by 2023, with the consent of residents; the deadline passed with many barriers still standing. Counts vary a lot depending on what is counted (walls, gates, closed roads), and the most recent figures reported on Wikipedia speak of 39 official peace walls in 2026. In a 2022 survey 83% of people said they wanted them removed; ten years earlier, in 2012, 69% of residents still thought they were necessary. Visit them with a guide, ideally one who tells the story with more than one voice.

**The border and Brexit, in brief.** After the Agreement the border became invisible. With Brexit the problem was how to avoid putting checks back on the island: the Northern Ireland Protocol kept the north aligned with European rules on goods, moving the checks to the Irish Sea, between Great Britain and Northern Ireland. The **Windsor Framework**, agreed in 2023, eased those checks. In the 2016 referendum almost 56% of Northern Irish voters had voted to remain in the European Union.

**The economy: two different measures.** You hear that Northern Ireland is worse off than the Republic. The numbers need care. According to the ONS, the UK statistics office, in 2023 GDP per head in Northern Ireland was £32,944, against a UK average of £39,403: seventh of twelve UK regions and nations, above Wales and the North East of England. A direct comparison with the Republic is misleading, though, because Irish GDP is inflated by the profits of multinationals headquartered in Dublin: in 2015 it showed growth of 26%. That is why the Irish statistics office also uses another measure, GNI*, which removes this effect and is much lower than GDP. The honest picture is this: the north is below the UK average, and a precise comparison with the south needs measures that GDP figures alone don't give.

## Variations
**More Belfast.** Day 12 is full. With an extra night in Belfast you can add Crumlin Road Gaol, the Ulster Museum, which also covers the Troubles, and City Hall with the testimonies of people who lived through them, and drive back to Dublin calmly the next day.

**Without Dingle.** With ten days, drop the Dingle Peninsula and go straight from Kerry up to the Cliffs of Moher.

**The other way round.** You can also do the loop clockwise, Belfast and the north first, then the Atlantic coast. It changes little: choose based on flights and free nights in Dublin.

**With Scotland.** Scotland is close and combines well with this trip, with the [Scotland road trip](/viaggi/scozia-highlands-distillerie), especially starting from Belfast. Check the connections at the time.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Passport and ETA for everyone**, even if you only go into the north for a day. With just an ID card you can't enter Northern Ireland.
- **Tell the rental company you're crossing the border**, when you book.
- **Drive slowly on country roads**: they are narrow, with walls and hedges right at the edge, and you drive on the left. After the border the limits are in miles.
- **Book ahead** Kilmainham Gaol, the Book of Kells (only on visittrinity.ie), Titanic Belfast and the Giant's Causeway car park.
- **The Cliffs of Moher outside peak hours**: online and before 11 or after 16 you pay less and find fewer people.
- **On the Ring of Kerry, go clockwise** if you want to avoid ending up behind the coaches.
- **At the murals and in the Bogside, be respectful**: photograph the walls, not the people, and avoid political comments.
- **Check your roaming** before going into the north.

## Chiusura personale
You cross Ireland for the landscapes, and they don't disappoint: the cliffs, Kerry, Connemara, the Antrim coast. The reason it's worth going all the way to the north is another one. Between Derry and Belfast you walk inside a history that is still recent, and the 1998 Agreement stops being a date in a book.
