# Krakow in a Long Weekend

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 in progress
**Periodo del viaggio:**
**Durata:** 3-4 days, 3 nights
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independently, all on foot and by public transport
**Categoria:** Europa · Polonia · Cracovia · storia · memoria · gastronomia

---

## Apertura personale
Of all the capitals of Central Europe, Krakow is the city that suffered least in World War II: while Warsaw was razed almost to the ground and rebuilt from scratch, Krakow's historic center has come down to today almost intact, and that's why in 1978 it ended up among the first twelve sites ever inscribed on the UNESCO list: together, among others, with the Galápagos, Yellowstone and, a few miles from here, the Wieliczka Salt Mine. It walks like a medieval city because it really is one, it's not a reconstruction.

It's also a city on which, inevitably, another history weighs: an hour and a half by bus away is Auschwitz-Birkenau, and any weekend in Krakow that ignores it completely leaves out the reason why, for many, that city and that name remain forever linked. It isn't an obligation, but the most honest choice is to include it, with the space and care it deserves, not as one stop among others.

Three or four days are enough to do both things well: the medieval Krakow of Rynek Główny and Wawel, the more intimate one of Kazimierz, and the separate day for Auschwitz-Birkenau. With four days you add the Wieliczka Salt Mine, which is the most classic day trip in the area and a world of its own, literally underground.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** **April-May and September-October**, with mild weather, fewer crowds and shorter lines. **June-August** is high season: it's hot, there are many people, and precisely in the summer months Auschwitz-Birkenau reaches its peak of visitors, with entry slots selling out sooner. **November-March** is cold, often below freezing and with possible snow, but the city holds up well even in winter and in December Rynek Główny hosts one of the best-known Christmas markets in Central Europe.
- **Currency:** Poland is in the European Union but **not in the eurozone**: you pay in złoty (PLN), not in euros, and it's the most common surprise for those who arrive assuming the opposite. Cards and contactless work almost everywhere; cash is mostly for small purchases.
- **Watch out for currency exchanges (kantor):** some around Rynek Główny and on Floriańska Street display flashy rates but applied to minor currencies instead of euros and dollars, or hide the gap between buy and sell in small print. Better a kantor with a complete, transparent rate board, a little off the most touristy axis.
- **Auschwitz-Birkenau, booking:** entry is **free** but online booking is **in practice mandatory**, on the official site **visit.auschwitz.org**, up to three months ahead. In the middle hours of the day (roughly from 7:30 until early or late afternoon depending on the month) **you enter only with a Museum guide-educator or an organized tour**: the independent visit without a guide is possible only in the remaining hours, and must in any case be booked at least a week ahead.
- **Auschwitz-Birkenau, how to get there:** direct bus from Krakow's bus station (**about 1h30**, departures every 20-40 minutes, around 34 PLN each way) or a train to Oświęcim (**about 1h40**) and then a 20-30 minute walk or a short shuttle bus. Alternatively, an organized tour with transfer, guide and ticket included (about 1h-1h30 of travel).
- **Wieliczka:** the Salt Mine is visited on the **Tourist Route** (2-3 hours, ticket around 120 PLN) or the more demanding **Miners' Route**. Online booking recommended, especially on weekends. In the mine it's always 57-61°F (14-16°C), regardless of the season on the surface.
- **The Hejnał:** every hour, from the tallest tower of St. Mary's Basilica, a trumpet plays live a short melody cut off abruptly mid-note. Legend has it that it recalls the guard struck by an arrow while sounding the alarm during a Tatar attack. It's worth stopping in the square on the hour to hear it.
- **Watch out for the venues "recommended in the street":** Krakow is among the most popular bachelor-party destinations in Europe, and around Rynek Główny, Floriańska Street and Kazimierz there are promoters (often young women) who invite tourists to "a fantastic bar." The bill that follows can reach hundreds of euros for a few drinks, with bouncers discouraging you from leaving until you pay. Anyone who approaches you in the street proposing a venue is not to be followed, period.
- **Getting around the city:** the historic center is all walkable. KMK trams and buses are extensive and cheap (time-based tickets, a few PLN) for the rest of the city. You don't need a car.
- **From the airport:** from **John Paul II (KRK, Balice)**, the direct train (SKA1 line) reaches Kraków Główny in **17-20 minutes**; there are also regular buses.
- **Food:** **pierogi** in dozens of variations, **żurek** (fermented rye flour soup, often served in a hollowed-out loaf), **bigos** (cabbage and meat stew), **zapiekanki** (toasted bread with mushrooms and melted cheese, street food born right in Kazimierz). The **bary mleczne**, cheap canteens inherited from the communist era, remain the most genuine and cheapest way to eat traditional dishes. To drink, **Polish vodka**, often flavored: the best known is **żubrówka**, with bison grass.
- **Hours:** state museums (Wawel included) often have a weekly closing day, and some rooms of the castle require separate tickets in limited numbers: check before building the schedule.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Rynek Główny and Wawel Hill
We started from the heart of the city: **Rynek Główny**, the medieval market square, among the largest in Europe, laid out in 1257 and never moved since. At the center, the **Sukiennice** (Cloth Hall), the old cloth hall rebuilt in Renaissance style in the 16th century: the ground floor is still a covered souvenir market, while upstairs there is a gallery of 19th-century Polish painting.

On the side of the square rises **St. Mary's Basilica**, Gothic, with two unequal towers, one taller than the other because of a legend about two rival architect brothers. Inside, the great **wooden altarpiece by Veit Stoss**, carved in the 15th century, is among the largest Gothic altarpieces in existence. Every hour, from the tallest tower, the **Hejnał mariacki** plays live, cut off abruptly mid-note.

In the afternoon we climbed **Wawel** hill: the **Royal Castle**, seat of Polish power for centuries, with the State Rooms, the Royal Apartments, the Treasury and the Armory (tickets often separate for the different sections, with a limited number of daily entries), and the **Cathedral**, the coronation and burial place of Polish kings, with the **Sigismund Bell** and the royal crypts.

We spent the evening in the historic center, inside the **Planty**, the old tree-lined avenues that follow the line of the medieval walls demolished in the 19th century.

### Day 2 — Auschwitz-Birkenau
This is a day apart, and has to be treated as such: the only thing on the schedule, with no other stops to squeeze in.

We left Krakow early for **Oświęcim**, about 41 miles (66 km) west (you get there by bus or train). The visit, with a guide-educator in the middle hours of the day (required in high season and most of the year), covers two sites linked by a free shuttle: **Auschwitz I**, the original camp, with the gate and the inscription "Arbeit macht frei," the museum blocks with the permanent and national exhibitions, Block 11 and the gas chamber preserved on site; and **Auschwitz II-Birkenau**, far larger, with the railway entrance of the Judenrampe where the selection took place, the barracks mostly of wood and the remains of the crematoria blown up by the retreating Germans in 1945.

The complete visit takes, all told, **3.5 hours** plus transport: it's best to allow a whole day, with no other plans before or after. It is a place that asks for respectful time, clothing and behavior; in some areas photographs are forbidden.

We returned to Krakow in the late afternoon. It's not the evening for elaborate plans.

### Day 3 — Kazimierz and Podgórze
In the morning we went to **Kazimierz**, the former autonomous town founded in 1335 and, from 1495, for centuries the center of Jewish life in Krakow, until the destruction of the community during the Nazi occupation. The **Old Synagogue**, the oldest in Poland, now a museum; the **Remuh Synagogue**, one of the two still active in the city, with the adjoining Jewish cemetery; the streets around **Plac Nowy**, the neighborhood's market square, now the center of the city's nightlife.

In the early afternoon we crossed the Vistula toward **Podgórze**: **Ghetto Heroes Square** (Plac Bohaterów Getta), with the rows of empty metal chairs commemorating the Jewish ghetto set up here by the Nazis in 1941, and the former **Oskar Schindler Factory**, now a museum devoted to the German occupation of Krakow: the necessary historical counterpoint to Kazimierz, a few minutes' walk away.

In the evening we went back to Kazimierz. Here you can take a food tour of pierogi, zapiekanki and Polish vodka tasting (see the dedicated experience page) or, on your own, a stroll among the bars of Plac Nowy.

### Day 4 — The Wieliczka Mine and a Last Stroll
In the morning we went to the **Wieliczka Salt Mine**, half an hour from Krakow: the **Tourist Route** descends through more than twenty chambers carved into the salt over seven centuries of work, to the **Chapel of St. Kinga**, an underground church sculpted entirely from salt, floor, chandeliers and bas-reliefs included. You go down on foot (over 800 steps) and come back up by elevator.

We returned to Krakow in the early afternoon for a last stroll: the **Rynek Underground Museum**, beneath the main square, with the archaeological remains of the medieval market uncovered during restoration; the **Barbican** and the remains of the walls, at the northern edge of the historic center; then free time for last purchases in the Sukiennice.

In the evening, in the center, the last dinner of the weekend.

## What to see / what to do
- **Rynek Główny and the Sukiennice**, the heart of the city since 1257.
- **The Hejnał** from the tower of St. Mary's, on the hour, to hear its sudden interruption.
- **Wawel Hill**, castle and cathedral together.
- **Auschwitz-Birkenau**, with the time and disposition it deserves.
- **Kazimierz**, among the historic synagogues and the life of Plac Nowy.
- **Podgórze**, Ghetto Heroes Square and the Schindler Factory.
- **The Wieliczka Salt Mine**, with the Chapel of St. Kinga carved in salt.
- **The Rynek Underground Museum**, beneath the main square.

## Possible extensions
- **Zakopane and the Tatra Mountains (+1 day):** about 2 hours by train or bus, for the Polish gateway to the mountains: the town itself, in wooden alpine style, and the Tatra National Park, with trails and hikes (skiing in winter).
- **Częstochowa and the Jasna Góra Sanctuary (+1 day, or half a day):** about 2 hours by train, for the Pauline monastery that houses the icon of the Black Madonna, the most important pilgrimage site in Poland.
- **Warsaw:** it's not a half-day extension. It's a different Poland, that of the capital rebuilt from scratch after the destruction of the war, and deserves an itinerary of its own.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
The **historic center** inside the Planty is the most convenient choice: everything on foot, with most of the tourist offering concentrated there. **Kazimierz** is the more lived-in and generally cheaper alternative, ten to fifteen minutes' walk from Rynek Główny, with an evening life of its own that the historic center doesn't have.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
The **bary mleczne**, the cheap canteens inherited from the communist era, for pierogi and everyday dishes at minimal prices. The **zapiekanki** of Plac Nowy as an evening snack. To drink, Polish vodka, which in the tourist bars of the center costs more than elsewhere: it's better to try it in a historic place rather than the first one pointed out by a street promoter.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Book Auschwitz-Birkenau well ahead**, on the official site, not on the spot.
- **Don't follow anyone who approaches you in the street proposing a bar**: it's the classic scheme of inflated bills.
- **Check the rates carefully at the kantor** before exchanging, especially near Rynek Główny.
- **Don't squeeze Auschwitz-Birkenau between two other stops**: it's a day on its own.
- **Bring a small backpack to Auschwitz-Birkenau**: there is a size limit, with paid storage for larger ones.
- **Dress in layers**, especially in winter: the cold in Krakow is dry but real, and at Birkenau there is almost no shade even in summer.
- **Check the seasonal hours** of Auschwitz-Birkenau and the closing days of the Wawel museums before building the schedule.
- **Pay in złoty**, don't expect euros just because you're in the European Union.

## Chiusura personale
What stays with me is the contrast between the two halves of the weekend: the medieval square with the trumpet that plays every hour and breaks off mid-note, and, an hour and a half away, a place that asks to be visited in silence. Krakow is both things at once, and it's the reason a weekend here is not just another European city break.
