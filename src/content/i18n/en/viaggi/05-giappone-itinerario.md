# Japan in Two Weeks: Tokyo, Mount Fuji, Kyoto, Hiroshima, Osaka and Chasing the Sakura

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 work in progress (complete itinerary, no fixed schedules, with food and logistics, and opening/closing anecdotes)
**Periodo del viaggio:** April 6-17
**Durata:** 12 days, 11 nights
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, JR Pass for the big train transfers, activated from the fourth day on
**Categoria:** Asia · Giappone · sakura · cultura · cibo · on the road

---

## Apertura personale
I arrived in Tokyo for the sakura bloom, but a few days off the peak: the cherry trees in early April were already near the end, with petals falling like pink snow (hanafubuki) and ever barer branches. I spent the first afternoon hunting for the best cherry trees around the city, knowing that perfect timing is almost impossible to hit in a country where the season lasts a few days. It was a spectacle all the same, even in its fading version, and it's the right way to open a trip that took me from Tokyo to Mount Fuji, then on to Kyoto, Hiroshima and Osaka.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** early April. Spring weather, between 8°C and 18°C (46-64°F), like spring in Italy. The cherry blossom (sakura) in Tokyo usually peaks between late March and early April: those who arrive in early April risk finding the fading phase (falling petals), but the areas farther north or at altitude, like Chureito, near Mount Fuji, which usually blooms a week after Tokyo, can offer the full show.
- **Documents/visa:** no visa needed for tourism up to 90 days, just a passport valid for the stay and a return ticket.
- **Currency:** Japanese yen, approximate rate 1€ = 160 yen. It's a good idea to exchange some cash before leaving: many small places don't accept cards.
- **Budget:** around 600-750€ for the whole stay on the ground (excluding flights and public transport passes), very variable depending on the restaurants you choose.
- **Transport:** JR Pass activated from the fourth day (it covers the main Shinkansen, Hikari and Sakura yes, Nozomi and Mizuho no, plus many local lines), supplemented with a rechargeable PASMO/IC card for metro, buses and konbini. To activate the JR Pass you need your passport, and it's an operation to allow plenty of time for: quite long queues can form at the ticket office.
- **Luggage:** a backpack or soft duffel, never a trolley. With all the travel on public transport and the station stairs, you regret a hard-shell suitcase right away.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno
### Day 1 (06.04) — Tokyo, Asakusa and Chasing the Sakura
We landed late in the morning and went straight to **Asakusa**, where we left our suitcases at the Agora Place Asakusa. The first lunch was street food at the **Ameyoko market**.

The afternoon was a hunt for cherry blossoms, a real sakura chasing among the best spots in the city. **Chidorigafuchi**, the moat of the imperial palace, is among the most photographed in Tokyo at this time of year. **Shinjuku Gyoen**, the national garden, is perhaps the safest bet for still finding flowers late in the season. Then **Ueno Park** and the **Meguro River**, with cherry trees along the canal, beautiful above all in the evening when they are lit up. *In early April you risk arriving when the flowers in Tokyo are already falling. If that happens, there's hanafubuki, the "petal storm" when the wind picks up, a spectacle just as beautiful in its own way.*

In the late afternoon, if you like, there's a drink at one of the historic izakaya on **Hoppy Street**, in Asakusa. Then we took a bit of time to get our bearings in the neighborhood and had dinner at **MoMo Paradise**, an all-you-can-eat chain for shabu-shabu and sukiyaki: a practical choice for the first evening, with no surprises on the menu, bookable in advance.

### Day 2 (07.04) — Kamakura, the Ancient Capital by the Sea
We left early: metro to Shimbashi, then the Yokosuka line to Kita-Kamakura (trains every 10-20 minutes, about 820 yen). The first stop was the **Engaku-ji** temple, one hour, 400 yen: if the sky is clear, from the big bell you can see Fuji in the distance. A quarter of an hour's walk away is **Kencho-ji** (450 yen), with the Butsuden, the Buddha hall, and, for those with time, the Hansobo shrine at the top of a steep staircase. Then the **Tsurugaoka Hachimangu** shrine.

We had lunch walking along **Komachi Dori**, the street of little restaurants and street food, tasting a bit of everything. In the early afternoon we were at **Kotoku-in**, the Great Buddha of Kamakura, about half an hour's visit. If time is left over, **Shichirigahama** beach is a couple of steps away: few people know that Kamakura is on the sea.

In the late afternoon we went back to Tokyo. The free time can be spent hunting for kimono to rent for photos: Sakaeya in Harajuku, the most expensive, Tansuya in Asakusa from 5,000 yen, the thrift shop Miyoshiya in Nakano from 1,100 yen. In the evening, **Shibuya Crossing** seen from above: free from the eighth floor of the Tourist Center, open until evening, or from the walkway of Shibuya Mark City; paid, 1,500 yen with a drink, from the **Mag8** rooftop. Then dinner, and back to the Agora Place Asakusa.

### Day 3 (08.04) — Mount Fuji and the Chureito Pagoda
We left early from Shinjuku toward **Kawaguchiko** on the direct **Fuji Excursion** train: about two hours and 4,130 yen (those who have already activated the JR Pass cover the stretch to Otsuki and pay only the Fujikyu supplement, about 1,770 yen). Alternatively there are direct buses from Shinjuku, Shibuya or Tokyo Station, two hours and 2,000-2,500 yen, to be booked in advance. For the pagoda we got off earlier, at the **Chureito/Shimoyoshida** stop.

At the **Chureito Pagoda**, in Arakurayama Sengen Park, we climbed the 398 steps to the most famous viewpoint in Japan: the red five-story pagoda in front, Fuji behind. Here the bloom arrives about a week after Tokyo, and the cherry trees were still full: the right place to catch the sakura we'd lost in the city. *Tip: arrive as early as possible, to avoid the crowd and get the best light.*

Then **Lake Kawaguchiko**, which we went around to look at Fuji from another side; the Mount Tenjo cable car adds another view from above. And before going back, an **onsen with a view of Fuji**: the Five Lakes area is full of hot springs, and a bath with the snowy mountain in front of you is among the most Japanese things you can do in a day. At the Hotel Mifujien, a historic ryokan open during the day even to those not staying there, admission is about 1,200 yen. In the evening we went back to Tokyo from Shinjuku and slept again at the Agora Place Asakusa.

### Day 4 (09.04) — Tokyo-Kyoto
Early at the station in Tokyo, we took the Hikari Shinkansen to Kyoto, two hours and forty minutes. We arrived mid-morning, left our luggage and went to the **Nishiki Market**, the big covered market, to be walked tasting a bit of everything: a couple of hours between food and shops, and at the end of the market a gallery opens up, running from clothes to electronics.

In the early afternoon we reached **Kiyomizudera**, one of the most famous temples in Japan, a finalist in the poll for the new seven wonders of the world: twenty-five minutes by bus from Nishiki or thirty-five on foot via Gion, and an hour's visit. For the rest of the afternoon there are two paths: the **Miyako Odori**, the traditional dance of geiko and maiko (6,000 yen, seasonal, worth checking the dates), or the **Funaoka Onsen**, a neighborhood hot spring bath, small, authentic and inexpensive.

We had dinner at **Menbaka Fire Ramen**, where the ramen arrives with a show of real fire: book in advance, the seats go fast. For those who want to stay out late there's the Pontocho area, with the **World Club** or **The Common One**. For three nights we slept at the Agora Kyoto Shijo.

### Day 5 (10.04) — Kyoto: Arashiyama, the Bamboo Forest and Gion
The morning began on the **Philosopher's Path**, along the canal among the cherry trees, named for Nishida Kitaro, who walked it every day meditating. Then a walking tour of **Gion**, the geiko quarter: on some streets taking photos of overly intrusive tourists is forbidden, there are signs and the fines are steep.

In the early afternoon we went down to Saga Arashiyama station and had lunch in the area. Then the **Arashiyama Monkey Park Iwatayama**: forty-five minutes of climbing among the Japanese macaques and Kyoto from above. The **Okochi Sanso Garden**, the beautifully kept garden of a famous Japanese film director, costs 1,000 yen with matcha tea and a sweet. Last, the **Arashiyama bamboo forest**, most beautiful toward sunset, when the daytime crowd thins out.

For dinner there's **Hamburg Labo**, Japanese-style hamburgers, or the conveyor-belt sushi at **Sushi No Musashi**, cheap and fun. The night at the Agora Kyoto Shijo.

### Day 6 (11.04) — Kyoto: Fushimi Inari, Nijo Castle, Kinkaku-ji
We left early for **Fushimi Inari**, the shrine of the thousand vermilion torii. The full loop up to the top of the mountain takes two and a half hours, but the first stretch, the one everyone photographs, is worth the visit even without reaching the summit. In late morning we were at **Nijo Castle**, one of the few castles to have come down intact from the feudal era, along with Himeji's, which comes later in the trip.

After lunch and a bit of free time, the **Kinkaku-ji**, the Golden Pavilion, open from morning to late afternoon: the pavilion covered in gold leaf reflected in the pond is one of the symbols of Japan.

In the evening there's the **tea ceremony** with Camellia, in the heart of Gion: 2,700 yen per person plus 3,000 for the kimono, to be booked by email well in advance because the seats are few. For dinner the addresses from the day before work; the last night we spent at the Agora Kyoto Shijo.

### Day 7 (12.04) — Kyoto-Hiroshima-Miyajima
The trick of this day is done the evening before: ship the heavy luggage to Osaka with takkyubin, from the hotel or a 7-Eleven, to travel light for two days. We left early and the Hikari Shinkansen took us to Hiroshima in about two hours.

In late morning we were at the **Peace Memorial** and the **Peace Park**, with the Museum: a couple of hours with the audio guide. It's an intense and necessary stop, to be approached calmly. After a quick lunch, the ferry, included in the JR Pass, took us to **Miyajima**. On the island the street food is mostly oysters, the local dish: it's not for everyone, but it should be tried at least once. We dedicated the afternoon to the **Itsukushima Shrine**, with the famous torii that seems to float on the water, and to the **Daisho-in**.

Toward evening we went back, checked in and had dinner at **Koba**, good both before and after dinner without changing places. We slept at the Toyoko Inn Hiroshima Station.

### Day 8 (13.04) — Hiroshima-Himeji-Osaka (or Kobe)
The Kodama Shinkansen to Himeji leaves early. *If there's room, it's worth booking the Hello Kitty themed train, all pink with a dedicated car: absurd but memorable, it runs on some services between Hiroshima and Himeji.*

In the morning we were at **Himeji Castle**, the most beautiful and best-preserved in Japan, with a guide; the combined ticket with the **Kokoen Gardens** costs 1,060 yen. We're close to Kobe, and lunch is the chance for real **Kobe beef**. The practical choice is **Fuku-tei**, skewers and takeaway burgers with excellent value; the Kobe teppanyaki steakhouses are another experience, 70-80€ for 150 grams, and call for a detour and more time.

The train to Osaka doesn't need to be booked and is taken calmly after lunch. After check-in we went to **Osaka Castle**: from the eighth floor the view over the city is beautiful, and inside the museum tells the history of Osaka with holograms. Toward evening **Dotonbori**, then the **Tombori River Cruise**, twenty minutes for 1,000 yen from Tazaemonbashi Harbour. For dinner there's the **Osaka Foodie Tour**, paid and to be booked separately, or **Torikizoku** near Namba: 3,600 yen for two hours of all you can eat and drink. We slept at the Sarasa Hotel Namba.

### Day 9 (14.04) — Osaka and Nara
From Namba the Kintetsu line goes to **Nara** (worth checking whether the JR Pass covers it). The first stop was a sweet one: at **Nakatanidou** you eat mochi, and around mid-morning there's the preparation show, always packed, so it's better to arrive early.

Then **Nara Park**, among the sacred deer roaming free and the temples: **Todaiji** with the great Buddha, one of the most beautiful in Japan, **Kasuga Taisha**, the shrine of a thousand lanterns, **Gangoji** if time remains, and the walk along **Old Nara Street** between the two main temples, where the deer gather. We had lunch at **Maguro Koya**, tuna and sashimi of remarkable quality.

In the afternoon we went back to Osaka for the **Kuromon Market** and the fluffy cheesecake from **Rikuro-Ojisan no Mise**, 900-1,000 yen for the whole cake, to share. We spent the evening in **Shinsekai**: the **Tsutenkaku Tower** (900 yen, closes in the evening, better not to arrive late) and, for the curious, **Tobita Shinchi**, the best-known red-light district in western Japan, to be looked at only from outside and with respect. For dinner there are the local specialties, kushikatsu, okonomiyaki, takoyaki, at **Jumbo-tsuribune Tsurikichi** or in one of the many little places in the area. Second night at the Sarasa Hotel Namba.

### Day 10 (15.04) — Osaka-Tokyo: Harajuku and Shibuya
Very early at Shin-Osaka we took the Hikari Shinkansen to Tokyo, about three hours. With the luggage dropped off we went to **Harajuku**: Takeshita Dori, the hyper-pop street with Totti's rainbow cotton candy cloud, and ten minutes away the **Meiji-Jingu**, an oasis of calm after that chaos. We had lunch at **Gyozaro**, gyoza specialty.

The afternoon we were in **Shibuya**: the Hachiko statue and Shibuya Crossing, this time from a different angle than on the first day. It's worth going up Shibuya Scramble Square, fourteenth floor and then the Sky Observatory, or looking out for free from the art gallery next door. Toward evening we checked in at the Sunshine City Prince Hotel and had dinner at **Kyomachi Koishigure Shinjuku**, a place with a 1950s atmosphere.

### Day 11 (16.04) — Tokyo: Tsukiji, Akihabara, Senso-ji, Shinjuku
In the morning we were at the **Tsukiji market**: the wholesale market has moved to Toyosu, but the historic area remains a lively market, perfect for a breakfast or a lunch of very fresh fish. Early risers can try to see the **sumo training**, at some stables visible from the street, like Arashio-beya in Hamacho: better to ask the day before whether there's training.

We had lunch in **Akihabara** at **Kyushu Ramen** and stayed in the kingdom of manga and anime: **Super Potato**, three floors of vintage consoles and video games, **Mandrake** for collectibles, **Don Quijote** for any souvenir, **Radio Kaikan**, ten floors of gadgets in front of the station. In the late afternoon we went back to Asakusa for **Nakamise Dori** and **Senso-ji**, open from morning to late afternoon (the hours may change, worth rechecking).

We spent the evening in **Shinjuku**. If there's time, it's worth a stop at **Golden Gai**, six alleys of tiny bars with five or six seats, to be tried at least once. We had the last dinner at **Gonpachi**, the place that inspired the restaurant scene in *Kill Bill*. Last night at the Sunshine City Prince Hotel.

### Day 12 (17.04) — Check out
Check-out in the morning and departure.

## Food and Izakaya: Practical Tips
Food is probably the real thread of this trip, and it's worth approaching it with a method:

- **Reservations are more complicated than expected.** Many Japanese restaurants, especially the best ones, have very few seats (sometimes 6-10 covers) and don't speak English on the phone. For larger groups it's almost always necessary to book days or weeks in advance, often by email or through the hotel concierge. The simpler places (ramen, small izakaya, neighborhood trattorias) instead don't take reservations: you show up and wait in line (even 30-60 minutes at the most famous places), but that's often exactly where you eat best.
- **Izakaya are the most practical solution when you're a group.** They are the Japanese equivalent of pubs: you share lots of small dishes in the middle of the table, the bill is easily split (just ask "betsu betsu no okaikei wo onegaishimasu" for separate bills), and many offer **tabehodai/nomihodai** deals (all-you-can-eat/drink for 1h30-2h at a fixed price), handy when you're many and don't want to handle a complicated bill. Note: the deal applies to the whole table, you can't choose it for only some of the diners.
- **The cover charge**: many restaurants apply a small "otoshi" (an unrequested opening nibble, a sort of cover charge) of a few euros per person: it's normal, not a rip-off.
- **The bill never includes taxes**: 10% has to be added to the total.
- **Extra tips from research**, for those who want to widen the list beyond the places already mentioned in the plan: in Tokyo high-end izakaya like Tanakada (Nishi-Azabu) are excellent for a special occasion; in Kyoto Muromachi Kaji is a kappo-izakaya much appreciated for its kaiseki-style dishes in a more casual version. Both are listed by the Michelin Guide as Bib Gourmand (good value), and need to be booked in advance.

## Onsen: The Other Side of the Trip
Japan offers more chances than the plan includes for a traditional hot bath, and it's worth fitting in at least a couple at a leisurely pace (Funaoka Onsen in Kyoto, the Kawaguchiko onsen on the Mount Fuji day):

- **Onsen and sentō**: the onsen uses natural hot spring water, the sentō is a public bath with heated tap water. The rules are the same.
- **You enter naked**, after washing carefully at the side showers, before getting into the shared tub (the tub water must not be soaped).
- **Tattoos**: the traditional rule forbids entry to those with visible tattoos (historically associated with the yakuza), but with the tourist boom the situation is changing fast. Many facilities accept small tattoos if covered with a special patch (in konbini you find them as "tattoo hidden sheets"), others accept them without problems. Better to ask beforehand, especially at the most traditional ryokan and onsen; facilities designed for tourists are almost always more permissive.
- **Mixed onsen**: rare but they exist, and there a towel or cloth is usually allowed instead of complete nudity.

## What to see / what to do
- **Chureito Pagoda and Mount Fuji:** the symbol image of Japan and, with a bit of luck, the right place to catch the sakura missed in Tokyo.
- **Fushimi Inari:** the thousand vermilion torii, best early in the morning.
- **Kinkaku-ji:** the Golden Pavilion, an icon.
- **Miyajima and the floating torii**, with the Hiroshima Peace Memorial balancing the day with a moment of reflection.
- **Himeji Castle:** the most beautiful feudal castle in Japan.
- **Nara deer park:** an almost surreal experience, with the sacred deer wandering free among the temples.
- **Shibuya Crossing and Shinjuku by night:** the most contemporary Japan, to be experienced at least one evening.
- **A real izakaya**, ideally small and local, to understand what it means to eat "the Japanese way" in company.

**Tokyo (Asakusa) — Agora Place Asakusa**
An inexpensive, functional hotel a couple of steps from the metro, with a lobby nicer than average and friendly, efficient staff. The most cited flaw in the reviews is the very small rooms (bathroom included), normal for Tokyo but to be factored in; a few reviews also report wifi problems and, in isolated cases, bedbugs.
*Alternatives:* budget: Khaosan Tokyo Kabuki, APA Hotel Asakusa Kaminarimon · mid-range: Mimaru Tokyo Asakusa Station, Hotel Wing International Asakusa · upscale without excess: The Gate Hotel Asakusa Kaminarimon by HULIC, Asakusa View Hotel

**Kyoto — Agora Kyoto Shijo**
A good mid-range choice: rooms more spacious than the Japanese average, with a separation between the bath area and the shower that reviews often cite as a strength, and a convenient location near Shijo Street. Customer service gets mixed ratings: some very positive reviews, others more critical about the handling of booking changes.
*Alternatives:* budget: K's House Kyoto, Piece Hostel Sanjo · mid-range: Mitsui Garden Hotel Kyoto Shijo, Hotel Vista Premio Kyoto · upscale without excess: Gion Hatanaka (ryokan with onsen), The Celestine Kyoto Gion

**Hiroshima — Toyoko Inn Hiroshima Station**
An inexpensive but reliable chain: simple, clean rooms, included breakfast appreciated in the reviews, convenient near the station, great for those who travel a lot with the JR Pass. The most recurring flaw is the thin walls, which let through corridor noise.
*Alternatives:* budget: APA Hotel Hiroshima Ekimae Ohashi, Centurion Hotel Residential Hiroshima Ekimae · mid-range: Hotel Sunroute Hiroshima, Courtyard by Marriott Hiroshima · upscale without excess: Hotel Granvia Hiroshima, Sheraton Grand Hiroshima Hotel

**Osaka (Namba) — Sarasa Hotel Namba**
A positive surprise in the reviews, with one of the highest scores in the area: rooms larger than average, helpful staff and small extras (pajamas, slippers, smartphone loan) that raise the perceived level for the price range. In practice it's already a mid-range choice, convenient for Namba and the Den Den Town district.
*Alternatives:* budget: Khaosan World Namba, First Cabin Namba · mid-range: Namba Oriental Hotel, Hotel Hokke Club Namba · upscale without excess: Swissotel Nankai Osaka, Hotel Nikko Osaka

**Tokyo (Ikebukuro) — Sunshine City Prince Hotel**
A well-located tower hotel, connected directly to the Sunshine City mall: convenient on rainy days for eating, shopping or getting around without going outside. Cordial staff and a facility rated "very good" in the reviews; the rooms are adequate but not generous, as in most Tokyo hotels.
*Alternatives:* budget: Toyoko Inn Ikebukuro-Eki Kita-guchi, Centurion Hotel Ikebukuro · mid-range: Hotel Grand City Ikebukuro, Mitsui Garden Hotel Ikebukuro Premier · upscale without excess: Hotel Metropolitan Tokyo Ikebukuro, Hotel Century Southern Tower

## Practical tips
- **JR Pass**: activate it only from Day 4 on (you need your passport, and you have to allow for the queue at the counter); before then you travel paying for single JR stretches.
- **Shipping heavy luggage** from one city to another (takkyubin, a service available at hotels or at 7-Eleven) saves the day when you change city and have many stops. It has to be arranged the evening before.
- **In temples**: always bring clean socks, in many temples you walk barefoot on tatami.
- **Always carry cash**: outside 7-Eleven, supermarket chains and bigger restaurants, cards are still not widespread.
- **Small restaurants**: if you don't speak Japanese, Google Translate with the offline dictionary and Google Lens to translate handwritten menus save the situation.

## Chiusura personale
From a trip this dense I take home above all the contrasts: the sakura chased across Tokyo, Fuji seen from Chureito with the cherry trees still in full bloom a week later, the Nara deer wandering free among the temples, and the nights between crowded izakaya and Shibuya Crossing. If I had to choose a single moment, I'd choose the one at the Chureito Pagoda: the postcard Japan that for once is really like that in person too.
