# Central America in 32 Days: Guatemala and Belize Overland, Panama by Separate Flight

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 in progress (itinerary and practical information complete and verified)

## Apertura personale
Guatemala and Belize are the part of Central America that truly borders: one continuous overland stretch, with a border open every day at Melchor de Mencos, that takes you from the volcanoes of the Sierra Madre to the second-largest barrier reef in the world in a little over an hour of driving. Panama is a different story, and it's better to say so right away instead of pretending it's a logistical detail: between the Guatemalan Petén (or southern Belize) and the province of Chiriquí lie Honduras, El Salvador, Nicaragua and Costa Rica, four countries this site doesn't cover and that aren't part of this itinerary. There is no sensible way to cover that distance overland within a single reasonable trip: the honest choice is to fly, and this itinerary treats it for exactly what it is: not a continuum, but two trips stitched together by a plane ticket. **Part 1**, twenty-two days, is a real overland loop: from Antigua and its volcanoes to Lake Atitlán, from the pools of Semuc Champey to the temples of Tikal in the Petén jungle, all the way to the islands of Belize by way of the most impressive cave in Mesoamerica. **Part 2**, ten days, is Panama: the capital with its canal, the self-governed islands of the Guna in San Blas, the coffee and the two oceans of Boquete, the Caribbean of Bocas del Toro. It's shorter than the first, and that deserves the same honesty with which elsewhere on this site destinations have been left out for reasons of season or route: not because there's less to see, but because four destinations make a complete shorter trip, not a half-finished one of nine.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** December-April is the dry season common to all stops but one. Bocas del Toro follows a Caribbean microclimate of its own, often sunnier in September-October and in February-March: planning it on the calendar of the rest of the country is one of the most common mistakes of people traveling in Panama.
- **Documents:**
  - **Guatemala:** passport with at least 6 months of remaining validity. No visa for Italian and EU citizens for up to 90 days. Watch out for a little-known rule: you cannot bring more than 10,000 USD (or equivalent) in cash into the country.
  - **Belize:** valid passport, visa-free entry for up to 30 days. Departure tax on exit, normally included in flights but paid separately when leaving overland; it doesn't affect this itinerary, which leaves by air from Belize City.
  - **Panama:** valid passport (at least 6 months remaining recommended), no visa for up to 90 days. A departure ticket and proof of funds may be requested on entry.
- **Currency:**
  - **Guatemala:** Quetzal (GTQ), roughly €1 = 8-9 GTQ.
  - **Belize:** Belize dollar (BZD), pegged 2:1 to the US dollar: you can travel in USD without changing anything, getting change in BZD.
  - **Panama:** the balboa exists only as coins; in practice you pay and get change in US dollars.
- **Payments:** cards are accepted at hotels and tourist restaurants in all three main stops (Antigua, San Pedro, Casco Viejo), but cash remains essential for lanchas, tuk tuks, markets, shuttles and above all in **San Blas**, where there are no ATMs or electronic payments of any kind.
- **Connectivity:** Tigo in Guatemala, Digi/Smart in Belize, +Móvil/Tigo in Panama. eSIMs are available in all three stops and are the most convenient way to avoid registering a SIM with your passport at every border. Weak or no signal across much of San Blas and in some inland areas of each country.
- **Health:** no mandatory vaccinations in the three countries except yellow fever for those arriving from at-risk areas. Hepatitis A and typhoid recommended everywhere; malaria prophylaxis concerns the low-lying areas of the Guatemalan Petén, to be assessed with a travel medicine clinic. Always bottled water (except in Panama City, where tap water is generally drinkable: an exception in the region).
- **Safety:** the classic tourist routes in the three countries are normally quiet; the real risk is concentrated in big cities outside tourist neighborhoods (Guatemala City, Belize City in the evening, the El Chorrillo and Calidonia neighborhoods in Panama City) and in overnight overland transfers, which should be avoided. The Darién border in Panama stays entirely outside this itinerary and is to be avoided in any case.
- **Getting around:** tourist shuttles and chicken buses in Guatemala; buses, water taxis and domestic flights (Tropic Air, Maya Island Air) in Belize; metro, Uber and Air Panama domestic flights in Panama. Overland travel times almost always exceed what maps estimate, especially on the unpaved roads of the Petén and Alta Verapaz.
- **Electricity:** 110-120V, 60Hz, type A and B sockets in all three countries: the standard doesn't change throughout the trip, but you still need an adapter from Italy.
- **Time zone:** Guatemala and Belize are both UTC-6 year-round; Panama is UTC-5. One hour ahead of the previous two stops, to keep in mind when fitting in the Part 2 flight.
- **Holidays:** Antigua's Semana Santa (March-April) is among the most famous processions in Latin America, with much higher prices and crowds. Garifuna Settlement Day (November 19) is the most heartfelt holiday on Belize's southern coast. Panama's Carnival (February or March) shuts the country down for four days.
- **The Belize-Panama flight:** direct **Copa Airlines** connection between Belize City (BZE) and Panama City-Tocumen (PTY), about 2h30 of flight, the only airline on the non-stop route. At the time of checking it operates only **two days a week** (Tuesday and Friday): the dates of this hinge have to be fitted around those flights, checking the updated schedule before booking, or you can go through a connection (San Salvador or Guatemala City) with more flexibility but longer travel times.
- **Two-part structure:** this itinerary is written as a single thirty-two-day story, but Part 2 (Panama, 10 days) also works well as a trip of its own, shorter and more focused than Part 1: see the transition note between Day 22 and Day 23.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Arrival in Guatemala City and Transfer to Antigua
**Part 1: Guatemala and Belize, the overland stretch (22 days).** We landed at La Aurora International Airport (GUA) in Guatemala City, the air hub of the whole trip. It doesn't pay to stay: the capital isn't the reason you come to Guatemala, and the transfer to **Antigua** (shuttle or private taxi, about an hour's drive) takes you straight to the right place to settle in. Antigua is the logistical base for almost the entire country: this is where you book the treks, the shuttles for the next stops, the Spanish courses. We arrived in the early afternoon, with time for a walk among the cobblestone streets and the volcanoes Agua, Fuego and Acatenango closing the horizon from every corner of the historic center. We spent the first evening getting the gear ready for the Acatenango trek, which starts two days later: you need real thermal layers, because the cold at the high-altitude camp surprises anyone who arrives thinking of the tropics.

### Day 2 — Antigua Guatemala: Santa Catalina Arch and Cerro de la Cruz
We devoted the day to the historic center, a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The **Santa Catalina Arch**, with Volcán de Agua framed in the background, is the most photographed image in Guatemala; the ruins of the Convent of Santa Clara and the La Merced church tell of the earthquakes that bent the city more than once. We climbed **Cerro de la Cruz** for the view over Antigua and the volcanoes: best in a group or during the hours patrolled by the tourist police, never alone outside those windows. In the afternoon, the municipal market and the handicraft market; for those who want an extra taste, there's a chocolate class at one of the schools downtown. A light dinner and early to bed: the next day's alarm is very early, with a heavy pack and six hours of climbing ahead.

### Day 3 — Climbing Acatenango Volcano
We began the two-day trek up **Acatenango volcano** (3,976 m / 13,044 ft). You climb for 5-6 hours through cornfields and then sparse pine forest, with over 1,500 meters (about 4,900 ft) of elevation gain on volcanic sand that gives way under your feet: the hardest stretch of the whole trip, altitude included. The camp sits above the clouds, with a direct view of nearby **Fuego volcano**, one of the most active in the world. You sleep in a tent or in the base hut, have dinner at camp and then the wait begins: Fuego erupts at regular intervals, sometimes several times an hour, and the first explosions arrive even before sunset. You need real thermal layers, not a sweatshirt: the night at altitude gets close to freezing even in the tropics.

### Day 4 — Fuego Erupting and the Descent from Acatenango
The night at camp is the part that stays most vivid from all of Guatemala: Fuego erupting lava a couple of kilometers away, visible without even getting out of the sleeping bag. Those with the legs for it join a supplementary hike to the base of Fuego, closer and more intense. In the morning we went down in 3-4 hours along the same trail as the ascent, this time sliding down the volcanic sand more than walking. Back to **Antigua** the same day, a hot shower and a free afternoon to recover: your legs make themselves felt the next day, but the list of things done in Central America starts right here.

### Day 5 — Chichicastenango and Lake Atitlán
We moved to **Chichicastenango**, about three hours by shuttle from Antigua. The day has to be chosen carefully, because the largest indigenous market in Central America fills the town only on **Thursday and Sunday**: outside those two days Chichi is a quiet, not very interesting town, and you need to know it in time when you set the dates of the trip. Among the stalls of textiles, masks and ceramics, and the food section in the side streets where you eat standing at the counters, what stays with you most is the staircase of the **Santo Tomás** church, where to this day copal and offerings are burned according to K'iche' rites on the steps of a Catholic building. It should be watched with respect, without photographing people or rituals without permission. In the late afternoon we continued toward **Panajachel**, on Lake Atitlán, about an hour and a half away.

### Day 6 — Panajachel and Lake Atitlán
It was the first day on **Lake Atitlán**, inside a volcanic caldera surrounded by three cones (Atitlán, Tolimán, San Pedro) and about ten Maya villages very different from one another. Panajachel is the transport hub and the place to get your bearings, with Calle Santander full of handicrafts, but it isn't the most interesting village on the lake: the most common mistake is to stop only there. In the afternoon we made our first **lancha** crossing to the other shore, just to get the rhythm of the public boats that connect the villages until sunset. Good to know for planning the next two days: from early afternoon the **Xocomil** wind picks up, which makes the crossings rougher, so outings should be scheduled in the morning.

### Day 7 — Village-Hopping on Lake Atitlán by Lancha
We devoted the whole day to touring the villages, the right way to understand the lake. The public lanchas take you from one town to another in ten to forty minutes: **San Juan la Laguna**, the most polished village, with its textile cooperatives and murals; **San Marcos la Laguna**, the lake's yoga and holistic hub, with the Cerro Tzankujil diving platform; **San Pedro la Laguna**, among the cheapest places in Latin America for a Spanish course. The atmosphere changes every ten minutes by boat, and that's what sets Lake Atitlán apart from the rest of Guatemala. Departures should be concentrated in the morning, before the Xocomil wind makes the afternoon crossings uncomfortable; you need cash in small-denomination quetzales to pay for the lanchas on board.

### Day 8 — Sunrise from the Indian Nose and Santiago Atitlán
We woke at four for the guided climb to the **Indian Nose**, the viewpoint that dominates the entire caldera. It's worth the early alarm only with a clear sky, because in the rainy season you climb in the dark to look at a cloud: a detail to factor in before booking. With the right weather, the sunrise over the lake and the three volcanoes is among the most beautiful views in all of Guatemala. For the rest of the morning we took the lancha to **Santiago Atitlán**, the largest and most traditional village on the lake, where the women still wear the tocoyal wrapped around their heads and the syncretic figure of **Maximón** is venerated. The afternoon was free for a last walk in Panajachel, before the long transfer the next day toward Alta Verapaz.

### Day 9 — Toward Lanquín and Semuc Champey
It was almost entirely a transfer day, the longest overland day of the whole trip: from Panajachel toward **Lanquín**, in the Alta Verapaz region, partly retracing the road toward the capital before heading north to Cobán. It's roughly 7-9 hours by tourist shuttle, with the last stretch on a dirt road that slowly descends into the valley. It's the same kind of long transfer faced by those arriving from Antigua or Guatemala City to reach Semuc Champey, and it should be counted as a day devoted entirely to the road, not a "pass-through" day between two stops. We arrived in Lanquín in the late afternoon and had dinner at one of the few comedores in town: outside the hostels, the food offering here is minimal.

### Day 10 — The Pools of Semuc Champey and the K'anba Caves
We went down to **Semuc Champey**, the natural staircase of turquoise limestone pools built on a rock bridge under which the Cahabón river runs. We climbed the pools from the bottom up, then took the steep 30-45 minute climb to the **Mirador** for the view from above: the only way to understand how the place works, with the river disappearing under the rock and re-emerging three hundred meters further on. In the afternoon we entered the **K'anba caves**, explored with a candle in hand and with some stretches to swim in the dark: the least touristy and most memorable thing in the area, to be skipped only in case of real claustrophobia. Water and snacks have to be brought from outside: inside the park there's almost nothing to be found.

### Day 11 — Transfer to Flores, on Lake Petén Itzá
A transfer day heading north, into the Petén jungle: from Lanquín to **Flores**, on Lake Petén Itzá, on a tourist shuttle that covers the distance in about 9-12 hours depending on the operator. It's the only direct connection available and it's best to book it a day ahead, because in high season seats sell out fast. We arrived late in the evening on the island of Flores, connected to the mainland by a short causeway: colorful houses, a quiet lakefront, the atmosphere of a town that lives almost solely off tourism to Tikal. A light dinner and early to bed, because the next day's alarm is in the dead of night, for sunrise in the most famous jungle in Mesoamerica.

### Day 12 — Sunrise at Tikal from Temple IV
We left the hotel around 3 a.m., with about 45 minutes of driving to the entrance of **Tikal** park. The sunrise ticket (in addition to the regular one, for a total of about 250 GTQ, cash required) grants access before the official 6 a.m. opening: you walk in the dark toward **Temple IV**, the tallest in the site, with the mandatory guide in that time slot. More than for the sunrise itself, often veiled by dry-season haze, it's worth it for the soundscape: the chorus of howler monkeys and birds waking at first light, before the buses arrive. For the rest of the day we visited the Gran Plaza, Temples I and II and the Mundo Perdido, walking for kilometers under the canopy of the living forest: that's what sets Tikal apart from the other Maya sites.

### Day 13 — The Melchor de Mencos Border and Arrival in San Ignacio
The last day in Guatemala. From Flores you go by shuttle or colectivo to the **Melchor de Mencos** border (open daily from 6 a.m. to 10 p.m.), about two hours away. Crossing on foot is simple, a few minutes between the two passport controls, but it's also the point of the trip where it pays to keep your guard highest: around the customs offices there are unofficial money changers and a few taxi drivers who insist the colectivo stop is "too far," to convince you to pay for a private ride. The defense is the usual one: change money only at official counters, ignore anyone who spontaneously offers to help in exchange for something, and take the green-plated colectivo taxis just outside the checkpoint to **San Ignacio**, in Belize, for a few dollars a trip. We settled in town: first contact with English and with Belize's higher prices.

### Day 14 — The ATM Cave (Actun Tunichil Muknal)
We devoted the day to the **ATM cave (Actun Tunichil Muknal)**, the most impressive thing in all of Belize. You reach it with a jungle hike and three river crossings, then you swim into an underground river and continue for hours in cold water, through passages where you have to turn sideways. In the upper chamber you take off your shoes to walk in socks among intact pottery and human remains left exactly where the Maya placed them over a thousand years ago, including the famous "Crystal Maiden." **No photography**: cameras, phones included, have been banned since an accidental fall damaged an artifact, and access is only possible with authorized guides by reservation. It isn't an experience for those who suffer from claustrophobia or have trouble with cold water: for those who prefer to stay outdoors there's Xunantunich, planned for the next day.

### Day 15 — Xunantunich and the Mountain Pine Ridge
A lighter day in the Cayo district. In the morning we went to **Xunantunich**, a Maya site reached by a small hand-cranked ferry across the Mopan river: a detail that's as much a part of the experience as the ruins. In the afternoon we detoured toward the **Mountain Pine Ridge**: the natural pools of **Rio On**, among large granite boulders, and the Green Hills Butterfly Ranch. For those who want to stay an extra day, the excursion to **Caracol**, the largest Maya site in Belize, inside the Chiquibul Forest Reserve, also departs from here; here it stays an optional extra, so as not to stretch an already dense itinerary further. A free evening in San Ignacio, where the Saturday market (if the dates coincide) is one of the best and cheapest places to eat in all of Belize.

### Day 16 — Toward the Southern Coast: Placencia
We moved toward the southern coast of mainland Belize: from San Ignacio to **Placencia**, along the Hummingbird Highway and then the Southern Highway, about 4-5 hours by bus or shuttle. Alternatively, to save time, there's a domestic flight from Belize City. Placencia sits on a thin sand peninsula, with the **Placencia Sidewalk** crossing the entire village on foot: it's the first place on the trip with postcard Caribbean sand, which on the northern islands, as you'll find out a few days later, is almost entirely missing. We settled in town, with time for a first walk and a swim before sunset.

### Day 17 — Placencia and the Placencia Sidewalk
We devoted the day to Placencia, with a boat trip to **Laughing Bird Caye**, a national park island off the peninsula, with shallow water and reefs close to the coast. For those traveling between March and June, around the full moons, there's the chance (never guaranteed) to go out snorkeling with **whale sharks** at Gladden Spit: an activity to treat as a seasonal bonus, not to build your planning around. In the afternoon, free time on the Sidewalk among grilled-fish restaurants and conch ceviche, with the usual price difference between the beachfront and the inner streets. For those who want it, the **Cockscomb Basin Wildlife Sanctuary**, the world's first reserve dedicated to jaguars, is a separate half-day trek, optional.

### Day 18 — Hopkins and Garifuna Culture
We moved to **Hopkins**, a Garifuna village on the coast, less than an hour from Placencia. The Garifuna descend from Africans who were never enslaved, deported here from the eastern Caribbean, with a language, cuisine and drumming of their own recognized by UNESCO. A **drum lesson** with a master from the village is the activity not to skip, and it says more than any museum. At the table the dish to look for is **hudut** (fish in coconut milk with mashed green plantain), served mostly at family restaurants. The rest of the day is free, between the village beach and an aimless walk: after the most intense days of Guatemala and the Cayo, Hopkins is designed precisely as a decompression day.

### Day 19 — Toward Belize City and Caye Caulker
We headed back north: from Hopkins to **Belize City**, about 3 hours along the Hummingbird and George Price Highways, then the **water taxi** from the downtown terminal to **Caye Caulker**, a crossing of about 45 minutes. Belize City requires caution, especially in the evening: the recurring advice is to use taxis instead of walking, but in this itinerary it remains only a pass-through point between a bus and a dock. We reached Caye Caulker in the afternoon: there are no cars on the island, only golf carts and bicycles, and the motto written everywhere, **Go Slow**, should be taken literally. In two days the pace of the trip slows on its own.

### Day 20 — Caye Caulker: Hol Chan and Shark Ray Alley
We took a half-day boat outing to **Hol Chan Marine Reserve**, the natural channel where the reef opens up and fish of every size, turtles and barracuda gather, and to **Shark Ray Alley**, where nurse sharks and rays have been approaching boats for decades: half an hour offshore to be in the water a meter from them, without them paying the slightest attention to those watching. Good to know before arriving: the coast of Caye Caulker is mostly mangroves and piers, without the classic Caribbean beaches, and anyone expecting them is thrown for half a day before understanding that here you swim offshore. The afternoon stayed free until sunset at **The Split**, the channel opened by a hurricane, the gathering spot for the whole island.

### Day 21 — Ambergris Caye and the Great Blue Hole
We moved by boat to **Ambergris Caye**, the biggest and most developed island in Belize, with **San Pedro** as its center: more restaurants, more dive centers, more golf carts. This is the day you decide how to approach the **Great Blue Hole**. The full-day dive (three dives, 300-400 USD, advanced certification often required) shows a blue wall with few stalactites and little fish, while the famous photo of the perfect circle can only be seen from above: a scenic flight costs much less and is truer to what you imagined. For those who stay on the water, **Secret Beach** on the west side of the island, with very shallow water, is the simpler alternative. Last Caribbean evening before the jump to Panama.

### Day 22 — Last Day on the Islands and Transfer to Belize City
The morning on Ambergris Caye stayed free (one last swim, one last seaweed shake on the waterfront) before the transfer to **Belize City**, ahead of the next day's flight. Those who prefer to avoid the morning water taxi can fly directly from San Pedro to Belize City with Tropic Air or Maya Island Air: fifteen minutes that save time and nerves. We slept near the **Philip S. W. Goldson (BZE)** international airport, from which the direct flight to Panama City departs. And so Part 1 closes: twenty-two days, two countries connected by a single overland border, from erupting volcanoes to the barrier reef, without a single intercontinental flight in between.

**Those with three weeks stop here, and it's a complete trip: twenty-two days across Guatemala and Belize, from the erupting volcanoes of Acatenango to the ATM cave, from the temples of Tikal to the barrier reef of Caye Caulker, one continuous overland stretch without a single unnecessary intercontinental flight.** Those who continue leave Maya Central America for Panamanian Central America, and it's worth being clear about what that means: you don't continue overland, because between here and Panama lie four countries (Honduras, El Salvador, Nicaragua, Costa Rica) that this itinerary doesn't touch and for which there is no sensible overland connection within a single trip. **The connection is a direct Belize City-Panama City flight** with Copa Airlines (about 2h30, the only airline on the non-stop route), which however operates only two days a week, Tuesday and Friday: the dates of this hinge have to be fitted around those flights, or you go through a connection (San Salvador or Guatemala City) with more flexibility but longer travel times. **The ten days of Panama that follow also work well as a trip of their own, shorter and more focused than the first part: four destinations instead of nine, without claiming to be a second trip as complete as the first.**

### Day 23 — The Flight to Panama City
**Part 2: Panama, a jump by air (10 days).** From Belize City airport we boarded the direct **Copa Airlines CM777** flight to **Panama City-Tocumen (PTY)**, about 2 and a half hours of flight. The operating days (currently Tuesday and Friday) should be checked in advance, before setting the dates for this stop. The time zone changes by an hour (Panama is UTC-5, one hour ahead of Guatemala and Belize), the currency only on paper: you go from the quetzal and the Belize dollar to the balboa, which in practice is the US dollar. From the airport, a taxi or Uber to the **Casco Viejo**, the colonial old town: first contact with a skyline of skyscrapers unlike anything seen in the previous three weeks. A quiet evening, with dinner at one of the rooftops in the old town.

### Day 24 — Panama City: Casco Viejo and the Miraflores Locks
We devoted the day to the capital. In the morning we went to the **Miraflores Locks** on the Panama Canal, and it's worth checking ship transit times in advance: without a ship maneuvering you're looking at an empty concrete basin, while a container ship entering with a few centimeters of margin on each side is worth the detour on its own. In the afternoon, the ruins of **Panamá Viejo**, the original city destroyed by pirates in 1671, and a walk through the **Casco Viejo** between Plaza de la Independencia and the colonial churches: the most interesting thing to look at is the sharp contrast between restored buildings and facades still propped up. We had lunch at the **Mercado de Mariscos** on the bay, where a ceviche in a plastic cup costs a couple of dollars. Good to know: outside the tourist zones of the Casco, it's best not to walk around after sunset.

### Day 25 — Toward San Blas: The Cartí Crossing
We woke around 5 for the most demanding transfer of all of Part 2: about 2h30-3h by **4x4** on a mountain road with steep grades to the port of **Cartí**, then the boat to the islands of the **San Blas (Guna Yala)** archipelago. It's an indigenous territory self-governed by the **Guna** people, not run by tourist chains: at the entrance you pay a community fee of about 20 USD per person (plus 2-5 USD at the port and sometimes a supplement for a specific island), **cash only**, because on the islands there are no ATMs or card payments. We arrived at a cabaña: deliberately basic lodgings (electricity on a schedule, rationed fresh water, almost no wifi), run directly by Guna families, with meals included in the package. The afternoon is free among shallow turquoise water and palms, to understand what you've embarked on.

### Day 26 — A Full Day Among the Islands of Guna Yala
We spent the whole day by boat among the islets of the archipelago: **Isla Perro**, with a wreck reachable by snorkeling, **Isla Aguja**, the **Cayos Holandeses** and the shallow natural pools in the middle of the open sea. In the Guna villages you can buy **molas**, the traditional hand-sewn textiles. Before photographing people or homes you must always ask permission, and you don't take coconuts or natural objects from the islands: these are community rules to be respected without exception. Know that scuba diving is banned by decision of the Sailas, the traditional Guna chiefs, to protect the reef: surface snorkeling only. Dinner of fish and, in season, lobster, still included in the package, before the last night on the island.

### Day 27 — Back to Panama City and the Flight to David
We returned by boat to Cartí and then by 4x4 to **Panama City**, retracing the mountain road in reverse: count on half a day of transfer alone. In the early afternoon we took the **Air Panama** domestic flight from Panama City to **David** (about an hour), the main hub of the Chiriquí province, and from there 40 minutes by car or bus to **Boquete**. The change of climate is the most immediate relief of all of Part 2: after the humid heat of the islands, in Boquete you sleep with the window open and no fan, something that hardly ever happens elsewhere on the trip. We settled in town and had dinner at one of the cafés that roast the local coffee on site.

### Day 28 — Boquete: Coffee and the Sendero Los Quetzales
A day devoted to the Chiriquí highlands, without the forced pace of the night climb planned for the next day. We took a tour of a **coffee finca** with a tasting: the area produces the prized **Geisha**, which breaks world price records at auction and is tasted here at a fraction of the cost elsewhere. For those who want to hike there's the **Sendero Los Quetzales**, one of the best places in the world to spot the quetzal, best with a local guide who knows the right times and spots. In the late afternoon, rest and preparing real winter gear (a sweatshirt isn't enough) for the midnight start toward Volcán Barú: the summit is reached before dawn, with wind and temperatures close to freezing.

### Day 29 — Sunrise Over Two Oceans from Volcán Barú
We set off around midnight for the climb of **Volcán Barú** (3,475 m / 11,401 ft), the highest point in Panama: about 13 km of dirt track each way, doable entirely on foot (10-12 hours round trip) or partly by **4x4**, for those who don't want to spend the whole night walking. With a perfectly clear sky, which in practice happens only on a few good days even in the dry season, from the summit you can see the **Pacific Ocean** and the **Caribbean Sea** together: it's one of the very few places in the world where that happens. Even without that luck, the night climb at altitude with the valleys lit up below remains a moment worth the effort. Descent and return to Boquete in late morning, with the rest of the day free to recover.

### Day 30 — From Boquete to Bocas del Toro
We moved toward the Caribbean: from Boquete you go down to **David** and from there cross the Cordillera toward **Almirante**, on the Caribbean coast, then board a **water taxi** of about 30 minutes to **Bocas Town**, on Isla Colón. It's an overland and sea route that avoids going back all the way to Panama City. Alternatively, those with a tight budget and time fly directly from David to Bocas del Toro. We arrived in the afternoon: houses on stilts, an atmosphere much more social and noisy than San Blas, hostels and people who almost always stay longer than planned. First stroll through Bocas Town, among bars over the water and Caribbean cooking: fish and chicken in coconut sauce, patacones everywhere.

### Day 31 — Bocas del Toro: Cayo Zapatilla and Starfish Beach
We took a day boat tour among the islets of the archipelago: **Cayo Zapatilla**, often the most beautiful spot of the whole tour, and **Starfish Beach**, which lives up to its name with starfish visible in water barely over a meter deep. Snorkeling stops at **Cayo Coral** and the chance to spot dolphins along the way, weather permitting: outings to the outer cayos get canceled when the sea is rough, and that's a real possibility to factor in. Beware of sands infested with **chitre**, midges that bite without you feeling them and leave marks for days: you need a real repellent, not the one for ordinary mosquitoes. We spent the evening in Bocas Town, among live music and parties that often move by boat to the nearby islands.

### Day 32 — Last Morning in Bocas Town and Departure
A last free morning in **Bocas Town**: a stroll through the fish market, one last swim or a surf lesson at Playa Bluff for anyone who hasn't tried it yet. Good to know before planning the return: Bocas del Toro follows a **microclimate of its own**, often sunnier in September-October and in February-March than in the rest of Panama, so the national "dry season" shouldn't be taken for granted here. The same-day transfer to **Panama City-Tocumen (PTY)** is done by Air Panama domestic flight (about an hour) or overland to Almirante and then by bus to the capital, depending on the connections available on the day of the international return. And so ends a trip in two parts (twenty-two days overland across Guatemala and Belize, ten days of Panama reached by air) designed to be honest about its own geography.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **Guatemala:** Antigua, historic center, as a base for the city and for Acatenango (plus one night in a tent or hut at the high-altitude camp); Panajachel for Lake Atitlán; Lanquín for Semuc Champey; the island of Flores for Tikal.
- **Belize:** San Ignacio for the ATM cave and the rest of the Cayo; Placencia village for the beach and Hopkins for Garifuna culture; Caye Caulker and Ambergris Caye/San Pedro for the islands.
- **Panama:** Casco Viejo in Panama City, for the atmosphere and evening life; a cabaña run by a Guna family in San Blas; Boquete town for the highlands; Bocas Town on Isla Colón for the Caribbean.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **Guatemala:** pepián, the national stew with roasted seed sauce, in Antigua; Guatemalan coffee roasted on site; the municipal market's menú del día for a few quetzales; the white fish of Lake Petén Itzá in Flores.
- **Belize:** rice and beans with stewed meat everywhere, lobster in season (mid-June to February) and conch ceviche on the islands; Garifuna hudut in Hopkins; the evening market and fish stalls in San Pedro, much cheaper than the waterfront.
- **Panama:** ceviche in a cup at the Mercado de Mariscos in Panama City; sancocho, the national soup, and carimañolas as street food; fish and lobster included in the San Blas packages; Boquete's Geisha coffee; fish and chicken in coconut sauce and patacones in Bocas del Toro.

## Practical tips
- **Certified guides, always.** For Acatenango, for the ATM cave and for San Blas: they aren't formalities, they're safety and respect for local rules.
- **The Chichicastenango market is only on Thursday and Sunday.** Fit the trip dates around these two days, not the other way around.
- **Beware of money changers and fake taxis at the Melchor de Mencos border:** change money only at official counters, ignore anyone who offers to help unasked, take only green-plated colectivo taxis.
- **No photography inside the ATM cave:** cameras and phones are banned without exception.
- **San Blas is self-governed Guna territory:** you pay in cash, there are no ATMs, scuba diving is banned and the rules (photos, natural souvenirs) must be respected without argument.
- **The Belize City-Panama City flight operates only two days a week** (Copa Airlines, Tuesday and Friday): check the updated schedule before setting the dates of the hinge between the two parts.
- **Belize costs almost double Guatemala and more than Panama:** budget for it, especially for diving and domestic flights.
- **Panama is paid for entirely in US dollars**, while Guatemala and Belize require cash in local currency for markets, lanchas and shuttles.
- **Bocas del Toro has a different microclimate from the rest of Panama:** don't rely on the national dry season alone to plan it.
- **Two-part structure:** this itinerary also works split in two, see the note between Day 22 and Day 23.

## Chiusura personale
What remains of this trip is, first of all, the contrast between the two parts, and it's a deliberate contrast, not a compromise. The first, across Guatemala and Belize, is a true overland stretch: you sleep above the clouds watching a volcano erupt, you swim in a cave with intact Maya artifacts, you walk beneath temples rising out of the jungle, you end up barefoot on an island where cars don't go. The second, Panama, is shorter and it is so for an honest geographical reason, not for lack of things to see: a jump by air that takes you to a different country, with its islands self-governed by the Guna, the world's priciest coffee grown a stone's throw from where you can see two oceans together, and one last Caribbean dip in Bocas del Toro. Putting together three countries that the rest of the region doesn't connect overland meant making an explicit choice (a flight instead of an invented continuum), but that was exactly the point: to tell Central America as it really is, not as it would be most convenient to tell it.
