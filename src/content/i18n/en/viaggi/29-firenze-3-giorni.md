# Florence in 3 Days (and the 2-Day Version That Holds Up)

**Periodo del viaggio:**
**Durata:** 3 days, 2 nights — with a compressed 2-day version
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independent, everything on foot
**Categoria:** Europa · Italia · Firenze · arte · Rinascimento · gastronomia

## Apertura personale
Florence is the most concentrated art city in Europe, and that is both its strength and its trap. You can walk across the historic center in twenty-five minutes, and inside those twenty-five minutes are the Uffizi, the David, Brunelleschi's dome, the Baptistery, Santa Croce, the Bargello and Palazzo Vecchio. It sounds easy. It isn't: the concentration is the problem, because three museums of that caliber in one day is too much, and after the fourth masterpiece you literally stop seeing.

Three days is the right measure: **one big museum a day, not two**, and the rest of the time spent walking. The rule that saves Florence is the opposite of efficiency: leave gaps in the schedule.

The other thing to know before leaving is that **the Duomo complex and the state museums close on different days**, and fitting them together badly is the most common planning mistake in this city: you show up on a Monday morning in front of a closed Uffizi and lose a third of your trip.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** **April-May and October-November.** Summer is hot, the center is packed and the lines become unbearable. Winter is cold and damp, but much emptier, and Florence in winter, with few people, is a different city. Avoid August.
- **Essential reservations:**
  - **Brunelleschi's Dome:** **you can only climb with a mandatory timed reservation**, included in a combined ticket for the Duomo complex. In high season the slots run out days ahead.
  - **Uffizi Gallery:** a reserved slot is strongly recommended; without one, the line lasts hours.
  - **Accademia Gallery** (the David): same thing, and the visit is short, since you go for one statue.
- **The Duomo ticket:** entry to the **cathedral is free** (with a line, often long). **The Dome, Baptistery, Giotto's Bell Tower, the crypt of Santa Reparata and the Museo dell'Opera del Duomo are instead on a single combined ticket**, with packages and names that change over time: it's worth checking the one currently in force. The combined ticket is **valid for a set number of days** from first activation, so it's best to use it all in a block.
- **The dome, in practice:** **463 steps, no elevator, no early exit.** The last stretch runs **between the two shells of the dome**, in a very narrow, sloping corridor where people go up and down in the same space. Anyone who is claustrophobic or has knee problems should know this beforehand. In return you pass **a meter from the frescoes of the Last Judgment** and come out at the highest point in the city.
- **The Gates of Paradise:** the gilded panels by Ghiberti on the Baptistery are **copies**. **The originals are in the Museo dell'Opera del Duomo**, restored, in a climate-controlled room. That museum, which almost everyone skips because it's the last item on the ticket, is one of the best in the city.
- **Firenze Card:** a 72-hour pass that includes a great many museums and priority access. It costs quite a lot and **only pays off for people who visit many museums in three days**, which is exactly the pace advised against here. Before buying it, work out the numbers against your real schedule.
- **Car:** useless and expensive. The center is a **ZTL (limited traffic zone) with active electronic gates**, and fines arrive at your home months later. You can get around all of Florence on foot; for the surrounding area there are trains.
- **From the airport to the centre:** from **Amerigo Vespucci airport (FLR)**, at Peretola, the T2 tram reaches Piazza dell'Unità, next to Santa Maria Novella, in about 20 minutes: ticket about €2 (since 1 August 2026) from the machines at the stops or in the Autolinee Toscane app, running from about 5:00 to 0:30 · white taxis from the official rank at a fixed fare to the centre, about €22-25, more at night and on holidays · Uber exists only as a chauffeur service, more expensive · from **Pisa (PSA)** the Pisa Mover reaches Pisa Centrale in 5 minutes, then a regional train to Santa Maria Novella in about 1 hour, about €9, or direct buses to Florence in about 1h10 · anyone renting a car must stay out of the ZTL. **Santa Maria Novella** station is in the center, and with the high-speed train Rome and Milan are a little over an hour and a half away.
- **Food:** the **bistecca alla fiorentina** is a cut at least **three fingers thick**, cooked rare and **sold by weight**: asking for it well done means asking for a different dish. Then **lampredotto**, a sandwich of beef fourth stomach sold at street stands, the true Florentine street food; **schiacciata**; **ribollita** and **pappa al pomodoro**, peasant cooking made from stale bread; **cantucci with vin santo**. **Tuscan bread is unsalted** and throws you off at the first bite: it has been that way for centuries, designed to go with flavorful cured meats and soups.
- **Wine:** **Chianti Classico** (the **black rooster**, or gallo nero, consortium) is the historic area between Florence and Siena, quite different from generic "Chianti." Wineries are visited **by appointment**.
- **Hours and closures:** many **state museums close on Monday**; the Duomo complex has its own calendar, with closures for religious celebrations. Always check before building your schedule.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — The Duomo complex and the center
The first day was entirely for the Duomo, as a block, because the ticket is combined and it makes sense to use it in sequence.

You start with the **dome**, in your reserved time slot, preferably early: 463 steps, the close-up passage past Vasari and Zuccari's **Last Judgment** frescoes, and then the lantern with all of Florence below. It's worth knowing what you're climbing. When Brunelleschi won the competition, **the drum was already built and nobody knew how to cover it**: the span was too wide for the wooden centering known at the time. The solution was a **self-supporting double shell with bricks laid in a herringbone pattern**, built **without scaffolding**, and it has stood since 1436. *My memory: the dome is cramped, but it's worth it.*

Then the **Baptistery**, the oldest building on the square. Inside, the dome is covered entirely in **Byzantine mosaics on a gold ground**, with a Last Judgment and an enormous Christ, and people stay far less time than it deserves because everyone is looking at the doors. Which outside, remember, are **copies**.

**Giotto's Bell Tower** only if your legs can take a second climb: it's 414 steps, the staircase is more comfortable than the dome's, and **the advantage is that from there you can see the dome**, which obviously you can't do from the dome.

In the afternoon we went to the **Museo dell'Opera del Duomo**, the part almost everyone cuts, and that's a mistake. It has **the original Gates of Paradise**, Donatello's wooden **Magdalene** and the **Bandini Pietà**, Michelangelo's last sculpture, unfinished, which he himself attacked with a hammer and in which he probably portrayed himself as Nicodemus. There is also the full-scale reconstruction of the Duomo's medieval facade, never finished and later demolished.

We closed the day in **Piazza della Signoria** and the **Loggia dei Lanzi**, an open-air sculpture museum, free and always open, with Cellini's **Perseus** and Giambologna's **Rape of the Sabine Women**. The David in the square is a copy.

**In practice:** Brunelleschi Pass about €30, valid three days for the dome, Baptistery, Bell Tower, Museo dell'Opera and the Santa Reparata crypt, sold only on the Opera's official site · the dome time slot should be booked weeks ahead in high season · the dome and Bell Tower close a few days a year for maintenance and celebrations: check the calendar · Loggia dei Lanzi free.

### Day 2 — Uffizi and Oltrarno
We spent the morning at the **Uffizi**, with our reserved slot. They are long and crowded, and to avoid coming out wiped out you have to **decide in advance what to see** instead of walking through all of it: the Botticelli rooms with the **Primavera** and the **Birth of Venus**, Michelangelo's **Doni Tondo**, Leonardo's **Annunciation**, the Caravaggios (the **Medusa** and the **Bacchus**) and Titian's **Venus of Urbino**. Almost everyone stops in the corridor with the view over the Arno and Ponte Vecchio, and rightly so.

A light lunch, then the **Oltrarno**, the part of Florence where you can breathe.

You pass by **Ponte Vecchio**, the only bridge to survive the German retreat of 1944. The goldsmiths' shops have been there since the sixteenth century, ever since the Medici drove out the butchers because they stank beneath the **Vasari Corridor** that runs over it. On the other side you enter a genuine artisans' neighborhood, with restoration, gilding and leatherwork workshops still open onto the street.

Then **Santo Spirito**, with its bare facade and Brunelleschi's interior, and the square that in the evening is the neighborhood's living room. And the **Brancacci Chapel** at Santa Maria del Carmine, with the frescoes by **Masaccio**: in the Expulsion from Eden, Adam and Eve have an expression that did not exist in earlier painting. **Access is limited and must be reserved**, and in recent years restoration work has affected it: it's worth checking before you go.

Alternatively, or in addition, there are **Palazzo Pitti** and the **Boboli Gardens**, enormous, each worth a whole afternoon on its own: if you choose them, the rest goes.

At sunset you climb to **San Miniato al Monte**, not to Piazzale Michelangelo. It's five minutes higher up, the Romanesque church is the most beautiful in Florence, the view is the same and there's a tenth of the crowd. **The monks sing vespers in Gregorian chant in the late afternoon** (times change with the season, so it's worth checking them), and attending in that church, with the light fading, is one of the best things you can do in the city without paying anything.

**In practice:** the Uffizi is closed on Mondays, full ticket around €25-30 depending on desk, online and season, with a cheaper afternoon slot · to get in at your booked time, show up a quarter of an hour early · Brancacci Chapel by reservation only, check for works · from Ponte Vecchio to San Miniato it's about 30 minutes uphill on foot, or bus 12 or 13 · San Miniato free.

### Day 3 — The David, the Medici Chapels and the final choice
In the morning we went to the **Accademia Gallery** for the **David**, with a reservation. The visit is short and should be taken for what it is: you go in for one statue. But the layout of the **Gallery of the Prisoners** is superbly done: Michelangelo's four unfinished slaves, figures that seem to be emerging half out of the block of marble, are lined up along the corridor that leads to the David beneath his tribune, and they are worth the ticket as much as the David itself.

Then **San Lorenzo** and the **Medici Chapels**. The **New Sacristy** is entirely by Michelangelo, architecture and sculpture together, with the allegories of Day, Night, Dawn and Dusk on the sarcophagi. It's a space that makes you uneasy, and that's deliberate. Next to it is the **Laurentian Library**, with its vestibule and staircase, also by Michelangelo, one of the strangest spaces in Italian architecture.

The afternoon is your choice, depending on what's left:
- **Bargello Museum**, the sculpture museum: Donatello's **bronze David**, the first free-standing nude since antiquity, and the two panels from the 1401 competition for the Baptistery doors, Ghiberti's and Brunelleschi's, displayed side by side. In terms of quality versus crowds, it's the best museum in Florence.
- **Santa Croce**, with the tombs of Michelangelo, Galileo and Machiavelli, **Dante's empty cenotaph** (he is buried in Ravenna, and Florence has never managed to get him back) and Brunelleschi's Pazzi Chapel.
- **Palazzo Vecchio** with the **Salone dei Cinquecento** and the climb up the **Arnolfo Tower**, or the tours through the hidden passages.

The last evening, at the **Sant'Ambrogio market** or in the neighborhood, away from the central circuit.

**In practice:** the Accademia, Medici Chapels and Bargello are closed on Mondays and have shared hours and tickets since March 2026: Accademia about €16 plus booking, Medici Chapels about €11, Bargello about €12, and a six-museum pass from about €38 for 72 hours · the David must be booked, the other two usually not off-season · the Sant'Ambrogio market works in the morning, closed on Sundays.

## The Two-Day Version
Two days in Florence hold up well, on one condition: **only one big museum, not two.** The program that works is this.

- **Day 1 — Duomo.** Dome in the reserved slot, Baptistery, Museo dell'Opera del Duomo in the afternoon. Piazza della Signoria and the Loggia dei Lanzi at sunset, free and always open. Evening in the center.
- **Day 2 — Uffizi and Oltrarno.** Uffizi in the morning with the slot, lunch, Ponte Vecchio and the Oltrarno in the afternoon, **San Miniato al Monte at sunset**. Evening at Santo Spirito.
- **What you give up:** the **David** and the **Medici Chapels**. It sounds absurd, but it's the right sacrifice: the Accademia Gallery is a forty-five-minute visit that nonetheless has to be booked, fitted in and reached, and over two days it costs half a morning for a statue of which there are two copies in the open air in the city. Anyone who really can't accept it should take the **opening slot on the first day** and move the dome to the afternoon, knowing the day becomes heavy.
- **Alternative for those who have already seen Florence:** skip the Uffizi and do the **Bargello plus the Museo dell'Opera del Duomo**. They are two extraordinary museums, half empty, and together they tell the story of Florentine Renaissance better than a dash through the Uffizi in the middle of the crowd.

## What to see / what to do
- **Brunelleschi's dome**, knowing what the 463 steps mean.
- **The mosaics of the Baptistery dome**, which almost nobody looks at.
- **The Museo dell'Opera del Duomo**, with the original Gates of Paradise and the Bandini Pietà.
- **The Uffizi, deciding in advance what to see** instead of walking through all of it.
- **Michelangelo's Prisoners** at the Accademia, besides the David.
- **The Brancacci Chapel** by Masaccio, if access is open.
- **San Miniato al Monte at sunset**, with the Gregorian vespers.
- **The Bargello**, the great museum everyone skips.
- **The Loggia dei Lanzi**, open-air sculpture, and free.
- **A lampredotto at the stand**, standing up.

## Possible extensions
- **Siena and San Gimignano (+1 day):** Siena with Piazza del Campo and the striped Duomo, San Gimignano with its towers. You can reach them as a day trip, ideally with a car picked up outside the ZTL or with the buses.
- **Chianti Classico (+1-2 days):** the black rooster area between Florence and Siena, with wineries **by appointment**. It's the natural link with the itinerary on the routes of the great wines.
- **Fiesole (+half a day):** a quarter of an hour by bus from the center, with the Roman theater, the Etruscan excavations and the view over Florence from above. It's the outing the Florentines make.
- **Lucca and Pisa (+1 day):** Lucca with walls you can walk or cycle along, far more beautiful than its reputation suggests; Pisa for the Piazza dei Miracoli, which is worth it in spite of everything.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **Inside the historic center** for a short stay, even if you pay more: Florence is small, and staying outside means losing time in a city where everything is ten minutes away.
- **Oltrarno** (Santo Spirito, San Frediano) is the best choice for those who want to be in the center but outside the tourist flow: you cross a bridge and the pace changes.
- **The Santa Croce or Sant'Ambrogio area** to be close to the markets and the Florence that lives there.
- **Santa Maria Novella** is convenient for trains, and more anonymous.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **The bistecca alla fiorentina**, rare and by weight, split between two. Well done, it's no longer that dish. *My memory: lampredotto doesn't do much for me, but the bistecca is incredible. And it's hard to eat badly.*
- **Where to eat bistecca in the center, four suggestions** (picked through research, not places we went; check opening hours before going): **Buca Lapi**, Via del Trebbio 1r, in the cellars of Palazzo Antinori, open since 1880, evenings · **Trattoria Mario**, Via Rosina 2r near the Mercato Centrale, lunch only, Monday to Saturday · **Trattoria Sostanza**, Via del Porcellana 25r, tiny, book well ahead · **Il Latini**, Via dei Palchetti 6r, listed in the Michelin Guide, closed Mondays.
- **Lampredotto at the stand**, the most Florentine way to have lunch.
- **Ribollita and pappa al pomodoro**, leftover-bread cooking, better than the description lets on.
- **Schiacciata** with cured meats, and the **unsalted bread**, which at first seems wrong.
- **Cantucci and vin santo** at the end of the meal.
- **To drink:** Chianti Classico with the black rooster, and the reds of the area if you head south.
- **The markets:** the **Mercato Centrale** and especially **Sant'Ambrogio**, smaller and far less touristy.
- **General rule:** restaurants around the Duomo and the Uffizi with a menu in six languages on display are to be avoided by definition. Two blocks further on you eat better for half the price.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Book the dome before anything else**, because it's the only entry that truly sells out.
- **Check the closing days** of the Uffizi, the Accademia and the Duomo complex before building your schedule.
- **One big museum a day.** After the third masterpiece you stop seeing: Stendhal syndrome is named after an illness suffered in Florence, and it's not entirely a legend.
- **Don't climb the dome** if you're claustrophobic or have delicate knees: there are no intermediate exits.
- **Go to the Museo dell'Opera del Duomo**, which is included in the ticket and which almost everyone wastes.
- **Climb to San Miniato instead of stopping at Piazzale Michelangelo.**
- **Never bring a car into the ZTL**, not even to unload luggage, without having clarified the procedure with your lodging.
- **Don't fill three consecutive days with museums:** the gaps in the schedule are the part that makes Florence work.

## Chiusura personale
What stays with me about Florence is a question of scale. You stand at the end of the Duomo's nave, which inside is surprisingly bare, almost unadorned, and you look up at a dome that seems ordinary until you know that when it was time to build it nobody in the world knew how it was done: the drum was already there, octagonal, forty-five meters wide, and for a couple of generations Florence had its cathedral with a hole where the roof should be. Then you climb, and halfway up you realize you are inside the wall, between one shell and the other, in a cavity designed six centuries ago by a man who never fully explained how he thought he'd pull it off.

That's what stays with me, more than Botticelli's Venus: that the Florentine Renaissance wasn't an artistic movement but a technical problem solved by people who treated it as a game, and that everything else (the David, the Uffizi, Ghiberti's doors) followed from that confidence.
