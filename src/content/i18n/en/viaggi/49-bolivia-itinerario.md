# Bolivia in 19 Days: La Paz, the Salar de Uyuni, Lake Titicaca and the Amazon

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 in progress (itinerary and practical information complete and verified; the first-person passages are written as a draft to be reread and personalized)
**Periodo del viaggio:** May to October — the dry season, when the Salar de Uyuni is dry and can be crossed in full, the tracks of the Sud Lípez are passable and the nights at altitude, however freezing, don't come with rain on top
**Durata:** 19 days, 18 nights
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independently, with domestic flights, night buses, the mandatory organized 4x4 tour in the Sud Lípez and local guides in the Potosí mines and the Madidi pampas
**Categoria:** Sud America · Bolivia · alta quota · deserti di sale · avventura · cultura andina · Amazzonia

## Apertura personale
Bolivia comes in steps: you land at over four thousand meters before you've even worked out where you are, and from there your body adapts one day at a time while the landscape changes just as abruptly. The bowl of La Paz, the Lake Titicaca plateau, the blinding white of the Salar de Uyuni, the still-working mines of Potosí, the colonial baroque of Sucre and finally the humid heat of the Amazon, all in the same trip. This itinerary follows that logic of steps: it starts in La Paz and gives you time to acclimatize before heading higher, climbs to Titicaca and comes back, then tackles the real heart of the trip (the Salar and the Sud Lípez, three days by 4x4 among colored lagoons and geysers that alone justify Bolivia), drops to Potosí and Sucre for the more historical and cultural part, and ends with the sharpest break of all: a forty-five-minute flight from the four thousand meters of the altiplano to the sea-level rainforest. It's the cheapest country on the continent and, altitude aside, also one of the easiest to get around on your own; but it's also the country where conditions on the ground, from road blockades to flights canceled for weather, matter more than anywhere else: here the plan has to stay flexible.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** this itinerary follows the dry season, **May to October**: the Salar de Uyuni is dry and can be crossed far and wide, the Sud Lípez tracks are passable and the skies over the altiplano stay clear. If you want the mirror effect of the flooded Salar, go in January-March, knowing that several stops on the tour (Isla Incahuasi included) become inaccessible. The two experiences don't fit in the same trip, and this itinerary picks the dry salt flat.
- **Documents:** passport with at least 6 months of remaining validity. Italian citizens don't need a visa for tourist stays of up to 90 days, but Bolivia's entry rules have changed several times in recent years: check them again on Viaggiare Sicuro shortly before leaving. If you arrive at or leave from Copacabana and decide to extend the trip into Peru, know that the Kasani/Yunguyo crossing closes in the evening, that it now requires an online pre-registration before entering Bolivia and that the customs office closes at lunchtime. Border procedures in this area have changed recently, so check everything right before departure.
- **Currency and payments:** boliviano (BOB). In recent years the country has gone through a severe shortage of foreign currency, with parallel exchange rates and ATMs that can run dry: better to arrive with US dollars in cash in good condition and find out about the current situation before leaving. Outside hotels and restaurants in the main cities cash rules, and the Uyuni and Amazon tours are almost always paid in cash.
- **Connectivity:** Entel, Tigo and Viva are the main operators, with cheap SIMs you buy with your passport. Coverage is decent on the altiplano and in the cities, absent on the salt flat, in the Sud Lípez and in the Amazon forest: for several days of this itinerary you're offline.
- **Health and altitude:** this is the real theme of the trip. La Paz sits at about 3,640 meters, El Alto and its airport above 4,000, Potosí at 4,000, the Salar at 3,650, and the Sud Lípez climbs to almost 4,800. Arriving on an international flight and going from sea level to four thousand meters in a few hours is a real physical shock, not a figure of speech. Take the first two days in La Paz completely easy, with no trekking or demanding physical activity; the Death Road, even though it's downhill, should only be tackled after acclimatizing. For the Amazon part, in Rurrenabaque, a yellow fever vaccination is recommended.
- **Safety:** after the change of government in 2025 the political and social situation remains tense, with protests, spontaneous strikes and above all frequent **road blockades** across the country, which can isolate whole areas for days and wipe out flights and transfers. Allow generous time margins and backup plans, especially on the overland stretches of this itinerary (Uyuni-Potosí-Sucre). For the rest: watch out for theft in tourist areas, and licensed guides are mandatory in the Sud Lípez and in Rurrenabaque.
- **Getting around:** cheap but not always efficient, with long distances and roads that get much worse in the rainy season. Night buses are the norm between the main cities. Domestic flights with BoA, Amaszonas or EcoJet save whole days, but on the smaller routes (La Paz-Rurrenabaque above all) they're also the most exposed to weather cancellations. In La Paz, Mi Teleférico is the fastest, cheapest and most spectacular way to get around town.
- **Electricity:** 220V (110V in some areas), type A and C plugs: Italian two-pin plugs almost always fit.
- **Time zone:** UTC-4 all year, 6 hours behind Italy during daylight saving time, 5 in winter.
- **Holidays:** the Oruro Carnival, in February, is a UNESCO heritage event and the most spectacular festival in the country; it falls outside the window of this itinerary, designed for the dry season.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Arrival in El Alto and first contact with La Paz
We landed at El Alto (LPB), one of the highest airports in the world, at over 4,000 meters: in a few hours you go from sea level to the highest altitude of this trip, and it's a physical shock to expect, not to underestimate. By taxi or cable car you reach central **La Paz**, wedged into a canyon at 3,640 meters. *Today's program is just one thing: nothing.* You settle in around Sopocachi or San Pedro, drink lots of water, take coca leaves or tea like the locals do, and at most go for a very short walk. A light first dinner and early to bed: the first two days at high altitude are non-negotiable, however much you want to rush out and see the city.

### Day 2 — La Paz: cable cars, the Witches' Market and Valle de la Luna
The second day is still for acclimatizing, but more active. The urban cable cars of **Mi Teleférico** (red and yellow lines for the best views) are public transport and the city's best viewpoint in one, and cost a few euro cents: try them more than once, maybe all the way up to El Alto, which towers over La Paz at more than 4,000 meters. At the **Mercado de las Brujas**, the Witches' Market, you see traditional Aymara remedies, llama fetuses included: they're a real part of Andean culture, not a tourist attraction. In the afternoon we took a taxi to the **Valle de la Luna**, eroded clay formations a few kilometers from the center. *You still feel it climbing the stairs: that's normal, your body hasn't fully adapted yet.* If you happen to be in El Alto in the evening, you'll find the cholitas luchadoras matches, the wrestling of Aymara women.

### Day 3 — Tiwanaku and transfer to Copacabana
We moved on to **Copacabana**, on Lake Titicaca: about 4 hours by road, with a stop at the ruins of **Tiwanaku**, capital of a civilization that predates the Inca, on the road to the lake. The journey includes crossing the Tiquina Strait on a wooden barge, a short passage that stays with you, with the car or bus loaded aboard separately from the passengers. We reached Copacabana in the late afternoon, in time to climb **Cerro Calvario**, the hill above town, for sunset over the world's highest navigable lake. For dinner **trucha**, the lake trout, which you find almost everywhere along the waterfront.

### Day 4 — Boat to Isla del Sol and a night on the island
On a shared boat from Copacabana we went to **Isla del Sol**, where according to Inca tradition the sun and the first Inca were born: it's more beautiful and less built up than the Peruvian islands on the other side of the lake. *Better to sleep there a night instead of visiting for the day:* the tourist boats all arrive and leave together, and as soon as they're gone the island goes quiet again. We walked along the island's ridge, always uphill at almost 4,000 meters and always with a backpack (it's no ordinary stroll), among terraces, stone paths and the ruins of **Chincana** and the **Roca Sagrada** at the northern end. We slept in a simple place with no heating: the nights stay cold all year.

### Day 5 — Isla del Sol at dawn and back to La Paz
The morning on the island is free, with the advantage of having slept there: empty paths, low light, not a boat in sight yet. Late in the morning we returned to Copacabana and from there overland to **La Paz**, again crossing the Tiquina Strait. *Tensions between the island's communities have at times limited access to the northern part: better to ask on the spot before planning the whole ridge route.* Free evening in La Paz, to recover from the walk and get ready for the most physical day of the trip, the next one.

### Day 6 — The Death Road by mountain bike
It's the most iconic activity in Bolivia: the mountain bike descent along the old Yungas road, the "most dangerous road in the world" until 2006. Today it's a cycling route of about 64 km and over 3,400 meters of descent, from the glacial pass of **La Cumbre** (4,650 m) to the subtropical forest of **Yolosa** (1,200 m), crossing three climate zones in a few hours. *Here the price of the tour isn't about comfort but about safety:* almost all reported accidents involve cheap operators with poorly maintained bikes and groups that are too big. It's worth spending the $120-170 for a premium operator (the benchmark is still Gravity Bolivia, which invented these tours in 1998) instead of saving 50 dollars on safety. We got back to La Paz in the late afternoon, legs finished and a full change of clothes necessary after the mud of the lower section.

### Day 7 — El Alto and the night bus to Uyuni
A transition day in **La Paz**, also useful for shaking off the previous day's fatigue. If the calendar lands on a Thursday or a Sunday, the El Alto market, one of the biggest in South America, is worth the detour. In the afternoon you pack for the three days of 4x4: heavy layers, because in the Sud Lípez you sleep below zero and a sleeping bag isn't always included in the tour. In the evening there's the **night bus** to **Uyuni**, a cheaper alternative to the direct flight, which covers the same route in about an hour. On this distance it's worth choosing the better companies, with reclining sleeper seats so you can really sleep.

### Day 8 — Uyuni, the train cemetery and entering the Salar
In the morning we arrived in **Uyuni**. Before the tour we stopped at the **train cemetery**, on the edge of town, with rusting locomotives abandoned since the early twentieth century. In the early afternoon the three-day 4x4 tour of the **Salar de Uyuni** began: over 10,000 square kilometers of salt at 3,650 meters, where the lack of reference points cancels any sense of distance. This is the time for forced-perspective photos, the ones where a bottle in the foreground looks as tall as a distant mountain. We stopped at **Isla Incahuasi**, the rock island covered in giant cacti in the middle of the white expanse, and watched sunset over the salt flat. The first night we spent in a **salt hotel**, with walls and furniture made from blocks of the flat itself.

### Day 9 — Sud Lípez: colored lagoons and a night above 4,000 meters
The second day of the tour is the one that stays with you most: you leave the white of the salt flat for the **Sud Lípez**, a high-altitude desert toward the Chilean border. On the **Laguna Colorada**, red from microscopic algae, thousands of flamingos move in groups against an almost lunar backdrop. We passed wind-sculpted rock formations (the most photographed is the **Árbol de Piedra**) and deserts above 4,500 meters. *It's a much tougher tour than the photographs suggest:* isolated dirt tracks, constant wind and a second lodging that's bare-bones, often without heating, where you sleep fully dressed. Dinner was prepared by the driver-cook, as for the whole tour.

### Day 10 — Sol de Mañana geysers, Laguna Verde and back to Uyuni
We left while it was still dark for the **Sol de Mañana** geysers, at almost 5,000 meters: fumaroles and mud pools boiling at dawn at twenty below zero, the highest and coldest point of the whole trip. Then a dip in the **Polques hot springs**, outdoors, with the freezing air and the boiling water in full contrast. The last stop is the **Laguna Verde**, at the foot of the Licancabur volcano, with a turquoise that changes when the wind stirs up the copper sediments on the bottom. From here, those continuing to Chile head down to San Pedro de Atacama; this itinerary turns back, with a long 4x4 transfer (most of the day) to **Uyuni**, where the tour ended in the evening.

### Day 11 — From Uyuni to Potosí
Overland we reached **Potosí**, at 4,000 meters, the city that for centuries financed the Spanish empire with the silver of Cerro Rico. *Even just walking around the center, in the first hours after arriving, you feel the altitude:* better a relaxed afternoon. The first visit was the **Casa Nacional de la Moneda**, the old colonial mint, to understand what silver mining meant before going into the mine the next day. For dinner, the hearty soups typical of the altiplano, made precisely for the cold at altitude.

### Day 12 — Potosí and the mines of Cerro Rico
It's the heaviest day of the trip, not physically but emotionally: the guided visit to the still-working tunnels of **Cerro Rico**, almost always with former miners as guides. You go in with helmet, lamp and boots into narrow, hot, dusty passages, beside men who work there ten hours a day with tools from another century. *It's not a tourist attraction and shouldn't be treated as one:* it's tradition to bring a small gift (coca leaves, soft drinks, gloves), which you buy at the miners' market before heading up. The visit isn't recommended for anyone claustrophobic or with breathing problems, also because of the altitude. The afternoon is for digesting what you've seen before moving on.

### Day 13 — From Potosí to Sucre
It's about 3 hours by road to **Sucre**, the constitutional capital, a white colonial city and UNESCO World Heritage Site at 2,800 meters. It's the gentlest altitude of the whole trip, and you feel it right away: you breathe better, the climate is mild, the streets are orderly. We spent the early afternoon free in the historic center, among white facades and colonial churches; the physical relief of the lower altitude is enough on its own to justify a longer stop than planned.

### Day 14 — Sucre: colonial center and Parque Cretácico
A day devoted to Sucre: the **Casa de la Libertad**, where Bolivia's independence was signed; the covered markets, where you eat well for next to nothing (the fruit juice stall is an institution); the **Parque Cretácico**, a vertical wall with thousands of dinosaur footprints pressed into what was once a lake shore. *If the dates fall on a Sunday*, the one-hour detour to **Tarabuco** for the Sunday textile market is worthwhile, one of the most authentic in the country for Yampara weavings. Sucre also has some of the best and cheapest Spanish schools in South America: if you have extra time, you can stay for a course.

### Day 15 — From Sucre to La Paz
The flight from Sucre to **La Paz** takes about an hour (the slower but cheaper alternative is the night bus). A transition day, useful for stocking up on cash (outside La Paz and Sucre ATMs are rare) and for repacking your bag ahead of the sharpest change of the trip: the next day you leave the four thousand meters for the sea-level Amazon forest.

### Day 16 — Flight to Rurrenabaque and entering the Yacuma pampas
On a small plane we flew from La Paz to **Rurrenabaque**: about 45 minutes that alone are worth the entry into this chapter of the trip, because in under an hour you go from the four thousand meters of the altiplano to the humid Amazon heat. *Good to know beforehand: these flights are canceled often because of weather, not only in the rainy season, since low clouds are enough to stop a landing on a strip like this. Keep a spare day and don't squeeze in an important connection right after.* On arrival we went by boat along the **Rio Yacuma** to the lodge in the **pampas**, and already in the early afternoon you see the first caimans on the banks. A night at the lodge, with mosquito nets and a serious repellent, not just any one: the mosquitoes here are on another level.

### Day 17 — Yacuma pampas: caimans, capybaras and pink dolphins
We spent the whole day on the boat along the river: **caimans**, **capybaras**, monkeys on the banks and hundreds of birds, with the **pink river dolphins** surfacing now and then beside the hull. It's the stretch of the trip where you see the most animals with the least effort. *Some guides offer to pull caimans out of the water or to look for anacondas so clients can touch them: it's a practice that harms the animals, and to refuse it you only need to ask to stay and watch without intervening.* The best moment of the day is late afternoon, with sunset over the flooded grasslands and the sky reflected in the shallow water.

### Day 18 — Piranha fishing and a night caiman search, back to Rurrenabaque
In the morning there's **piranha** fishing from the boat: you catch more than you'd expect, and it's a good way to spend the hottest hours. The night before or this one you go out by boat for the **night caiman search**, with torches picking up the red of the eyes just above the waterline. In the morning you return by boat to **Rurrenabaque**. If you still have time, you can add a detour to **Madidi National Park**, the real primary forest: more tiring and with less guaranteed sightings than the pampas, but one of the most biodiverse areas on the planet. Free evening in town, on the Beni River.

### Day 19 — Return to La Paz and departure
The return flight to **La Paz** should be treated with the same caution as Day 16, because of possible weather cancellations. The last hours in the city depend on the time left (one last ride on the cable car, one last market), then the transfer to El Alto for the international flight home. Nineteen days, from the 3,640 meters of La Paz to the almost 5,000 of the Sud Lípez down to the sea level of the Amazon: the highest, cheapest and edgiest Bolivia in South America, in a single trip.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **La Paz:** Sopocachi or San Pedro, handy for restaurants, evening life and the agencies that run the Death Road and Uyuni tours.
- **Copacabana and Isla del Sol:** Copacabana for the convenience of transport and the sunset from Cerro Calvario; one night on Isla del Sol, in simple places with no heating. Sleeping there changes everything compared with a day visit.
- **Salar de Uyuni and Sud Lípez:** the first night of the tour a salt hotel, with walls and furniture made from blocks of the salt flat; the second night, in the Sud Lípez above 4,000 meters, a very basic lodging, often without heating.
- **Potosí:** in the center, near the Casa de la Moneda and the miners' market.
- **Sucre:** in the colonial historic center, the most pleasant place to stay on the whole trip.
- **Rurrenabaque and the pampas:** during the tour a lodge along the Rio Yacuma, basic but with mosquito nets; one night in town at Rurrenabaque before the return flight.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **La Paz:** salteñas mid-morning from the covered markets (a local habit, not a breakfast); hearty soups against the cold at altitude; the Sopocachi area has the most refined offering in the city.
- **Copacabana:** trucha, the lake trout, found almost everywhere along the waterfront.
- **Sud Lípez:** meals included in the tour, prepared by the driver-cook: simple, but surprisingly good given the isolation.
- **Potosí:** hearty soups, suited to four thousand meters of altitude.
- **Sucre:** the best quality of life in the country, with the covered markets and the fruit juice stall as a city institution.
- **Rurrenabaque and pampas:** meals included in the tours, simple: rice, fish, vegetables.

## Practical tips
- **Take the first two days in La Paz completely easy:** in a few hours you go from sea level to over 4,000 meters, and it's a real physical shock before it's a figure of speech.
- **Road blockades:** build them into the itinerary, don't treat them as a remote possibility. They can isolate whole areas for days and wipe out flights and transfers, especially on the overland stretches between Uyuni, Potosí and Sucre.
- **Book the Uyuni tour in advance, not on the spot:** in Uyuni the street agencies and pop-up ones offer lower prices, but they change the itinerary after payment, pack in more passengers than planned or put drivers without a license or insurance behind the wheel. Booking from La Paz or online with a verifiable agency cuts the risk a lot.
- **Don't skimp on the Death Road operator:** almost all reported accidents involve cheap operators with poorly maintained bikes; it's worth spending the $120-170 of the premium tier.
- **Flights to Rurrenabaque are often canceled for weather**, not only in the rainy season: keep a spare day both on the way there and, above all, on the way back, before an international flight.
- **Always carry cash:** outside La Paz and Sucre ATMs are rare or empty, and tours are almost always paid in cash.
- **The Potosí mines are not a tourist attraction:** approach them with respect, and skip them if you're claustrophobic or have breathing problems.
- **In the pampas, refuse practices that handle wildlife** (caimans pulled out of the water, anacondas sought out so you can touch them): just ask to stay and watch.
- **If you extend the trip to Peru from Copacabana**, check the closing hours of the Kasani/Yunguyo crossing and the pre-registration procedures, which changed recently.

## Chiusura personale
What stays with me from this trip is the physical feeling of altitude, more than any single image: the short breath climbing the stairs in La Paz in the first two days, the absurd cold of the Sud Lípez geysers at dawn, the relief of breathing properly again in Sucre, and finally the humid heat of the Amazon arriving after a forty-five-minute flight like another planet. In between, the contrasts that make Bolivia different from any other country in South America: the cholitas' wrestling in El Alto and the tunnels of Cerro Rico in the same trip, the flamingos of Laguna Colorada and the pink dolphins of the Rio Yacuma, a witches' market and the colonial mint that smelted silver for an empire. It's not a comfortable country (ATMs run dry, roads get blocked, flights are canceled for weather), but altitude aside it's probably the easiest in the whole continent to get around on your own.
