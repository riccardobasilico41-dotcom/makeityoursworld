## Apertura personale
Bolivia comes in steps: you land at over four thousand meters before you've even worked out where you are, and from there your body adapts one day at a time while the landscape changes just as abruptly. The bowl of La Paz, the Lake Titicaca plateau, the blinding white of the Salar de Uyuni, the still-working mines of Potosí, the colonial baroque of Sucre and finally the humid heat of the Amazon, all in the same trip. This itinerary follows that logic of steps: it starts in La Paz and gives you time to acclimatize before heading higher, climbs to Titicaca and comes back, then tackles the real heart of the trip (the Salar and the Sud Lípez, three days by 4x4 among colored lagoons and geysers that alone justify Bolivia), drops to Potosí and Sucre for the more historical and cultural part, and ends with the sharpest break of all: a forty-five-minute flight from the four thousand meters of the altiplano to the sea-level rainforest. It's the cheapest country on the continent and, altitude aside, also one of the easiest to get around on your own; but it's also the country where conditions on the ground, from road blockades to flights canceled for weather, matter more than anywhere else: here the plan has to stay flexible.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** this itinerary follows the dry season, **May to October**: the Salar de Uyuni is dry and can be crossed far and wide, the Sud Lípez tracks are passable and the skies over the altiplano stay clear. If you want the mirror effect of the flooded Salar, go in January-March, knowing that several stops on the tour (Isla Incahuasi included) become inaccessible. The two experiences don't fit in the same trip, and this itinerary picks the dry salt flat.
- **Documents:** passport with at least 6 months of remaining validity. Italian citizens don't need a visa for tourist stays of up to 90 days, but Bolivia's entry rules have changed several times in recent years: check them again on Viaggiare Sicuro shortly before leaving. If you arrive at or leave from Copacabana and decide to extend the trip into Peru, know that the Kasani/Yunguyo crossing closes in the evening, that it now requires an online pre-registration before entering Bolivia and that the customs office closes at lunchtime. Border procedures in this area have changed recently, so check everything right before departure.
- **Currency and payments:** boliviano (BOB). In recent years the country has gone through a severe shortage of foreign currency, with parallel exchange rates and ATMs that can run dry: better to arrive with US dollars in cash in good condition and find out about the current situation before leaving. Outside hotels and restaurants in the main cities cash rules, and the Uyuni and Amazon tours are almost always paid in cash.
- **Connectivity:** Entel, Tigo and Viva are the main operators, with cheap SIMs you buy with your passport. Coverage is decent on the altiplano and in the cities, absent on the salt flat, in the Sud Lípez and in the Amazon forest: for several days of this itinerary you're offline.
- **Health and altitude:** this is the real theme of the trip. La Paz sits at about 3,640 meters, El Alto and its airport above 4,000, Potosí at 4,000, the Salar at 3,650, and the Sud Lípez climbs to almost 4,800. Arriving on an international flight and going from sea level to four thousand meters in a few hours is a real physical shock, not a figure of speech. Take the first two days in La Paz completely easy, with no trekking or demanding physical activity; the Death Road, even though it's downhill, should only be tackled after acclimatizing. For the Amazon part, in Rurrenabaque, a yellow fever vaccination is recommended.
- **Safety:** after the change of government in 2025 the political and social situation remains tense, with protests, spontaneous strikes and above all frequent **road blockades** across the country, which can isolate whole areas for days and wipe out flights and transfers. Allow generous time margins and backup plans, especially on the overland stretches of this itinerary (Uyuni-Potosí-Sucre). For the rest: watch out for theft in tourist areas, and licensed guides are mandatory in the Sud Lípez and in Rurrenabaque.
- **Getting around:** cheap but not always efficient, with long distances and roads that get much worse in the rainy season. Night buses are the norm between the main cities. Domestic flights with BoA, Amaszonas or EcoJet save whole days, but on the smaller routes (La Paz-Rurrenabaque above all) they're also the most exposed to weather cancellations. In La Paz, Mi Teleférico is the fastest, cheapest and most spectacular way to get around town.
- **Electricity:** 220V (110V in some areas), type A and C plugs: Italian two-pin plugs almost always fit.
- **Time zone:** UTC-4 all year, 6 hours behind Italy during daylight saving time, 5 in winter.
- **Holidays:** the Oruro Carnival, in February, is a UNESCO heritage event and the most spectacular festival in the country; it falls outside the window of this itinerary, designed for the dry season.
- **From the airport to the centre:** from **El Alto (LPB)** to La Paz takes 30-45 minutes, longer with traffic or roadblocks · airport taxi about 70-120 BOB, price to be agreed before getting in because there's no meter; the hotel transfer is more relaxing · shared minibuses about 5 BOB, awkward with luggage and with the altitude · InDrive works and is often cheaper · avoid unmarked cars and anyone offering to change money away from the desks · for domestic flights: **Sucre (SRE)** airport is about 30 km from town, a good half hour by taxi; at **Rurrenabaque (RBQ)** the town is a few minutes away by taxi or motorbike taxi.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Arrival in El Alto and first contact with La Paz
We landed at El Alto (LPB), one of the highest airports in the world, at over 4,000 meters: in a few hours you go from sea level to the highest altitude of this trip, and it is a physical shock to plan for, not to underestimate. By taxi or cable car you reach central **La Paz**, wedged into a canyon at 3,640 meters. *Today's plan has only one item: nothing.*

You settle in Sopocachi or San Pedro, drink plenty of water, take coca as leaves or tea as the locals do, and at most go for a very short walk. A light first dinner and early to bed: the first two days at altitude are non-negotiable, however much you want to go out and see the city straight away.

**In practice:** airport to center about 30-45 minutes by taxi, preferably an official one or one called by the hotel · change a little cash at the airport and the rest in town · no alcohol and no heavy meals on the first evening · headache and shortness of breath are normal; strong or worsening symptoms should be seen by a doctor.

### Day 2 — La Paz: cable cars, the Witches' Market and Valle de la Luna
The second day is still for acclimatizing, but more active. The urban cable cars of **Mi Teleférico** (red and yellow lines for the best views) are both public transport and the best viewpoint in the city, and they cost a few euro cents: try them more than once, perhaps up to El Alto, which towers over La Paz at over 4,000 meters. At the **Mercado de las Brujas**, the Witches' Market, you see traditional Aymara remedies, llama fetuses included: they are a real part of Andean culture, not a tourist attraction.

In the afternoon we took a taxi to the **Valle de la Luna**, eroded clay formations a few kilometers from the center. *You still feel the effort climbing stairs: that's normal, the body hasn't fully adjusted yet.* Anyone in El Alto in the evening can catch the cholitas luchadoras, the wrestling of Aymara women.

**In practice:** cable car paid per ride, with a separate ticket at each line change · Valle de la Luna has an entrance fee of about 15 bolivianos, about an hour's visit · cholitas luchadoras usually on Sunday afternoons at El Alto's Multifuncional, times to check; many go with a tour that includes transport · better not to walk around El Alto alone after dark.

### Day 3 — Tiwanaku and transfer to Copacabana
We moved to **Copacabana**, on Lake Titicaca: about 4 hours by road, with a stop at the ruins of **Tiwanaku**, capital of a civilization older than the Incas, along the road to the lake. At the site you see the semi-subterranean temple, the **Kalasasaya** platform with the Gate of the Sun and, a short way off, the cut blocks of **Puma Punku**; the two museums, lithic and ceramic, help you understand what you are looking at. It's best to get there in the morning and give it a couple of hours, with a guide or on a tour that combines Tiwanaku with the transfer.

The journey includes crossing the Strait of Tiquina on a wooden barge, a short passage that stays with you, with the car or bus loaded on board separately from the passengers. We reached Copacabana in the late afternoon, in time to climb **Cerro Calvario**, the hill above the town, for sunset over the highest navigable lake in the world. *The climb is short but steep, at 3,800 meters: take it slowly.* For dinner **trucha**, the lake trout, found almost everywhere along the waterfront.

**In practice:** single Tiwanaku ticket about 100 bolivianos for foreigners, covering sites and museums · at the Strait of Tiquina passengers get off and cross on a separate small boat, paying a few bolivianos · Cerro Calvario about 30-40 minutes' climb · few ATMs in Copacabana: arrive with cash.

### Day 4 — Boat to Isla del Sol and a night on the island
On a shared boat from Copacabana we went to **Isla del Sol**, where according to Inca tradition the sun and the first Inca were born: it is more beautiful and less built-up than the Peruvian islands on the other side of the lake. *Better to spend a night there than to visit on a day trip:* the tourist boats all arrive and leave together, and as soon as they're gone the island falls silent again. Boats leave Copacabana pier usually in the morning and early afternoon, landing at **Yumani**, in the south, or **Challapampa**, in the north: take the morning one to have the whole day.

We walked along the island's ridge, always uphill at almost 4,000 meters and always with our backpacks (it is no ordinary stroll), among terraces, stone paths and the ruins of **Chincana** and the **Roca Sagrada** at the northern tip. The full north-south route takes a few hours of walking, and the communities charge a small toll for each section. We slept in a simple place without heating: the nights stay cold all year.

**In practice:** Copacabana-island crossing about an hour and a half to two hours, ticket bought at the pier · there are no ATMs or card payments on the island: cash for lodging, meals and tolls · light backpack, leave the rest at a hotel in Copacabana · high-factor sunscreen and a hat, the sun burns at this altitude even in the cold.

### Day 5 — Isla del Sol at dawn and back to La Paz
The morning on the island is free, with the advantage of those who slept there: empty trails, low light, no boats in sight yet. It's the right moment to walk down the **Inca Stairway** at Yumani and its spring, or for a last stretch of trail to a viewpoint over the lake and the snow-capped Cordillera Real, before the day groups arrive.

In the late morning we went back to Copacabana and from there overland to **La Paz**, again crossing the Strait of Tiquina. *Tensions between the island's communities have at times restricted access to the northern part: ask locally before planning the whole ridge route.* Free evening in La Paz, to recover from the walk and get ready for the most physical day of the trip, the next one.

**In practice:** book or confirm the return boat the day before with your lodging · Copacabana-La Paz about 3.5-4 hours by bus, with both tourist buses and minibuses · book the Death Road tour for the same evening, if not already done, and ask for the hotel pick-up time.

### Day 6 — The Death Road by mountain bike
It's Bolivia's most iconic activity: the mountain bike descent along the old Yungas road, the "world's most dangerous road" until 2006. Today it is a cycling route of about 64 km and over 3,400 meters of descent, from the glacial pass of **La Cumbre** (4,650 m) to the subtropical forest of **Yolosa** (1,200 m), crossing three climate zones in a few hours.

*Here the price of the tour is a matter of safety, not comfort:* almost all reported accidents involve cheap operators with poorly maintained bikes and groups that are too big. It is worth spending the $120-170 of a premium operator (the benchmark is still Gravity Bolivia, which invented these tours in 1998) rather than saving 50 dollars on safety. We got back to La Paz in the late afternoon, legs finished and in need of a full change of clothes after the mud of the lower section.

**In practice:** hotel pick-up very early, around dawn, and return in the evening · community toll along the road about 50 bolivianos, often not included in the price: ask beforehand · heavy layers for the high start and light clothes for the finish · swimsuit or dry change for the final shower offered by many operators.

### Day 7 — El Alto and the night bus to Uyuni
A transition day in **La Paz**, also useful for shaking off the previous day's fatigue. If the calendar falls on a Thursday or Sunday, the El Alto market, one of the largest in South America, is worth the detour: you get there by cable car and walk for hours among car parts, clothes, animals and street food. Those staying in the center can use the morning for **Calle Jaén**, the city's best-preserved colonial street, or to withdraw cash calmly, because in the following days ATMs become scarce.

In the afternoon you pack for the three days in a 4x4: heavy layers, because in Sud Lípez you sleep below freezing and a sleeping bag is not always included in the tour. In the evening there's the **night bus** to **Uyuni**, a cheaper alternative to the direct flight, which covers the same route in about an hour. On this distance it pays to choose the better companies, with reclining bed seats so you actually sleep. *Documents, money and phone stay on you, not in the hold or the overhead rack.*

**In practice:** La Paz-Uyuni buses usually leave between 8 and 9 pm, about 10 hours of travel, arriving at dawn · book tickets with the tourist companies a day or two ahead · buses are often cold at night: keep a sweatshirt and a light blanket at hand · eat dinner before boarding.

### Day 8 — Uyuni, the train cemetery and entering the Salar
In the morning we arrived in **Uyuni**. Before the tour we stopped at the **train cemetery**, on the edge of town, with rusting locomotives abandoned since the early 1900s. In the early afternoon the three-day 4x4 tour of the **Salar de Uyuni** began: over 10,000 square kilometers of salt at 3,650 meters, where the lack of reference points cancels any sense of distance. It's time for the perspective photos, the ones where a bottle in the foreground looks as tall as a distant mountain.

We stopped at **Isla Incahuasi**, the rocky island covered in giant cacti in the middle of the white expanse, and watched the sunset over the salt flat. We spent the first night in a **salt hotel**, with walls and furniture made from blocks of the flat. *The sun's glare on the white is fierce even under a hazy sky: proper sunglasses and sunscreen from the first minute.*

**In practice:** a shared three-day tour roughly 200 to 350 dollars per person depending on agency and category · entrance fees often extra and in cash: Isla Incahuasi about 30 bolivianos, Eduardo Avaroa reserve about 150 the next day · before leaving, check that the 4x4 has seat belts and a spare wheel · water supply and some snacks for three days.

### Day 9 — Sud Lípez: colored lagoons and a night above 4,000 meters
The second day of the tour is the one that leaves the deepest mark: you leave the white of the salt flat for **Sud Lípez**, a high-altitude desert toward the Chilean border. The day runs from lagoon to lagoon, with short stops at the water's edge and long stretches of track in between. On **Laguna Colorada**, red from microscopic algae, thousands of flamingos move in groups against an almost lunar backdrop; the color is brightest in the afternoon, when the wind ruffles the water.

We passed wind-carved rock formations (the most photographed is the **Árbol de Piedra**) and deserts above 4,500 meters. *It is a much harder tour than the photos suggest:* isolated dirt tracks, constant wind and a basic second lodging, often unheated, where you sleep in your clothes. Dinner was cooked by the driver-cook, as for the whole tour.

**In practice:** entrance to the Reserva Eduardo Avaroa about 150 bolivianos, in cash, at the park entrance · no phone signal all day · roadside toilets cost a few bolivianos: keep coins ready · rent a sleeping bag from the agency if not included · anyone struggling with altitude should tell the driver right away, who usually carries oxygen.

### Day 10 — Sol de Mañana geysers, Laguna Verde and back to Uyuni
We set off while it was still dark for the **Sol de Mañana** geysers, at almost 5,000 meters: fumaroles and mud pools bubbling at dawn at twenty below zero, the highest and coldest point of the whole trip. *At the geysers, don't walk past the marked limits: the ground around the pools can give way.* Then a soak in the open-air **Polques hot springs**, icy air and scalding water in full contrast.

The last stop is **Laguna Verde**, at the foot of the Licancabur volcano, with a turquoise that changes when the wind stirs the copper sediments on the bottom. From here those continuing to Chile go down to San Pedro de Atacama; this itinerary turns back, with a long 4x4 transfer (most of the day) to **Uyuni**, where the tour ended in the evening.

**In practice:** wake-up around 4-5 am · swimsuit and towel already in your daypack for the springs, separate entrance of a few bolivianos · back in Uyuni in the late afternoon or evening · sleep in Uyuni and leave a tip for the driver-cook at the end of the tour.

### Day 11 — From Uyuni to Potosí
Overland we reached **Potosí**, at 4,000 meters, the city whose Cerro Rico silver financed the Spanish empire for centuries. The road from Uyuni is paved and crosses the altiplano for about four hours, among adobe villages and llama herds. You leave at an easy pace after the tour's fatigue and arrive in time for lunch. *Even just walking around the center, in the first hours after arriving, you feel the altitude:* better an unhurried afternoon.

Our first visit was to the **Casa Nacional de la Moneda**, the old colonial mint, to understand what silver mining meant before going into the mine the next day. It can only be seen on guided tours at set times, lasting a couple of hours. Afterwards, a walk around **Plaza 10 de Noviembre** and the churches of the center. For dinner, the hearty soups typical of the altiplano, made precisely for the cold at altitude.

**In practice:** Uyuni-Potosí bus about 4 hours, several departures a day · Casa de la Moneda with a ticket of about 40 bolivianos, guided only: check tour times, and from 2026 the building is undergoing restoration work · book today the mine visit for tomorrow.

### Day 12 — Potosí and the mines of Cerro Rico
It is the heaviest day of the trip, not physically but emotionally: the guided visit to the still-active tunnels of **Cerro Rico**, almost always with former miners as guides. You go in with helmet, lamp and boots into narrow, hot, dusty passages, next to men who work there ten hours a day with tools from another century. *It is not a tourist attraction and should not be treated as one:* it is customary to bring a small gift (coca leaves, soft drinks, gloves), bought at the miners' market before going up.

The visit is not advisable for anyone with claustrophobia or breathing problems, also because of the altitude. The tour usually starts in the morning and lasts half a day, with a couple of hours underground; choose an agency with guides who used to be miners and small groups, not the cheapest one. The afternoon is for digesting what you've seen before moving on.

**In practice:** mine visit about 100-150 bolivianos per person with a Spanish-speaking guide, 150-250 with an English-speaking one, with gear and transport · dynamite demonstrations are illegal and dangerous: refuse them · old clothes that can get dirty · a dust mask is recommended.

### Day 13 — From Potosí to Sucre
It's about 3 hours by road to **Sucre**, the constitutional capital, a white colonial UNESCO World Heritage city at 2,800 meters. You can leave in the morning by bus or shared taxi, which is faster, and the road drops more than a thousand meters through ever greener valleys. It's the gentlest altitude of the whole trip, and you feel it at once: you breathe better, the climate is mild, the streets are orderly.

We spent the early afternoon free in the historic center, among white façades and colonial churches; the physical relief of the lower altitude alone justifies a longer stay than planned. The natural starting point is **Plaza 25 de Mayo**, with the cathedral; at sunset you walk up to the **Recoleta viewpoint**, above the city, looking over the tiled roofs and the surrounding hills.

**In practice:** Potosí-Sucre about 3 hours by bus or shared taxi from the terminal · walk up to the Recoleta about 20 minutes from the square · sleep in the historic center · ATMs work better in Sucre than elsewhere: a good moment to stock up.

### Day 14 — Sucre: colonial center and Parque Cretácico
A day for Sucre: the **Casa de la Libertad**, where Bolivia's independence was signed; the covered markets, where you eat well for very little (the fruit juice stall is an institution); the **Parque Cretácico**, a vertical wall with thousands of dinosaur footprints pressed into what was once a lake shore. Go to the park in the morning, on the tourist bus from the center, and take the guided tour that goes down close to the wall.

*If your dates fall on a Sunday*, the hour-and-a-half to two-hour detour to **Tarabuco** is worth it for the Sunday textile market, one of the most authentic in the country for Yampara weaving. Sucre also has some of the best and cheapest Spanish schools in South America: anyone with extra time can stay for a course.

**In practice:** Parque Cretácico about 25 minutes from the center, entrance around 30 bolivianos with a guide · the Tarabuco market is held only on Sunday mornings, colectivo or tour from the center · Casa de la Libertad by guided tour, check times on site · at the Mercado Central a full lunch costs a few bolivianos.

### Day 15 — From Sucre to La Paz
The flight from Sucre to **La Paz** takes about an hour (the slower but cheaper alternative is the night bus). It's best to take a morning flight, so you reach La Paz with half a day to spare; Sucre airport is outside the city, about half an hour by taxi from the center.

A transition day, useful for stocking up on cash (outside La Paz and Sucre ATMs are rare) and for reorganizing your pack for the trip's sharpest change: the next day you leave four thousand meters for the sea-level Amazon rainforest. Heavy altitude gear, down jacket included, can stay in storage at the La Paz hotel; for the pampas light long-sleeved clothes, a hat and shoes that can get wet are enough.

**In practice:** Sucre-La Paz flight about an hour, night bus about 12-14 hours · check the day before that the Rurrenabaque flight is confirmed · buy strong repellent and sunscreen in La Paz, they cost more in the Amazon · sleep near the center, but with a taxi booked to El Alto airport.

### Day 16 — Flight to Rurrenabaque and entering the Yacuma pampas
In a small plane we flew from La Paz to **Rurrenabaque**: about 45 minutes that alone are worth entering this chapter of the trip, because in under an hour you go from the four thousand meters of the altiplano to the humid Amazon heat. *Know this beforehand: these flights are often cancelled for weather, not only in the rainy season, because low clouds are enough to prevent landing on a runway like this. Keep a day's margin and don't book an important connection right away.*

On arrival we went by boat along the **Río Yacuma** to the lodge in the **pampas**, and already in the early afternoon you see the first caimans on the banks. Before the boat there's a stretch of about three hours on a dirt road to **Santa Rosa del Yacuma**, where you pay the protected area entrance. Night at the lodge, with mosquito nets and serious repellent, not just any: the mosquitoes here are on another level.

**In practice:** on the La Paz-Rurrenabaque route today it is mainly EcoJet flying, with few flights a week: check the days and book early · pampas entrance about 150 bolivianos, in cash, often excluded from the tour price · book the three-day pampas tour with a Rurrenabaque agency with good reviews on animal treatment.

### Day 17 — Yacuma pampas: caimans, capybaras and pink dolphins
We spent the whole day on a boat along the river: **caimans**, **capybaras**, monkeys on the banks and hundreds of birds, with **pink river dolphins** surfacing every now and then beside the hull. It's the stretch of the trip where you see the most animals with the least effort. Outings focus on early morning and late afternoon, when the animals are most active; in the hot middle hours you rest at the lodge.

*Some guides offer to pull caimans out of the water or to look for anacondas for clients to touch: it is harmful to the animals, and to refuse it you just ask to watch without interfering.* The best moment of the day is late afternoon, with the sunset over the flooded grasslands and the sky reflected in the shallow water.

**In practice:** hat, long sleeves and repellent on the boat too · binoculars useful for birds and monkeys · the walk through the grasslands looking for anacondas is done in boots, usually supplied by the lodge · meals included in the tour.

### Day 18 — Piranha fishing and a night caiman search, back to Rurrenabaque
In the morning there's **piranha** fishing from the boat: you catch more than you'd expect, and it's a good way to spend the hottest hours. The night before or this one you go out by boat for the **night caiman search**, flashlights catching the red of their eyes just above the waterline. Many tours also add a dawn outing to watch the sun rise over the pampas.

In the morning you return by boat to **Rurrenabaque**. Those with more time can add a detour to **Madidi National Park**, the true primary forest: more tiring and with less guaranteed sightings than the pampas, but one of the most biodiverse areas on the planet. Free evening in town, on the Beni river.

**In practice:** return from Santa Rosa to Rurrenabaque about 3 hours on dirt road, arriving in the early afternoon · one night in Rurrenabaque before the flight · Madidi needs at least two or three extra days and a dedicated agency · headlamp for the night outing.

### Day 19 — Return to La Paz and departure
The return flight to **La Paz** should be taken with the same caution as on Day 16, because of possible weather cancellations. Anyone with an international flight the same day runs a real risk: that's why the margin belongs here, at the end, even more than on the way out.

The last hours in the city depend on the time left (one last cable car ride, one last market), then the transfer to El Alto for the international flight home. Nineteen days, from La Paz's 3,640 meters to almost 5,000 in Sud Lípez down to the sea level of the Amazon: the highest, cheapest and roughest Bolivia in South America, in a single trip.

**In practice:** if the Rurrenabaque flight is cancelled, the overland alternative to La Paz takes many hours by bus · from central La Paz to El Alto airport about 30-45 minutes, more with traffic or blockades · get to the airport at least three hours before the international flight · spend your last bolivianos first: they exchange badly outside the country.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **La Paz:** Sopocachi or San Pedro, handy for restaurants, evening life and the agencies that run the Death Road and Uyuni tours.
- **Copacabana and Isla del Sol:** Copacabana for the convenience of transport and the sunset from Cerro Calvario; one night on Isla del Sol, in simple places with no heating. Sleeping there changes everything compared with a day visit.
- **Salar de Uyuni and Sud Lípez:** the first night of the tour a salt hotel, with walls and furniture made from blocks of the salt flat; the second night, in the Sud Lípez above 4,000 meters, a very basic lodging, often without heating.
- **Potosí:** in the center, near the Casa de la Moneda and the miners' market.
- **Sucre:** in the colonial historic center, the most pleasant place to stay on the whole trip.
- **Rurrenabaque and the pampas:** during the tour a lodge along the Rio Yacuma, basic but with mosquito nets; one night in town at Rurrenabaque before the return flight.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **La Paz:** salteñas mid-morning from the covered markets (a local habit, not a breakfast); hearty soups against the cold at altitude; the Sopocachi area has the most refined offering in the city.
- **Copacabana:** trucha, the lake trout, found almost everywhere along the waterfront.
- **Sud Lípez:** meals included in the tour, prepared by the driver-cook: simple, but surprisingly good given the isolation.
- **Potosí:** hearty soups, suited to four thousand meters of altitude.
- **Sucre:** the best quality of life in the country, with the covered markets and the fruit juice stall as a city institution.
- **Rurrenabaque and pampas:** meals included in the tours, simple: rice, fish, vegetables.

## Practical tips
- **Take the first two days in La Paz completely easy:** in a few hours you go from sea level to over 4,000 meters, and it's a real physical shock before it's a figure of speech.
- **Road blockades:** build them into the itinerary, don't treat them as a remote possibility. They can isolate whole areas for days and wipe out flights and transfers, especially on the overland stretches between Uyuni, Potosí and Sucre.
- **Book the Uyuni tour in advance, not on the spot:** in Uyuni the street agencies and pop-up ones offer lower prices, but they change the itinerary after payment, pack in more passengers than planned or put drivers without a license or insurance behind the wheel. Booking from La Paz or online with a verifiable agency cuts the risk a lot.
- **Don't skimp on the Death Road operator:** almost all reported accidents involve cheap operators with poorly maintained bikes; it's worth spending the $120-170 of the premium tier.
- **Flights to Rurrenabaque are often canceled for weather**, not only in the rainy season: keep a spare day both on the way there and, above all, on the way back, before an international flight.
- **Always carry cash:** outside La Paz and Sucre ATMs are rare or empty, and tours are almost always paid in cash.
- **The Potosí mines are not a tourist attraction:** approach them with respect, and skip them if you're claustrophobic or have breathing problems.
- **In the pampas, refuse practices that handle wildlife** (caimans pulled out of the water, anacondas sought out so you can touch them): just ask to stay and watch.
- **If you extend the trip to Peru from Copacabana**, check the closing hours of the Kasani/Yunguyo crossing and the pre-registration procedures, which changed recently.

## Chiusura personale
What stays with me from this trip is the physical feeling of altitude, more than any single image: the short breath climbing the stairs in La Paz in the first two days, the absurd cold of the Sud Lípez geysers at dawn, the relief of breathing properly again in Sucre, and finally the humid heat of the Amazon arriving after a forty-five-minute flight like another planet. In between, the contrasts that make Bolivia different from any other country in South America: the cholitas' wrestling in El Alto and the tunnels of Cerro Rico in the same trip, the flamingos of Laguna Colorada and the pink dolphins of the Rio Yacuma, a witches' market and the colonial mint that smelted silver for an empire. It's not a comfortable country (ATMs run dry, roads get blocked, flights are canceled for weather), but altitude aside it's probably the easiest in the whole continent to get around on your own.
