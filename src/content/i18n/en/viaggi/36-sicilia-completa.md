# All of Sicily: The Complete Loop in 17 Days

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 work in progress (itinerary and practical information verified; the first-person passages are written as a draft to reread and personalize)
**Periodo del viaggio:**
**Durata:** 17 days, 16 nights — a complete loop of the island, from Palermo to Catania
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independent, with a rental car
**Categoria:** Europa · Italia · Sicilia · archeologia · arte · gastronomia

---

## Apertura personale
There's another itinerary on this site that says not to try to do all of Sicily and to take half of it. That is still the right advice for anyone with ten days. This is the itinerary for those who have seventeen, and then the whole discussion changes: all of Sicily can be done, but only if you give it the time it asks for, which is the time of a nation and not of a region.

The island is as big as Lombardy and has a road network that is not Lombardy's: **motorways exist on only two routes**, everything else is state road, and map times need to be **increased by about a third**. Seventeen days seem like a lot until you try to fit in two Greek archaeological parks, an active volcano, eight Baroque cities, the largest concentration of Arab-Norman art in the world, a coastal reserve, salt pans and a couple of islands of Phoenician history.

The structure is a **counterclockwise loop**: you land in Palermo, go down the west, pass through the archaeological south, come back up for the Baroque and Syracuse, finish on Etna and fly out of Catania. No backtracking, no pointless transfers.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** **mid-April to early June**, and **mid-September to late October.** July and August are scorching (**the interior regularly exceeds 40 degrees**), expensive and packed, and archaeological sites without shade become dangerous. **March and November** are excellent for cities and archaeology, with the sea out of the picture and some coastal facilities closed. **Winter** is mild and empty, and for a trip of only cities and sites it works very well.
- **Flights:** **open in Palermo and close in Catania** (or vice versa), with a car rental on a **one-way basis**. There is a surcharge, but it spares you **four hours of transfer back** and a lost day. It is the choice that more than any other makes the complete loop make sense.
- **From the airport to the centre:** from **Palermo Falcone Borsellino (PMO)** the **Prestia e Comandè** bus runs to Politeama and the Central Station in about 50 minutes, every half hour from 5am to 1am, about €6 online and €6.50 on board; there's also the Trinacria Express train to Centrale, about an hour; taxis have fixed fares by zone, about €39-50 (€50 to the Central Station). From **Catania Fontanarossa (CTA)** the **Alibus** goes to the Central Station in 20-30 minutes, about €4, ticket on board or in the app, but there's no service between 00:30 and 4:40; taxi at a fixed fare of about €27 to Piazza Duomo or the Central Station, on trial until the end of 2026. With a one-way rental you pick up and drop off the car at the airport: photograph it at both ends.
- **Car:** **essential.** The Sicilian rail network is slow, single-track for much of its length, and covers almost nothing of what's in this itinerary. Practical notes: **travel times need to be increased by a third** on the interior state roads; the **ZTL** (limited-traffic zones) of historic centers (Palermo, Syracuse-Ortigia, Ragusa Ibla, Noto) are active and fined; fuel costs more than in the rest of Italy.
- **Bookings:** Sicily doesn't have the heavy quotas of the big art cities of the center-north, but a few things need organizing:
  - **The ascent to Etna's summit zone:** cable car plus off-road vehicle, and **above roughly 2,900 meters a guide is mandatory**. **INGV publishes bulletins on the activity: in case of an eruption, access above a certain altitude is closed without notice.** You need a plan B.
  - **The Palatine Chapel** in Palermo has hours conditioned by the institutional activities of the Palazzo dei Normanni, which is **the seat of the Regional Assembly**: **on Friday, Saturday and Sunday the visiting route changes**. Check beforehand.
  - **Wineries** (Etna, Marsala, Vittoria) are visited **by appointment**, never without.
- **Combined tickets:** almost all Sicilian archaeological parks have combined options that are worth it (for example Valley of the Temples plus museum, Syracuse plus the Paolo Orsi museum). Single prices are in any case low compared with the center-north.
- **The sun:** **the Valley of the Temples, Selinunte and Segesta have no shade.** In summer they are visited **at night or first thing in the morning**. Hat, water, closed shoes, and sunscreen in every season.
- **The two cuisines.** Sicily changes cuisine halfway across the island, and the trip notices:
  - **In the west** North African influence dominates: **fish couscous** in Trapani and San Vito Lo Capo, **busiate with Trapanese pesto** (almonds, tomato, basil, garlic), **bottarga** and tuna, **panelle** and **crocchè** in Palermo, **pane ca meusa**, **sfincione** and **stigghiola** on the street.
  - **In the east** pasta and rockfish prevail: **pasta alla Norma** in Catania, **sea urchins** in Syracuse, swordfish in the Strait, **Modica chocolate** worked cold.
  - **Everywhere:** **cannoli filled to order and never before** (if they're already full in the window, the shell is soggy), **cassata**, **granita with a brioche col tuppo for breakfast** (made with water, sugar and fruit, not with cream), and the **arancino war**: **masculine in Catania, feminine in Palermo**, a real cultural fault line nobody jokes about.
- **Wine:** **Etna Rosso** and **Etna Bianco** on the slopes of the volcano (Nerello Mascalese and Carricante), **Nero d'Avola** and **Cerasuolo di Vittoria** in the south-east, **Marsala** in the west, **Grillo** and **Catarratto** among the whites, **Passito di Pantelleria** for dessert.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Arrival in Palermo
We landed in Palermo and picked up the car, which for the first few days **stayed parked**: Palermo is explored on foot, and by car it's torture.

We took our first walk along the axis of the **Quattro Canti**, the Baroque crossing of the two main streets with its four curved facades, and as far as the **Fontana Pretoria**, which Palermitans called "the fountain of shame" because of its nudes, bought in Florence and reassembled here piece by piece.

The first evening was **street food**: **panelle** of chickpea flour, **crocchè**, **sfincione**, and **pane ca meusa** for those who dare, a sandwich of veal spleen and lung, boiled and then fried in lard, "schietto" with lemon or "maritato" with ricotta and caciocavallo. It's the oldest street food in the city and it doesn't do half measures.

**In practice:** from the airport to central Palermo about 45 minutes by shuttle, train or car · car in a garage or a guarded car park for the first days · ZTL restricted traffic zone active in the historic centre · street food in the evening at the historic markets, in cash.

### Day 2 — Arab-Norman Palermo
It's the day that explains Sicily better than any other.

We started at the **Palazzo dei Normanni** with the **Palatine Chapel**, the absolute masterpiece. Commissioned by **Roger II** in the 12th century, it has **Byzantine mosaics on a gold ground**, a **Latin basilica plan** and a **muqarnas wooden ceiling by Arab craftsmen**: an Islamic honeycomb ceiling, with inscriptions in Arabic and scenes of court life, above a Catholic church decorated by Greek artists. **Three cultures in the same building, at the same moment, by the explicit will of the patron.** It was a policy of government, not a chance syncretism.

Then the **Cathedral**, a manual of layering: it was a basilica, then a mosque, then a Norman cathedral, then reworked in the eighteenth century with a neoclassical dome that clashes and is now part of the building's identity. Inside are the **imperial tombs**, including that of **Frederick II**, in red porphyry.

Then **San Giovanni degli Eremiti** with its **five red domes** and the cloister, and the **Martorana** (Santa Maria dell'Ammiraglio), with its Byzantine mosaics and the **portrait of Roger II crowned directly by Christ**: a precise political statement, the king's power comes from God and not from the Pope.

In the afternoon we visited the **oratories of Serpotta**, small confraternity rooms covered entirely in **white stucco** (allegorical figures, putti, little theaters), of a virtuosity that has to be seen up close. They are on side streets and almost always empty.

**In practice:** the Cappella Palatina's visit route changes on Friday, Saturday and Sunday, check beforehand · separate tickets for the Palazzo dei Normanni, the Cathedral (tombs and roofs), San Giovanni degli Eremiti and the Martorana · shoulders and knees covered in churches · everything on foot.

### Day 3 — The Markets, the Catacombs, Palazzo Abatellis
We spent the morning in one of the **historic markets**, **Ballarò**, the **Capo** or the **Vucciria**, which are the most visible legacy of the Arab centuries: the souk layout, the **abbanniata** (the cry of the vendors, which is a form of song), the cooking stalls in the middle of the street. Ballarò is the liveliest and the least tamed.

Then the **Capuchin Catacombs**, which need to be explained beforehand to avoid misunderstandings. They are underground corridors in which **thousands of mummified bodies** are displayed, standing, dressed, divided by social category: clergy, professionals, women, children. The practice went on for **over three hundred years**, into the twentieth century, and was a **status symbol**: families paid to maintain their loved one's place. The last is **Rosalia Lombardo**, who died at two in 1920, embalmed with a method that preserved her in an impressive way. **It's not a place for everyone**, and it's right to know it before going in; it's also one of the most extraordinary documents on the relationship between a city and its dead.

In the afternoon we went to **Palazzo Abatellis**, the regional gallery. There's the **Triumph of Death**, an enormous detached fifteenth-century fresco in which death is a skeletal rider on a skeletal horse crossing a garden of aristocrats, and there's the **Annunciata** by **Antonello da Messina**, a very small panel, with the Madonna raising a hand toward the viewer and the blue veil framing her face. It's one of the most beautiful portraits in Italian painting and it hangs in an almost empty room.

In the evening, the **Teatro Massimo**, if there's a performance: it is **the largest opera house in Italy** and can also be visited by day.

**In practice:** historic markets at their best in the morning · Catacombs with a ticket, no photos allowed · check Palazzo Abatellis and Teatro Massimo hours on the official sites, the theatre is seen on a guided tour · watch your wallet at the markets.

### Day 4 — Monreale and Cefalù
In the morning we climbed up to **Monreale**, a few kilometers above Palermo. The **Duomo** has **about six thousand square meters of gold-ground mosaics** covering the walls completely, with a **Christ Pantocrator** in the apse whose hand alone is as tall as a person. It is the most extensive medieval mosaic cycle in Italy. The **cloister** next to it has **228 paired columns**, each with a different capital and decoration, in a square courtyard with an Arab fountain in the corner. Give both time.

In the afternoon we went to **Cefalù**, on the north coast, a little under an hour of motorway. There's another **Norman cathedral** with another **Pantocrator**, older than the one at Monreale and, for many, more beautiful; a historic center squeezed between the mountain and the sea; the **medieval wash house** where the water still runs; and the **Rocca**, which you climb in an hour for the view.

Then you return to Palermo or, if you prefer, spend the first night already moving west.

**In practice:** Monreale half an hour by car from Palermo, also by bus · the cloister has its own ticket, separate from the Cathedral · Cefalù just under an hour on the motorway, park outside the historic centre · the Rocca is an hour's climb, with water and closed shoes.

### Day 5 — Segesta, Erice and Trapani
We left Palermo and headed west.

First **Segesta**. The **Doric temple** stands alone on a hill in the middle of the countryside, **with no city around it**, and it is **unfinished**: the columns were never fluted and there's no trace of a roof, which makes it a unique document of how a Greek temple was built. A little higher up, on the top of the mountain, is the **theater**, overlooking an empty valley all the way to the sea.

Then we went up to **Erice**, a medieval village at **about 750 meters** on a mountain plunging into the sea, reachable by car or by **cable car** from Trapani. The streets are polished stone, the houses closed, and **the fog rises from the sea and wraps the town** so often that it's almost its identity. At the top is the **Castle of Venus**, built on the site of a very ancient sanctuary dedicated first to Astarte, then Aphrodite, then Venus, which was a landmark for sailors all over the Mediterranean. **The pastry shops of Erice** are famous for almond sweets and for **genovesi** filled with cream, still warm.

Night in **Trapani** or nearby.

**In practice:** Segesta with a ticket, paid shuttle up to the theatre · Erice by car or by cable car from Trapani, which stops in strong wind · a sweater even in summer for Erice's fog · night in or around Trapani.

### Day 6 — The Salt Pans, Motya and Marsala
It's the strangest day of the itinerary, and one of the most beautiful.

The **salt pans between Trapani and Marsala** are shallow basins separated by embankments, with the **windmills** that were used to pump the water and grind the salt, and white pyramids of salt covered with tiles. **At sunset the water turns pink** because of halophilic microorganisms, and reflects the sky. The **Stagnone Reserve** is a very shallow, protected lagoon.

In the middle of the lagoon is **Motya**, a small flat island where the **Phoenicians** founded a colony in the 8th century BC. We reached it with a short boat ride, and the island can be toured on foot in a couple of hours: there are the walls, the **cothon** (an artificial harbor basin), the **tophet** and, in the museum, the **Motya Charioteer** (the *Giovinetto di Mozia*), a Greek marble statue of a young man in a clinging pleated tunic, of a quality you don't expect in western Sicily. There is also, under a few centimeters of water, a **submerged road** that connected the island to the mainland and that under certain conditions can still be walked.

In the afternoon we went to **Marsala**, which gives its name to the wine. It was an English merchant, at the end of the eighteenth century, who realized that the local fortified wine withstood sea voyages, and an industry was born from there. **The historic wineries can be visited by appointment.** **Garibaldi's Thousand** also landed at Marsala, in 1860.

**In practice:** salt pans at sunset · boat to Mozia from the landing on the Stagnone, a few minutes, with the museum ticket extra · a couple of hours for the island · Marsala wineries by appointment only.

### Day 7 — Zingaro, Scopello and San Vito Lo Capo
A day of coast and walking.

The **Zingaro Reserve** was **the first nature reserve established in Sicily, in 1981**, and its story deserves telling. A coastal road was supposed to run through it, and in 1980 several thousand people made **a march along the route** to stop it. The road was not built and the reserve was born. Today there are **about seven kilometers of coastal trail** without a single car, with **coves of white pebbles** reachable only on foot (Cala Capreria, Cala dell'Uzzo, Cala Marinella) and Mediterranean scrub running down to the sea. **There are two entrances, north and south, and the full route is about four hours round trip**: you can also do just the first stretch. **Bring water: inside the reserve you can't buy anything.**

At the south entrance is **Scopello**, with its **baglio** and the **tonnara** facing the **sea stacks**, one of the most photographed spots in Sicily.

In the afternoon we reached **San Vito Lo Capo**, with its pale sand beach beneath Monte Monaco and the **couscous**, which here is made with fish, steamed in the **cuscusiera** and comes from the North African tradition passed down through the fishermen. **In September there is a festival dedicated to it** that fills the town.

**In practice:** Zingaro with an entrance ticket, north entrance from San Vito and south from Scopello · full route about four hours there and back · bring water and food, trail shoes · in summer leave at dawn · in San Vito fish couscous, festival in September.

### Day 8 — Selinunte and the Cave di Cusa
We went down the southern coast.

**Selinunte** is **the largest archaeological park in Europe**: a vast area with the acropolis on the hill plunging into the sea, the eastern hill with more temples, the sanctuary of Malophoros and a whole city around. Many of the temples **collapsed in an earthquake and were left as they fell**: you walk among **column drums as big as rooms**, piled one on another. **It takes half a day and it takes the shade that isn't there**: go early.

A few kilometers away are the **Cave di Cusa**, which almost nobody visits and which are the most impressive thing in the area. It is the quarry the blocks of Selinunte came from, and **when the Carthaginians destroyed the city in 409 BC the work stopped abruptly**. **Column drums half extracted** were left in place, still attached to the bedrock, with the circular groove cut around them, and others already detached and ready for transport. They have been there for twenty-four hundred years, exactly as they were left.

Evening toward **Agrigento** or **Sciacca**.

**In practice:** Selinunte half a day, long distances inside between the acropolis and the eastern hill · go in at opening time, no shade · Cave di Cusa a few kilometres away, reduced hours to check · evening towards Agrigento or Sciacca.

### Day 9 — Agrigento and the Valley of the Temples
The **Valley of the Temples** is the reason Sicily is on half the world's lists. It is a long ridge with a row of fifth-century BC Doric temples, not a valley: the name is a nineteenth-century translation error.

**The Temple of Concordia** is among the best-preserved Doric temples in the world, and for a precise reason: **in the 6th century AD it was converted into a Christian church**, and this saved it from the demolition that struck the others. Then the **Temple of Juno** at the top of the ridge, the **Temple of Hercules**, the oldest, and the **Temple of Olympian Zeus**, never finished, which would have been the largest Doric temple ever built and of which a **telamon** almost eight meters tall lies on the ground.

**In summer it's visited at night or first thing in the morning**: there's no shade over an enormous area and at noon in July it's an oven.

Not to be skipped: the **Archaeological Museum**, with the reassembled telamon, and the **Kolymbethra Garden**, a citrus garden inside the archaeological area (originally a large Greek-era water-collection basin), **run by the FAI**, which almost no one enters and where you walk in the shade among the citrus trees with the temples above your head.

**In practice:** Valley of the Temples with a combined ticket including the Archaeological Museum · evening openings in summer (in 2026 from 20 June to 20 September) · FAI's Kolymbethra on a separate ticket · two entrances with car parks at the two ends of the ridge · water and a hat.

### Day 10 — The Scala dei Turchi and the Interior
In the morning we went to the **Scala dei Turchi**, the **white marl** cliff that descends in steps into the sea, **with the warning that access has been regulated and in certain periods completely closed** because of erosion and vandalism: **check its status before going**, and respect it.

Then we headed up toward the interior, the part almost everyone skips and which changes your perception of the island. There's no sea, there are no foreign tourists, and there is the agricultural, latifundist Sicily from which half of Italian emigration set out.

**Villa Romana del Casale** at **Piazza Armerina**, a UNESCO site, has **3,500 square meters of Roman floor mosaics from the 4th century**, the most extensive and best preserved in the world, saved because a medieval landslide buried them. You walk them on raised walkways under a modern roof: there's the **Great Hunt**, sixty meters long, with the capture of animals for the circus games, and there's the room of the "bikini girls," athletes in surprisingly modern sports attire.

If time remains, **Caltagirone** with its staircase of **142 painted ceramic steps**, each one different, or **Enna**, at the exact geographic center of Sicily, at 900 meters, from where on clear days you can see Etna.

**In practice:** check Scala dei Turchi access before setting off (in 2026 limited, about €6, 20-minute slots) · Agrigento to Piazza Armerina about two hours by car · Villa Romana del Casale with a ticket, best in the morning · Caltagirone or Enna only if there's light left.

### Day 11 — Ragusa Ibla and Arriving in the Baroque
We entered the **Val di Noto**, a UNESCO site: **eight cities rebuilt after the earthquake of 1693**, all in the same style and at the same moment, a unique case of Baroque urban planning on a regional scale.

**Ragusa** is the right gateway, because it has a peculiarity: **it was rebuilt twice**. One part of the population rebuilt the city on the same rock spur, and that is **Ibla**, a labyrinth of alleys and staircases; another part rebuilt it on a nearby plateau, by more modern criteria, and that is **upper Ragusa**. You go down from one to the other on very long staircases, with the panorama opening up.

**Ibla** at sunset, with the **Cathedral of San Giorgio** at the top of the staircase and the **Giardino Ibleo** at the end of the spur, is the most beautiful piece of Sicilian Baroque.

**In practice:** ZTL in Ragusa Ibla, park outside and walk down · comfortable shoes for the stairways between upper Ragusa and Ibla · Ibla at sunset · to sleep, Ibla or the surrounding countryside.

### Day 12 — Modica, Scicli and Noto
It's the day of the other three, all twenty kilometers from one another, to be done without hurry.

**Modica** is built inside two ravines, with the **Cathedral of San Giorgio** at the top of a 250-step staircase and the **cold-worked chocolate**: a technique that came from the Spanish, and before them from the Aztecs, in which **the sugar doesn't melt** and stays in crystals, so the chocolate is grainy under the teeth and keeps the taste of pure cacao.

**Scicli** is the smallest and the least altered, with the facades of the main street and the **Palazzo Beneventano** with its grotesque masks.

**Noto** is the most scenographic, built entirely in **local limestone that turns golden at sunset**: the main street is a continuous sequence of churches and palaces with balconies held up by brackets carved into monsters and horses. **It is the most photographed city of Sicilian Baroque and must be seen at the right hour.**

**In practice:** about twenty kilometres between one town and the next · ZTL in Modica and Noto, paid car parks at the edge of the centre · Modica chocolate in the historic shops, with tastings · Noto in the last light of the day.

### Day 13 — Vendicari and Arrival in Syracuse
In the morning we were at the **Vendicari Reserve**, one of the best-preserved coastal areas of Sicily: the **salt pans**, the **abandoned tonnara** with its chimney on the beach, the marshes and sandy beaches. **In spring and autumn there are flamingos**, and the reserve is a stopover on the migration route between Europe and Africa. A little further south is the beach of **Calamosche**, in a bay enclosed between two headlands, reachable only on foot.

In the afternoon we moved to **Syracuse** and settled in **Ortigia**, the island where the historic center stands and where everything is explored on foot.

In the evening, the **Ortigia market** and dinner with **sea urchins**, which here are the specialty.

**In practice:** Vendicari with several entrances and paths between beaches and salt pans · Calamosche only on foot, about twenty minutes from the car park · binoculars for the flamingos · ZTL in Ortigia, car in a park-and-ride then on foot · look for sea urchins in season.

### Day 14 — Syracuse
**For a long period Syracuse was the most powerful city of the Greek world**, bigger than Athens, and the **Neapolis Archaeological Park** preserves its traces: the **Greek theater** carved into the living rock; the **Ear of Dionysius**, an artificial cavity twenty-three meters high with extraordinary acoustics, named by Caravaggio, who said it looked like an auricle; and the **Altar of Hieron**, the largest altar of antiquity.

Then we explored **Ortigia** on foot. The **Cathedral** is the most extraordinary building in the city because it is a **fifth-century BC Doric temple converted into a church**: **the Greek columns are still there, built into the walls**, and you touch them with your hand as you enter a Baroque church. Then the **Fountain of Arethusa**, a freshwater spring two meters from the sea with **papyrus** growing wild, and the **Maniace Castle** on the tip.

Anyone with time can add the **Paolo Orsi Museum**, one of the most important archaeological museums in Italy, and the church of **Santa Lucia alla Badia** with the **Burial of Saint Lucy** by **Caravaggio**, painted during his flight from Malta.

**In practice:** Neapolis with a ticket, combined with the Paolo Orsi Museum · from the Neapolis to Ortigia about half an hour on foot or a few minutes by car · Cathedral with a ticket · the Caravaggio of Santa Lucia alla Badia is on Piazza Duomo · Neapolis early in the morning, no shade in summer.

### Day 15 — Etna
We moved toward the volcano, for the day you come east for.

**Check the INGV bulletin the night before.** The ascent to the summit zone is made by **cable car plus off-road vehicle**, and **above a certain altitude a guide is mandatory**. You walk on black lapilli that crunch and give way underfoot, with fumaroles coming out of the cracks and the ground warm to the touch where the flows are recent.

**Real layers:** you leave Catania at twenty-five degrees and at three thousand meters it can be five, with strong wind.

In the afternoon there are the **Silvestri craters** at low altitude, free and reachable by car, or an **Etna winery by appointment**. The vines here are often **ungrafted**, because **phylloxera** never reached the volcanic soil, and there are vines over a hundred years old. In twenty years Etna has become one of the most interesting wine territories in Europe, and it's worth understanding why on the spot.

**In practice:** INGV bulletin the evening before · cable car from Rifugio Sapienza, then off-road vehicle and guide, to book · trekking shoes, windproof jacket, layers · Silvestri craters free · Etna wineries by appointment only.

### Day 16 — Taormina and the Alcantara Gorges
We reached **Taormina** early in the morning, before the tour buses. The **Greco-Roman theater** overlooking Etna and the sea is one of the most spectacular settings in the Mediterranean, and the fact that the Greeks oriented it that way is no accident. In high season the town is packed and has prices from another region; by mid-morning it's already unmanageable.

In the afternoon we were at the **Alcantara Gorges**, the **columnar basalt** canyon carved by the river into a lava flow: the walls are made of regular vertical prisms, formed by the rapid cooling of the lava on contact with water. You go down and walk in the riverbed, **with icy water even in August**.

Last evening in **Catania** or on the Etna side.

**In practice:** Taormina has a ZTL, car in the public car parks below town then shuttle or stairs · theatre at opening time · Alcantara Gorges with different access points, some paid, and water shoes · last evening in Catania or on Etna.

### Day 17 — Catania and Departure
We spent the last morning in **Catania**, which divides opinion: it's lively, chaotic, built entirely in **black lava stone** after the earthquake of 1693, and less beautiful than Palermo. Take it for what it is.

There's the **Pescheria market**, in a square behind the Cathedral, one of the most theatrical fish markets in Italy; **Via dei Crociferi** with its row of Baroque churches; and the **Piazza del Duomo** with the lava stone elephant.

A last granita with brioche, last purchases (**Bronte pistachio**, Modica chocolate, capers, Etna wine, salt from the Trapani salt pans) and a flight from Catania.

**In practice:** Pescheria only in the morning, closed on Sundays · central Catania all on foot, leave the car outside the ZTL · airport about twenty minutes from the centre · one-way car drop-off with margin before the flight.

## How to Shorten It
- **In 10 days you do half**, and it's the advice that holds for most people: the **eastern** half (Etna, Syracuse, Val di Noto, Agrigento) is the right choice the first time, and has a dedicated itinerary on the site.
- **In 12-13 days** you do the complete loop by cutting the slowest days: shorten Palermo to two days, combine Marsala and Zingaro, skip Cefalù and do the Val di Noto in two days instead of three.
- **In 7 days, only the west:** Palermo, Monreale, Segesta, Erice, the salt pans, Motya, Selinunte and Zingaro. It's the Sicily least trodden by international tourism and has the best value on the island.
- **What not to do:** squeeze the complete loop under twelve days. You end up spending the vacation on the state roads.

## What to see / what to do
- **The Palatine Chapel**, with the Arab ceiling above the Byzantine mosaics.
- **Monreale**, six thousand square meters of mosaics, and the cloister with 228 columns.
- **The Triumph of Death and Antonello's Annunciata** at Palazzo Abatellis.
- **The Cave di Cusa**, frozen at the day the Carthaginians arrived.
- **The Temple of Concordia**, saved because it became a church.
- **The Kolymbethra Garden**, where almost no one goes.
- **The mosaics of Villa Romana del Casale.**
- **The Cathedral of Syracuse**, where you touch fifth-century BC Doric columns.
- **Etna, checking the INGV bulletin.**
- **The salt pans at sunset**, with pink water and windmills.
- **The Zingaro Reserve**, born from a protest march.

## Possible extensions
- **Aeolian Islands (+5-8 days):** from Milazzo, with Stromboli in permanent eruption. They have a dedicated itinerary, best by boat.
- **Egadi, Pantelleria and Lampedusa (+7-10 days):** the truly Sicilian islands, with a dedicated itinerary.
- **Madonie and Nebrodi (+2-3 days):** the villages of the mountain interior (Castelbuono, Petralia, Gangi), with woods, mushrooms and a Sicily that looks like no postcard.
- **Malta (+3-4 days):** connected by sea in season from Pozzallo and Catania, it's a coherent historical complement.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **Central Palermo**, between the Massimo and the Quattro Canti, to be within walking distance of everything, with the car in a garage and not on the street.
- **Trapani or Marsala** for the western part: a convenient base for the salt pans, Erice, Motya and Segesta.
- **Agrigento area** only for the night tied to the Valley of the Temples, which is not a city to linger in.
- **Val di Noto**, based in **Ragusa Ibla**, **Modica** or **Noto**: they are all twenty kilometers from one another.
- **Ortigia** in Syracuse, which is the historic center on an island.
- **Etna side** for the last nights: the agriturismi on the slopes have better value than the city, and you're already at altitude for the ascent.
- **Note:** in the west and the interior prices are clearly lower than in eastern Sicily and on the north-east coast.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **In the west:** fish couscous in Trapani and San Vito, busiate with Trapanese pesto, pane ca meusa and panelle in Palermo, sfincione, stigghiola.
- **In the south:** Modica chocolate, the fish of Vendicari, Hyblaean cooking.
- **In the east:** pasta alla Norma in Catania, sea urchins in Syracuse, swordfish.
- **Everywhere:** cannoli **filled to order**, cassata, granita with a brioche col tuppo for breakfast, and Bronte pistachio.
- **To drink:** Etna Rosso and Bianco, Nero d'Avola, Cerasuolo di Vittoria, Grillo, Marsala, and Passito for dessert.
- **General rule:** in Sicily you eat badly mostly in places with a view. In the interior towns and in neighborhood trattorias the average level is very high and prices are low.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Open in Palermo and close in Catania** with a one-way rental: it spares you a return day.
- **Increase by a third** the travel times estimated by the maps on the interior state roads.
- **Don't go in July and August**, the worst period on every single parameter.
- **Check the INGV bulletin** before going up Etna, and have a plan B.
- **Don't do Agrigento, Selinunte or Segesta at midday in summer.**
- **Check the access status of the Scala dei Turchi**, regulated and at times closed.
- **Check the hours and route of the Palatine Chapel**, which change on the days of Regional Assembly activity.
- **Book the wineries**, everywhere: they are not shops.
- **Don't skip the interior** to stay only on the coast: it's where Sicily is most itself.
- **Bring water into the Zingaro Reserve**, where nothing can be bought.
- **Beware of the ZTL** in historic centers and of unauthorized parking attendants in the big cities.

## Chiusura personale
You can do the whole of Sicily, but only if you give it the time it asks for: a loop from Palermo to Catania, with map times stretched by a third and without skipping the interior, which is where the island is most itself. And not in July or August.
