# Milan in 2 Days, the 1-Day Version, and the Lakes as Day Trips

## Apertura personale
Milan has a reputation as a city you don't visit, and the reputation is thirty years out of date. It isn't an art city in the way Florence or Venice are: you don't walk around Milan open-mouthed at every corner, and anyone who arrives expecting that is disappointed by the first afternoon. It's a city to be taken for what it is: a compact center with two or three things of absolute caliber, a density of contemporary art that has no rival in Italy, and a way of spending evenings unlike any other Italian city.

Two days are more than enough for the center. The point is that Milan, more than a destination, is **a base**: it's forty minutes by train from Lake Como, an hour from Bergamo Alta and an hour and a half from the Borromean Islands, and the day on the lake is almost always the strongest memory of the trip.

The thing to do **right away**, even before buying flights, is to try to book the **Last Supper**.

## Scheda pratica
- **The Last Supper (Leonardo's Cenacolo):** it is **the hardest reservation in Italy**. You enter **only by reservation**, in **capped groups**, for a **visit of a few minutes** (on the order of fifteen), because the room's microclimate has to be protected. **Tickets are released in batches months ahead and sell out in no time.** Showing up on the spot is useless. You have to note the date and time sales open in your calendar and try on that very day. Anyone who doesn't succeed hasn't ruined the trip: it has to be accepted beforehand, not in front of the closed door.
- **Duomo:** entry to the cathedral and the **climb to the terraces** are separate tickets, with combined packages that also include the **Duomo Museum** and the **early Christian church of San Giovanni alle Fonti beneath the square**. **The terraces can be reached by elevator or on foot**, and should be done: you walk **among the spires and gargoyles**, on the roof, and it's an experience with no equivalent in Italy. The Duomo has **135 spires and more than three thousand statues**, and its construction lasted almost **six centuries**.
- **Useful reservations:** besides the Last Supper, it's worth booking the Duomo terraces in high season and the **Museo Teatrale at La Scala** if you want to look out over the auditorium from a box (**the view depends on rehearsals under way and is never guaranteed**).
- **Transport:** the metro is the best in Italy and covers everything necessary. You get around Milan by metro and on foot, no exceptions. **The center is Area C**, paid and plate-based: you don't need a car.
- **Airports:** **Malpensa** with the direct train to Cadorna and Centrale; **Linate**, very close, **connected to the center by the metro**; **Bergamo Orio al Serio** with buses, and in that case it's worth considering a stop in **Bergamo Alta** on arrival or departure.
- **When to go:** **spring and autumn.** Milanese summer is muggy and the city empties; **in August many businesses close** and finding something open becomes a problem. Winter is cold and gray but the city works very well, and in December there is the Christmas season around Sant'Ambrogio. **During Salone del Mobile week (April) and the fashion weeks lodging prices triple and the city is full**: check that your dates don't coincide, unless that's exactly the purpose.
- **Food:** **risotto alla milanese** with saffron; **cotoletta**, which in the traditional version is **bone-in and thick**, cooked in butter (the thin, wide one, the so-called "elephant's ear," is another thing, good but different); **ossobuco** with gremolada, **cassoeula** in winter, **michetta** and **panettone**.
- **The aperitivo:** in Milan it's a real institution, not a fad, and between six and eight the city comes out. Areas: Navigli, Brera, Porta Venezia, Isola. It should be distinguished from the all-you-can-eat buffet aperitivo, a student formula that rarely gives the best.
- **Free and underrated:** **San Maurizio al Monastero Maggiore**, **the Cimitero Monumentale**, **Pirelli HangarBicocca**. Three things of very high level that cost nothing (admission may change: check beforehand) and that almost no tourist does.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Duomo, center, Brera and Castello
We began with the **Duomo**, and we began by going up. **The terraces** in the morning, when the light is good and there are fewer people: you walk on the Candoglia marble roof among the spires, with the gargoyles an arm's length away, and at the end is the **Madonnina**. By very long custom it remained the highest point of the city: when taller skyscrapers were built, a copy of the statue was placed on top so as not to break the rule.

Then the interior, huge and dark, with the stained glass, and **the descent to the early Christian church beneath the square**, where there is the **baptistery in which, according to tradition, Ambrose baptized Augustine**.

We crossed the **Galleria Vittorio Emanuele II** (with the ritual of spinning your heel on the bull in the mosaic, touristy but harmless) to **Piazza della Scala**. From outside the **Teatro alla Scala** is deliberately anonymous, and that's part of its identity. The **Museo Teatrale** lets you in and, when there are no rehearsals, **look out over the auditorium from a box**. It's worth it even just to realize how small it is compared with how you imagine it.

We spent the afternoon in **Brera**. The **Pinacoteca** is one of Italy's great museums and has three works that alone justify the ticket: Mantegna's **Dead Christ**, with the foreshortening of the feet in the foreground that in the fifteenth century was a technical provocation; Raphael's **Marriage of the Virgin**; Hayez's **The Kiss**, which is the image of the Risorgimento. Next door is the **Brera Botanical Garden**, a garden hidden among the buildings that almost nobody finds.

The surrounding neighborhood is made of narrow streets and courtyards, and it's the most pleasant part of Milan to cross aimlessly.

At the end of the afternoon we reached the **Sforza Castle**. The courtyard is crossed for free, and inside is the **Rondanini Pietà**, Michelangelo's last sculpture, which he was working on **a few days before dying** at eighty-eight. It's unfinished and re-carved over an earlier version: you can still see an arm from the first draft, detached from the figure. It resembles nothing else of his, and it's displayed alone in a large room, in silence. Behind the castle is **Parco Sempione** with the Arco della Pace.

Aperitivo and evening in **Brera** or at the **Navigli**, with the restored **Darsena** and the two canals, Grande and Pavese. They are what remains of the canal system that carried marble from the quarries of Lake Maggiore to the Duomo's construction site.

### Day 2 — The Last Supper, Sant'Ambrogio and contemporary Milan
The morning is for the **Last Supper**, if you managed to book: you enter in groups, through two airtight doors, and have a few minutes in the refectory. It helps to know what you're looking at. Leonardo **did not paint it as a fresco**, but in tempera on dry wall, so he could work slowly and go back over details, and that's exactly why it began to deteriorate **while he was still alive**. What you see is the result of centuries of restorations and a twenty-two-year intervention. On the opposite wall, which nobody looks at, there's a perfectly preserved Crucifixion by Montorfano: it's the point of comparison that explains everything.

If the Last Supper isn't available, **the morning fills up without problems**. There's **Sant'Ambrogio**, an eleventh-century Romanesque basilica with its quadriporticus, the Carolingian golden altar and the crypt with the bodies of the saints. And above all there's **San Maurizio al Monastero Maggiore**, a couple of steps away, **entirely frescoed by Bernardino Luini and workshop**, which they call the Sistine Chapel of Milan. A wall divides it in two: in front the part for the faithful, behind the part for the cloistered nuns, who followed the services through a grille. **It's free, it's always half empty, and it's the most surprising thing in central Milan.**

The afternoon is for the Milan of the other century, according to taste:
- **Pirelli HangarBicocca:** an enormous former industrial plant converted into an exhibition space, with Anselm Kiefer's permanent installation **"The Seven Heavenly Palaces"**: seven concrete towers up to twenty meters tall, in dim light, inside a shed. **Admission is free** (better to check before going) and it's one of the most powerful things you can see in Italy on the contemporary.
- **Fondazione Prada**, with the Tower, the bar designed by Wes Anderson and the building clad in **gold leaf**.
- **Museo del Novecento**, in Piazza Duomo, which ends with a room overlooking the cathedral: it's the best way to see the Duomo from inside a museum.
- **Cimitero Monumentale**, free, an open-air museum of funerary sculpture: aedicules, temples, monuments of industrial families and the Famedio at the entrance.

At the end of the day, the northern quadrant: **Porta Nuova** with the **Bosco Verticale**, two residential towers with hundreds of real trees on the balconies, which in summer give shade and in winter let the light through, and the walk toward **Isola**, the neighborhood with the most life in new Milan. Alternatively **CityLife**, with the three towers by Isozaki, Hadid and Libeskind and the park around them.

## The One-Day Version
One day in Milan works very well, better than in almost any other Italian city, because the center is compact and the metro is fast. The program:

- **Morning:** **Duomo terraces** at opening, interior of the cathedral, Galleria, Piazza della Scala.
- **Midday:** **San Maurizio al Monastero Maggiore** (twenty minutes, free, and it's the thing you tell people about when you get back) and **Sant'Ambrogio** if time remains. Quick lunch.
- **Afternoon:** **just one thing among the Brera Pinacoteca and the Sforza Castle with the Rondanini Pietà.** Brera if you're interested in painting, the Castle if you want a shorter visit and prefer to stay outdoors.
- **Evening:** aperitivo in Brera or at the Navigli, and dinner.
- **If you have the Last Supper:** it becomes the pivot of the day and you cut the afternoon museum.

**What doesn't fit in one day:** contemporary Milan (HangarBicocca, Fondazione Prada, the skyscrapers). It's the right sacrifice, because they are the most off-center pieces.

## The Lakes as Day Trips
For many people the lake excursion is the best part of a trip to Milan. Three options, in order of convenience.

**Lake Como — the standard choice, and the right one the first time.**
The practical way to do it without a car is the **direct train from Milano Centrale to Varenna**, about an hour. **Varenna** is the right village to start from precisely because **it's the only one of the three classics reachable by train** (Bellagio and Menaggio can only be reached by boat or bus), and it is smaller and more beautiful than its fame suggests, with staircases going down to the lake and the promenade over the water.

From Varenna the **ferry** connects **Bellagio** and **Menaggio** in a few minutes: the triangle can be done in a day by boat, and it's the right way to see the lake, **from the water** and not from the road. **Bellagio** is the postcard village, beautiful and very crowded; **Villa Melzi** in Bellagio and **Villa Carlotta** in Tremezzo have gardens that in May, with the azaleas, are the reason to come in that precise week.

**Villa del Balbianello**, on a promontory between Lenno and Ossuccio, is the most scenic of the lake's villas and is **an FAI property**: **access is regulated, often by reservation, with weekly closing days**, and you reach it on foot along a path or by water taxi. Check everything before going.

**Como city** can be reached in under an hour from Cadorna or Centrale and is a more convenient but less beautiful base: it has the Duomo, the walls, the funicular to Brunate and its silk history. Anyone with only one day should choose **Varenna and the boat triangle**, not Como city.

**Bergamo Alta — the most underrated excursion.**
About fifty minutes by train, then the **funicular** up to the walled city. Piazza Vecchia with the Civic Tower, the **Colleoni Chapel** and the Basilica of Santa Maria Maggiore, the Venetian walls that can be walked with the plain below. It takes half a day, costs very little and has none of the lake's crush. **Try the casoncelli**, and the **polenta**, which here is a serious dish.

**Lake Maggiore and the Borromean Islands — the longest and most scenic excursion.**
Train to **Stresa**, about an hour and a half, and a boat to the islands: **Isola Bella** with its baroque palace and terraced garden with white peacocks, **Isola dei Pescatori**, a still-inhabited fishing village, and **Isola Madre** with the botanical garden. It's a full day and must be organized with the boat timetables in hand.

**Alternatively, for those who want the lake without the crowds, there's Lake Orta**: smaller, harder to reach and much quieter, with the **Island of San Giulio** and the Sacro Monte above Orta.

## What to see / what to do
- **The Duomo terraces**, walking on the roof among the spires.
- **The Last Supper**, if you manage to book it on the day tickets are released.
- **San Maurizio al Monastero Maggiore**, free, half empty, entirely frescoed.
- **The Rondanini Pietà** at the Castle, Michelangelo's last work.
- **Mantegna's Dead Christ** at Brera.
- **Kiefer's Seven Heavenly Palaces** at HangarBicocca.
- **The Cimitero Monumentale**, an open-air sculpture museum.
- **The aperitivo between six and eight**, which in Milan is a social fact.
- **Varenna and the boat triangle** on Lake Como.
- **Bergamo Alta by funicular**, the excursion almost nobody puts on the schedule.

## Possible extensions
- **Certosa di Pavia (+half a day):** half an hour by train, a marble facade that is one of the most beautiful excesses of the Lombard Renaissance.
- **Franciacorta (+1 day):** the area of classic-method sparkling wines between Brescia and Lake Iseo, with wineries **by appointment**. It ties in with the itinerary on the routes of the great wines.
- **Turin (+2 days):** an hour by high-speed train, and it's the city that best completes Milan because in character it is its exact opposite.
- **Dolomites (+5-7 days):** Milan is a convenient starting point heading east for the Alpine itinerary.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **Inside the Navigli ring** (the expanded historic center) to be within walking distance of everything.
- **Brera** for the best location and the highest prices; **Porta Venezia** and **Isola** for the best compromise between price, neighborhood life and metro.
- **The Centrale area** for those who arrive and leave by train or do lake excursions: convenient and not very pleasant.
- **To avoid:** booking without knowing it during the weeks of the Salone del Mobile or fashion, when prices go off the scale.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **Risotto alla milanese**, yellow from saffron and creamy.
- **Bone-in cotoletta**, thick, in butter, and not the thin version, which is a different dish.
- **Ossobuco with gremolada** and, in winter, **cassoeula**.
- **The aperitivo** in the Brera, Navigli, Porta Venezia or Isola area, between six and eight.
- **Panettone** at Christmastime, from the historic pastry shops.
- **To drink:** Franciacorta, the reds of the Oltrepò Pavese and Campari, which was born here.
- **The point about Milan:** it's the Italian city with the best foreign cuisine and the greatest variety. It makes no sense to eat only Milanese for two days.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Try to book the Last Supper on the exact day tickets are released**, months ahead. There are no alternatives.
- **Don't expect a diffuse art city.** Milan is enjoyed if you know what you're going to see.
- **Don't bring a car:** Area C, expensive parking, excellent metro.
- **Check that your dates don't fall in the Salone del Mobile** or the fashion weeks.
- **In August check the opening hours**, because half the city closes.
- **On the lake, start from Varenna** and get around by boat instead of driving on the coastal road, which is narrow, congested and without parking.
- **For Villa del Balbianello, check opening days and reservations** before planning the day.
- **Don't do Como city** if you only have one day: the lake is seen much better from Varenna.

## Chiusura personale
What stays with me about Milan isn't the Duomo, and it's almost embarrassing to admit. It's the afternoon when, by chance or because someone suggested it, you push open the door of San Maurizio on Corso Magenta (a flat, anonymous facade, on a street where the trams run) and inside is a church painted entirely, from floor to ceiling, without a single free wall. There's no line, no ticket office, hardly anyone. You sit for ten minutes and look at the dividing wall that separated the cloistered nuns from the rest of the world, with the grille through which they followed Mass, and then you go out and take the tram again.

Milan works like that: it doesn't put anything on display, it doesn't explain what it has, and it lets the best things be found by whoever walks past. It's a city to be courted rather than visited, and that's why someone who spent two days there judges it badly and someone who lived there two years never speaks ill of it again.
