# Normandy and Brittany in Two Weeks

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 work in progress (itinerary and practical information verified)
**Periodo del viaggio:**
**Durata:** 12 days, 11 nights
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independently, with a rental car
**Categoria:** Europa · Francia · Normandia · Bretagna · storia · costa

---

## Apertura personale
Normandy and Brittany are two different trips set one after the other: one historical and dense, the other about coast and walking. The order matters (you go from dense to slow, not the other way around) and this itinerary follows that logic: five days of Normandy, seven of Brittany, with Mont-Saint-Michel as the hinge between the two regions.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** May-September. The weather remains a variable in every month: always bring the rain jacket.
- **A car is necessary** for almost the whole route, except for the main cities.
- **Mont-Saint-Michel:** before leaving, check the calendar of the big tides; better to sleep in the bay than to see it as a day trip from afar.
- **Rip currents** on the Atlantic beaches: a serious danger, not a formal warning.
- **From the airport to the centre:** the trip starts in Rouen and the handiest airport is **Paris Charles de Gaulle (CDG)**, about 2 hours' drive away, where it makes sense to pick up the rental car · without a car: RER B to Gare du Nord in about 35 minutes, about €13 from the machines, metro to Saint-Lazare and train to Rouen in about 1h15-1h30, 2h30-3 hours in all · official flat-rate taxi to Paris about €56-65, only from the signed rank: anyone offering a ride inside the terminal is unlicensed · at the end of the trip **Rennes (RNS)**, a little over an hour from Vannes: bus C6 to the centre in about 25 minutes, about €2, or a taxi in about 15 minutes.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno
### Day 1 — Rouen
We started with the dense Normandy, the one of history. In **Rouen** there's the cathedral Monet painted again and again, the Gros-Horloge over the street and the square where Joan of Arc was burned. One day is enough to get into the rhythm. You start at **Notre-Dame Cathedral**, whose façade changes color over the day, as in Monet's series: the warmest light comes in the afternoon, so it is worth coming back.

From there the Rue du **Gros-Horloge** leads under the Renaissance clock, which can also be visited from inside, to the **Place du Vieux-Marché**, where Joan of Arc was burned in 1431: today there is a modern church with a scaled roof. In the afternoon the **Historial Jeanne d'Arc**, in the archbishop's palace, tells the story of her trial, and the **Aître Saint-Maclou**, an old half-timbered ossuary, shows the most unusual side of Rouen. *The old town is small and walkable: pick up the rental car the next day.*

**In practice:** from Paris to Rouen is about an hour and a half by train · the cathedral is free to enter outside service times · the Gros-Horloge and the Historial have separate tickets, a few euros for the first and a little more for the second.

### Day 2 — The D-Day beaches (part 1)
We moved to Bayeux, the base for the beaches, but before seeing them we stopped at the **Mémorial de Caen**. The order matters: without the context of the museum, the beaches are just beaches. From Rouen to Caen is about an hour and a half by car. The **Mémorial** deserves at least three hours: the route starts in the 1930s and runs through the war to the landings and the Battle of Normandy, with a section on the Cold War.

Arrive at opening time, have lunch at the museum and in the afternoon continue to **Bayeux**, half an hour away, where you drop your bags for three nights. In the evening you stroll through the center, around the cathedral and along the river Aure, with its old water mills. Bayeux was one of the first towns liberated in June 1944 and came through the war almost intact: the center is still the medieval one.

**In practice:** Mémorial de Caen about 21 euros, open 9 a.m. to 7 p.m. from April to September, with shorter hours in winter and an annual closure of a few weeks in January · book online in high season · to sleep, Bayeux, less than half an hour from all the beaches.

### Day 3 — The D-Day beaches (part 2)
We spent a whole day on the landing sectors: **Omaha Beach**, the American cemetery at Colleville, the **Pointe du Hoc** and the artificial harbor at Arromanches. Omaha is a perfectly ordinary beach, and that's exactly what makes it hard to bear. The most logical sequence runs from west to east. You start in the morning at the **Pointe du Hoc**, the cliff that the American Rangers scaled with ropes and ladders, the ground still pitted with bomb craters.

Then **Omaha Beach** and, above the beach, the **American cemetery at Colleville**, with more than nine thousand white crosses lined up toward the sea and a visitor center that tells the names and stories. Along the way you can stop at the German battery of **Longues-sur-Mer**, with its guns still in the casemates. The afternoon ends in **Arromanches**, where at low tide you can see the remains of the Mulberry artificial harbor.

**In practice:** the American cemetery and the Pointe du Hoc are free, the cemetery closes in the late afternoon with hours that change between summer and winter · the sites are twenty to thirty minutes apart by car · enter the cemetery in silence and dressed respectfully.

### Day 4 — Bayeux and the Tapestry
We devoted the morning to the **Bayeux Tapestry** (check beforehand the state of the renovation work). The afternoon is free, in Bayeux or at the seaside in Cabourg. For the **Tapestry**, checking before you leave is essential: the Bayeux museum closed for works in September 2025, with reopening planned for 2027, and between September 2026 and July 2027 the embroidery is on loan to the British Museum in London: anyone traveling in that period will not find it in Bayeux.

There is still **Notre-Dame Cathedral**, consecrated in 1077 in the presence of William the Conqueror, with its Romanesque crypt. Just outside the center is the **Museum of the Battle of Normandy** and, opposite it, the **British war cemetery**, the largest Second World War British cemetery in France. In the afternoon **Cabourg**, the Belle Époque seaside resort, with its promenade and the Grand Hôtel that Proust used to frequent.

**In practice:** before leaving, check the museum's website for the state of the works and the reopening date · the cathedral is free to enter · from Bayeux to Cabourg about fifty minutes by car.

### Day 5 — Étretat and Honfleur
We headed to the coast: the cliffs of **Étretat**, with the chalk arches over the sea, then **Honfleur** and its Vieux Bassin. In the late afternoon we aimed toward Mont-Saint-Michel. At the table, during these Norman days, there was camembert and cider, with calvados in the middle of the meal. The day involves a lot of driving: from Bayeux to **Étretat** is about two hours, crossing the Seine estuary on the Pont de Normandie.

In Étretat you first climb the **Falaise d'Aval**, from where you see the Porte d'Aval and the rock needle, and then the **Falaise d'Amont**, with the sailors' chapel. You drive back to **Honfleur**, less than an hour away, for lunch around the **Vieux Bassin**, the harbor lined with tall narrow houses, and for the church of Sainte-Catherine, built of wood. Then you set off for the Mont, with a good two and a half hours on the road.

**In practice:** the Pont de Normandie is a toll bridge · in Étretat the central parking lots fill up early, the one at the entrance to the village is easier · *if you go down to the beach below the cliffs, check the tide: the water closes the passages faster than it looks.*

### Day 6 — Mont-Saint-Michel
The Mont is the hinge between the two regions, and should be experienced by sleeping in the bay, not seen as a day trip from afar (the calendar of the big tides should be checked before leaving). We arrived in the early afternoon and walked on the causeway with the Mont growing in front of us. We visited the abbey late in the day, when the buses had left. The bay, on foot, is never crossed alone.

The access system is simple once you understand it: you leave the car in the parking lots on the mainland and reach the Mont on foot, a walk of about two and a half kilometers that ends on the footbridge, or with the free **Le Passeur** shuttle, which takes about twelve minutes. The village and the ramparts are free; you pay for the **abbey**, at the top, with its suspended cloister and the Knights' Hall. During the **big tides** the Mont becomes an island again for a few hours, and you watch the show from the ramparts. Crossing the bay on foot is done only with an authorized guide, because of the quicksand and the speed at which the water rises.

**In practice:** the shuttle runs from the parking lots from 7:30 a.m. to midnight · parking is paid by the day, more expensive in high season · the abbey ticket costs around 13-16 euros depending on the season: book online and check the summer evening openings.

### Day 7 — Mont-Saint-Michel at dawn, then Saint-Malo
We went back to look at it at dawn, then entered Brittany and the trip slowed down. In **Saint-Malo** we walked the ramparts with the tide, and in Cancale, nearby, there are oysters. At dawn the best spot is the dam on the Couesnon, at the start of the footbridge, with the Mont backlit and the bay still empty. Then an hour's drive to **Saint-Malo**, the corsair city enclosed in its walls.

The **ramparts walk** is free and takes about an hour; at low tide you can walk out to the islet of **Grand Bé**, where Chateaubriand is buried, but you must come back before the water covers the passage. In the afternoon **Cancale**, twenty minutes away, with oysters eaten at the market on the harbor, looking out at the oyster beds in the bay. If you have time, go on to the **Pointe du Grouin**, one of the finest headlands on the north coast.

**In practice:** access times for Grand Bé depend on the tide and are posted at the start of the passage · in Saint-Malo leave the car in the parking lots outside the walls · to sleep, Saint-Malo or Dinan, as a base for the north coast.

### Day 8 — Dinan and the Pink Granite Coast
**Dinan** is a medieval town enclosed in its walls. Then the north coast, with the first stop on the **Côte de Granit Rose**. On paper Breton distances seem small and they aren't: better not to underestimate them. **Dinan** is explored in the morning: you enter the half-timbered center, walk down the steep **Rue du Jerzual** to the little port on the river Rance, and climb back along the walls, with a view of the viaduct.

Lunch is in the center, with a galette. In the afternoon you head west, on roads through farmland and villages: it is about an hour and a half by car to **Perros-Guirec**. The **Côte de Granit Rose** looks best toward evening, when the low sun turns the granite boulders on the shore pink and orange. *Breton roads are free but slow: distances in kilometers are misleading, what counts is time.*

**In practice:** in Dinan the most convenient parking is near the walls, above the center · dual carriageways in Brittany are toll-free · to sleep, Perros-Guirec or Trégastel, to have Ploumanac'h close at hand the next morning.

### Day 9 — Ploumanac'h and the GR34
In the morning we were at **Ploumanac'h**, among the pink granite blocks. Then we did a stretch of the **GR34**, the customs officers' path that goes around the whole coast: it never ends, and the landscape changes every two hours of walking. On the Atlantic beaches rip currents are a serious danger, not a formal warning.

The most famous stretch of the **GR34** around here is the **Sentier des Douaniers** between Perros-Guirec and Ploumanac'h, about three hours there and back, among oddly shaped rocks, coves and the **Mean Ruz lighthouse**, itself made of pink granite. Walk it early, before the crowds, and finish in Ploumanac'h with a coffee on the harbor. In the afternoon, if you want to keep walking, continue toward Trégastel, among beaches and islets reachable at low tide. *The GR34 is more than two thousand kilometers long: here you get a taste, choosing the stretch to suit your legs.*

**In practice:** the Sentier des Douaniers is easy and waymarked, suitable for children too · swim only at supervised beaches, where flags show the day's conditions · in Ploumanac'h the harbor parking fills up early in summer.

### Day 10 — Crozon Peninsula
We moved west, to the **Crozon peninsula**: cliffs and very few people. From Perros-Guirec to the peninsula is about two hours by road, so leave early. The Crozon peninsula is shaped like a cross, with four points on the sea: the most spectacular is the **Pointe de Pen-Hir**, with its sheer cliffs and the **Tas de Pois** sea stacks in front.

Close by is **Camaret-sur-Mer**, a fishing port with the Vauban tower and abandoned boats beached on the shore. In the afternoon the wilder **Cap de la Chèvre**, and **Morgat**, with its sheltered beach and sea caves visited by boat when the sea allows. The GR34 runs along the whole peninsula, and even an hour's walk from one point to another is enough.

**In practice:** boat trips to the Morgat caves are seasonal and depend on the sea · on the headlands the wind is often strong, so bring a jacket even in summer · to sleep, Crozon, Morgat or Camaret, with few hotels that need booking in good time.

### Day 11 — Carnac and the Gulf of Morbihan
The **Carnac alignments**, then the **Gulf of Morbihan**, with a possible trip to Gavrinis. In the evening, savory galettes and sweet crêpes, with cider drunk from a bowl. From Crozon to Carnac is about two hours. The **alignments** are thousands of menhirs in rows kilometers long, in the fields of Ménec, Kermario and Kerlescan: start at the **Maison des Mégalithes**, which explains the site, then walk the path that runs around the fields.

The afternoon is for the **Gulf of Morbihan**, an inland sea full of islands, where the tide creates strong currents between the headlands. It is best seen from the little harbors of Larmor-Baden or Arradon, or from a boat. The **Gavrinis cairn**, a megalithic tomb covered in carvings, can only be reached by boat from Larmor-Baden, a fifteen-minute crossing. *Book the Gavrinis trip in advance: there are few places each day.*

**In practice:** from April to September you can enter the enclosures of the alignments only on a guided tour, from October to March access is free, and the path around them is open all year · Gavrinis can be visited from spring to autumn, with hours and prices to check · to sleep, Carnac or Vannes.

### Day 12 — Departure
The last morning is in Vannes or Auray, then you head home. **Vannes** takes a couple of hours: the walls with their gardens and old wash houses, the half-timbered houses around Saint-Pierre Cathedral and the harbor, where the gulf begins. Alternatively **Auray**, with the port of **Saint-Goustan**, the quarter of stone and timber houses on the river, smaller and quieter.

Early in the morning both are empty, and it is the best time for photos of the harbors. Have lunch with a last plate of seafood and leave in the early afternoon. *If you have to return the car in Paris or at a distant airport, count on many hours of driving: consider going back by train from Vannes.*

**In practice:** from Vannes to Paris the TGV takes about two and a half to three hours · Rennes, with its station and airport, is a little over an hour's drive from Vannes · returning the car in Brittany rather than where you picked it up may carry a fee.

## What to see / what to do
- The D-Day beaches, preceded by a museum that gives the context.
- Mont-Saint-Michel, sleeping in the bay, seen in the evening and at dawn.
- A stretch of the GR34, in any Breton coastal town.
- The Carnac alignments.

## Possible extensions
- **Belle-Île or Île de Bréhat (+1-2 days):** a Breton island by ferry.
- **Western Brittany, Ouessant (+1-2 days):** the westernmost island.
- **Paris (+3-5 days):** a natural connection from Rouen or Rennes.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
Bayeux for the D-Day beaches, the bay of the Mont for Mont-Saint-Michel, Saint-Malo or Dinan for the north coast, Carnac or Vannes for the Morbihan.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
Camembert and cider in Normandy, calvados in the middle of the meal, savory galettes and sweet crêpes in Brittany with cider in a bowl, oysters at Cancale.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Right order**: a museum before the D-Day beaches.
- **Don't cross the bay of the Mont alone.**
- **Don't underestimate Breton distances.**
- **Always bring the rain gear.**

## Chiusura personale
What stays with you is the contrast between Omaha Beach, which is a perfectly ordinary beach and for that very reason hard to bear, and the GR34 in Brittany, which never ends and gives back another kind of intensity: that of a landscape that changes every two hours of walking.
