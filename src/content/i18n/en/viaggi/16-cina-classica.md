# Classic China in 15 Days: Beijing, Xi'an, Shanghai, Hong Kong and Macau

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 in progress
**Periodo del viaggio:**
**Durata:** 15 days, 14 nights
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independent, with high-speed trains booked from Italy
**Categoria:** Asia · Cina · grandi classici · alta velocità · megalopoli · storia

---

## Apertura personale
Classic China is the trip everyone pictures when they think of China: the Great Wall, the Terracotta Army, the Bund. It's also the trip that, more than any other, gets decided before you leave, sitting at a computer, because here almost nothing that works elsewhere works: not credit cards, not maps, not tickets bought on the spot. If you show up with four things sorted (trains, payments, connection, museum reservations), you find a country that is surprisingly easy, safe and cheap. If you put them off, you'll sort them out anyway, but you'll lose your first three days. This itinerary is built on the single east-west axis that high-speed rail makes simple, and it ends in two places that are China only on paper: Hong Kong and Macau.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** April-May or September-October. In any case avoid Golden Week (October 1-7) and Chinese New Year: half the country is on the move, trains are sold out weeks ahead and the booking sites become unusable. The first days of May are also holidays and crowded.
- **Documents/visa:** passport valid for at least 6 months. Italian citizens can enter visa-free for up to 30 consecutive days, under an exemption extended through December 31, 2026, with multiple entries allowed. Hong Kong and Macau have their own border: you leave and re-enter, and each re-entry into mainland China starts a new 30-day period. Every foreigner must be registered with the police within 24 hours of arrival. Hotels do it at check-in; if you're staying in a private apartment you have to go in person.
- **Currency:** renminbi/yuan (CNY). Hong Kong uses the Hong Kong dollar, Macau the pataca (but Hong Kong dollars are accepted at par).
- **Payments, critical point number one:** mainland China lives almost entirely without cash. **Alipay and WeChat Pay must be installed, verified and linked to a Visa or Mastercard before you leave**, with a stable connection and your passport at hand: doing it once you've landed is much harder. Physical cards only work in high-end hotels. By law cash can't be refused, but many small vendors don't have change: keep it as a backup, not as your main method. ATMs that accept foreign cards exist (the most reliable are Bank of China and ICBC), but not all of them do. **Turn the VPN off when paying**, otherwise the anti-fraud system blocks the transaction.
- **Connectivity, critical point number two:** the Great Firewall blocks Google in every form, WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook, YouTube and most news sites. The simplest solution is a **foreign roaming eSIM**, which routes your traffic outside China and gets around the problem with nothing else needed. A local SIM costs less but goes through the firewall and needs a VPN, which you must install and test **before** leaving, because app stores in China don't offer one. Download these anyway beforehand: offline maps, a camera translator and, to get around like the locals, Amap.
- **Trains, critical point number three:** tickets go on sale **15 days before departure**, at a time that changes from station to station (the 12306 app tells you), and on the busiest routes they're gone within hours. The official 12306 app has an English version that accepts registration with a passport and payment with a foreign card, but account verification takes time and needs to be done well in advance. Alternatively, Trip.com takes bookings up to two months ahead and converts them as soon as sales open, for a fee. The ticket is electronic and tied to your passport, which becomes your travel document: you touch it to the turnstile reader and walk through. At the station you need 30-40 minutes of margin, and the gates close a few minutes before departure, no exceptions.
- **Mandatory reservations:** the Forbidden City and the Terracotta Army have named entry tied to your passport and **physical ticket offices closed**: without an online reservation you don't get in. The Forbidden City sells out days ahead; the Terracotta Army opens sales only 7 days before. The Shaanxi History Museum is free, but the daily quotas run out in minutes.
- **Approximate budget:** up to Shanghai, much lower than you'd expect: trains 40-90€ per leg, good hotels 40-70€, meals 4-10€. Hong Kong flips the math: it's the most expensive city of the trip, especially for lodging.
- **Language:** outside high-end hotels, English almost doesn't exist, much less than in Japan or Thailand. The camera translator is the tool that makes you independent, and the hotel address saved in Chinese characters solves the taxi problem.
- **Scams:** the tea ceremony and the "art student" are the two constants in Beijing, Xi'an and on Shanghai's Nanjing Road. They've worked for twenty years and still work. There's only one rule: don't follow into a venue anyone who stopped you on the street.
- **Electricity:** 220V, type A and I outlets, often combined. The small Italian two-pin plug fits many outlets but not all, and the Schuko and three-pin ones don't: bring a universal adapter.
- **From the airport to the centre:** at Beijing Capital (PEK) the Capital Airport Express runs to Dongzhimen in about 20-30 minutes for 25 yuan; from Daxing (PKX) the Daxing Airport Express reaches Caoqiao in about 20 minutes for 35 yuan, then you carry on by metro · pay with Alipay or the metro QR code in the app · taxis only from the official queue, about 100-150 yuan from Capital and 150-200 from Daxing; no drivers offering rides in the arrivals hall · leaving from Hong Kong (HKG), the Airport Express links Hong Kong Station in 24 minutes for about HKD 120 (less from Kowloon and with Octopus), until about half past midnight; buses A21 and A11 cost about HKD 40.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Arrival in Beijing
You land at Capital (PEK) or Daxing (PKX): check which one before booking the transfer, because Daxing is much farther out. Both are connected to the center by metro or express trains. *Before leaving, check the current entry rules: in recent years Italians have been able to enter visa-free for short stays, but conditions and deadlines change.* When you leave the airport it helps to already have an international SIM or an eSIM with a built-in VPN, because many Western apps don't work in China.

We kept the first day light on purpose, because the jet lag hits: a walk through the hutong around the Drum Tower and Houhai Lake and a first dinner at a neighborhood restaurant, to test the camera translator on the menu. It's also the moment to check in a shop that Alipay or WeChat Pay work, before finding out in an awkward situation. The **hutong**, the lanes of courtyard houses of old Beijing, are best explored on foot or by bike, aimlessly, stepping into courtyards only where the door is open.

**In practice:** Daxing to the center by express train about 20 minutes to Caoqiao plus the metro, Capital to the center by Airport Express in about half an hour · Alipay and WeChat Pay accept foreign credit cards, link them before leaving · some cash in yuan is still useful for small stalls · during Chinese holidays (Lunar New Year and the first week of October) trains and museums are overrun: better avoided.

### Day 2 — Forbidden City, Tiananmen and Jingshan
We entered the **Forbidden City** at opening time, with the booking made weeks before and passport in hand: without it, you don't get in. The route is one-way from south to north. Leave the central axis early, where ninety percent of visitors walk, and slip into the side courtyards to the east, where the halls are small and almost empty. *Allow at least three hours, and inside there are few places to eat: water and a snack in your backpack.*

Out of the north gate, we crossed the road and climbed the steps of **Jingshan Hill**: ten minutes up and you have the whole complex below, golden roofs lined up all the way to Tiananmen. Almost nobody pairs this with the visit, yet without this view the layout of the palace stays incomprehensible. In the afternoon **Tiananmen Square** (security checks at the entrance, passport required) and, in the evening, Peking duck at a place that does it professionally.

**In practice:** Forbidden City closed on Mondays (except holidays), named tickets on the official website or via app with your passport number, sales open a few days ahead and places run out · Jingshan with a ticket of a few yuan · Tiananmen may require an online reservation, check beforehand · Peking duck at the historic restaurants should be booked for the evening.

### Day 3 — The Great Wall at Jinshanling
A full day: early-morning departure and about 2h30 of transfer. **Jinshanling** is the most accessible only partly restored stretch from Beijing, about ten kilometers of wall along a ridge, with half-collapsed watchtowers and grass between the stones. You walk 3-4 hours on uneven steps, without a meter of shade and with no refreshment points: water, hat and shoes with good soles are mandatory. In return, after half an hour of walking you turn around and there's nobody.

*Alternatives if a full day isn't possible: **Mutianyu**, restored but beautiful, with a chairlift up and a toboggan down, half a day; **Gubei Water Town and Simatai**, the only stretch you can visit at night, which however requires a night outside the city. **Badaling** only as a last resort: it's the most convenient and the most crowded in the country.* The best light on the ridge is in the morning and late afternoon; in winter the wind on the wall is freezing and the steps can ice over, while in summer the heat weighs more than the effort.

**In practice:** for Jinshanling a car with driver or an organized tour from Beijing, as public transport is awkward · entrance ticket of about 65 yuan from April to October and 55 the rest of the year, cable car extra · Gubei Water Town with online booking and a night in one of the town's hotels · on Chinese holidays all the stretches near Beijing fill up.

### Day 4 — Temple of Heaven, hutongs and Summer Palace
Early start and the **Temple of Heaven** at opening, but for the park more than the temple: there, every morning, hundreds of retirees dance, sing in choirs, play cards and do sword exercises. It's the most beautiful and least touristy scene in the city, and by ten it's over. On the way north it's worth stopping at the **Lama Temple** (Yonghegong), the city's great Tibetan Buddhist monastery, still active, with incense smoke in the courtyards and the eighteen-meter Buddha carved from a single sandalwood trunk in the last hall.

In the afternoon we went to the **Summer Palace**, on Kunming Lake, less monumental than the Forbidden City and much nicer to walk. The painted Long Corridor and Longevity Hill take a couple of hours; with more time you cross the lake by boat. Then back to the center and a last evening in the hutong. A warning: the ones around Houhai are now bars and shops, real life is in the lanes two blocks further on.

**In practice:** the Temple of Heaven park opens very early, the halls later, with a combined ticket of a few dozen yuan · Lama Temple on metro lines 2 and 5, Yonghegong stop, entry a few dozen yuan, modest clothing and no photos inside the halls · Summer Palace about an hour by metro from the center, named tickets with passport.

### Day 5 — Train to Xi'an and the walls by bike
**High-speed train Beijing-Xi'an, about 4h30.** At the station allow 30-40 minutes of margin: checks are serious and stations are as big as airports. Tickets are in your name: buy them on the official Chinese railway app or on platforms like Trip.com with your passport, which then works as the ticket at the gates. *On Chinese holidays trains sell out weeks ahead: buy as soon as sales open.*

At Xi'an North we took the metro to the center and settled inside the walls. In the late afternoon, the **ride around the Ming walls by bike**: fourteen kilometers of wall, wide enough to cycle on, with the old city on one side and skyscrapers on the other. The right moment is the last hour of light, when the red lanterns light up on the gate towers. Dinner in stages in the **Muslim Quarter**: cumin lamb skewers, roujiamo, biang biang noodles. You eat a little at each stall and move on, without sitting down.

**In practice:** Beijing West station for Xi'an, check on the ticket · you get onto the walls at one of the main gates, ticket and bike rental separate, the loop takes about two hours · rental closes before sunset: start in time · in the Muslim Quarter ask prices first, and in souvenir shops you bargain.

### Day 6 — Terracotta Army
We spent the morning at the **museum of Qin Shi Huang's mausoleum**, 40 minutes from the center. The reservation is named and tied to your passport, and sales open only 7 days before: put it in your calendar like an appointment.

**Public bus 5 (306)** leaves from the train station, cheap and direct. In the station square, meanwhile, there are "tourist buses" that take you to minor mausoleums and pass them off as the main site: it's the most common rip-off in the city.

On site, some people recommend visiting the pits in reverse order (three, two, one), because after number one the others look like construction sites. Don't skip the **bronze chariots museum** out of tiredness: the two chariots are the most technically impressive pieces of the whole site.

The afternoon is free for the **Giant Wild Goose Pagoda** or the **Shaanxi History Museum** (free, but with daily quotas that run out in minutes).

**In practice:** entry about 120 yuan, named online booking up to 7 days ahead · bus 306 from the east square of Xi'an railway station, about an hour · a guide or audio guide at the entrance helps to read the pits · for the afternoon, the Shaanxi History Museum should be booked as soon as sales open, closed on Mondays.

### Day 7 — Train to Shanghai and the Bund
**High-speed train Xi'an-Shanghai, about 6 hours.** It's the longest leg of the trip and best taken in the morning, to have the afternoon free. On board there's free hot water and a trolley with simple meals, but many travelers bring snacks and instant noodles: worth doing the same. *On arrival, check whether the train stops at Shanghai Hongqiao or another station, because metro times to the center vary.*

We settled in the former French Concession, the nicest area to sleep in, and the first outing was to the **Bund at blue hour**, when the lights of Pudong come on and the sky isn't black yet. The trick worth more than a tourist cruise is the **public ferry** across the river for a few yuan: from the deck you see the colonial buildings on one side and the skyscrapers on the other. The Bund and Pudong lights go off late in the evening, so be there early.

**In practice:** from Hongqiao to the French Concession about 30-40 minutes by metro · public ferry between the Bund and Pudong for a few yuan, paid with a transport card or QR code · on holiday evenings the Bund is packed and access is restricted · French Concession hotels should be booked early for weekends.

### Day 8 — Shanghai: French Concession, Yuyuan, Pudong
In the morning on foot in the **former French Concession**, among the plane trees and low brick houses: it's the Shanghai that matters, and you only discover it by walking. The lanes of **Wukang Road** and around, with 1930s villas and cafés, are easy to cover in a couple of hours, stepping into the longtang, residential lanes closed by a gateway.

In the afternoon the **Yuyuan Garden** and the old-city bazaar (very crowded, best early or late) and then a viewpoint in **Pudong**: the Shanghai Tower or a rooftop bar, which costs as much as a cocktail and has the head-on view. Evening in the French Concession bars. Watch out on Nanjing Road: it's where the tea scam is most widespread in all of China. *The scam is always the same: friendly young people suggesting a tea ceremony or a bar, and in the end a bill of hundreds of euros. Just decline with a smile.*

**In practice:** Yuyuan with a separate ticket for the garden, the surrounding bazaar is free · at the bazaar and markets bargain hard, starting from a fraction of the asking price · Shanghai Tower observatory with a ticket of a couple of hundred yuan, pointless in haze · Shanghai metro is convenient and payable with the Alipay QR code.

### Day 9 — Suzhou and the classical gardens
A day in **Suzhou**, 25 minutes by high-speed train from Shanghai. Do it **on a weekday**: the gardens are small and at weekends the crowds erase exactly what they exist for. Leave early and reach the first garden at opening time, when the light is still low over the ponds and the groups haven't arrived.

Two or three gardens are more than enough. The **Humble Administrator's Garden** is the most famous and requires online booking with passport; the **Master of the Nets Garden** is the smallest, the least visited and probably the most beautiful. They are compositions built on a precise rule, that no opening frames the same scene twice, and walking inside you grasp it immediately. Between gardens walk along **Pingjiang Road**, the canal street of the old town, with its white houses and stone bridges.

**In practice:** frequent trains from Shanghai to Suzhou or Suzhou North, bookable even the day before · garden tickets of a few dozen yuan, in your name with passport · avoid the gardens during Chinese holidays · in town get around by metro or by taxi via app.

### Day 10 — A water village
A day in one of the canal towns around Shanghai. **Zhujiajiao** is reached by metro in an hour and is the most convenient, but also the most commercial. **Tongli** combines well with Suzhou and has a UNESCO garden. **Wuzhen** is the best kept and the most photogenic, especially the west sector lit up at night, but it's a museum-town from which residents were moved out. **Nanxun** is the least visited and the only one still partly inhabited.

*The choice changes the day more than it seems: if you just want to see a canal, take Zhujiajiao and don't think twice; if you have a night to spend, sleep in Wuzhen and see the west sector after sunset.* In all the towns the early morning and after-dinner hours are the quietest, while at midday the groups from Shanghai arrive. Rowing boats on the canals have fixed prices posted at the pier; in souvenir shops, on the other hand, you bargain.

**In practice:** Zhujiajiao by metro line 17 from Shanghai, free entry to the town and tickets for individual sites · Wuzhen and Nanxun by bus or car from Shanghai in about an hour and a half to two · for Wuzhen West an entrance ticket, more expensive with the evening visit · at weekends and on holidays all the towns are crowded.

### Day 11 — To Hong Kong
**High-speed train Shanghai-Hong Kong West Kowloon (about 8 hours)** or a flight from Shanghai (2h30). On paper the train is fascinating; in practice, over this distance, the flight saves half a day. Either way you cross a **real border**, with passport control leaving mainland China and entering Hong Kong. Italians enter without a visa for 90 days.

*Hong Kong has different rules from the mainland: Western apps and websites work without a VPN, you pay with an Octopus card or by card, and the currency is the Hong Kong dollar.* We spent the first evening on the **Tsim Sha Tsui** waterfront, with the island skyline opposite, and had our first taste of cha chaan teng, local diners serving hybrid Anglo-Cantonese food for a few dollars. At eight in the evening the light show on the skyscrapers can be watched from the waterfront, free.

**In practice:** at West Kowloon border controls are inside the station, arrive early for departure · Octopus card on sale at the airport and in MTR stations, valid for metro, trams, ferries and shops · from Tsim Sha Tsui the metro and Star Ferry reach the island in minutes · Hong Kong hotels are expensive and small: book early.

### Day 12 — Hong Kong: Peak, Star Ferry, markets
We went up **Victoria Peak**, to be done only with clear skies, because up there the view is what counts. The historic tram has a queue that by late afternoon exceeds an hour; bus 15, which goes up from the same area, leaves right away and costs a fraction. At the top skip the paid platform and take **Lugard Road**, the flat path around the summit: same view, free and with nobody.

The afternoon between **Sheung Wan** and the longest outdoor escalators in the world, the evening in the **Mong Kok** markets, from the flower market to Temple Street. Sheung Wan is the district of dried herb and mushroom shops, neighborhood temples like Man Mo and steep streets. *In the Mong Kok and Temple Street markets bargain hard: the first price is almost always high, and you can walk away without embarrassment.* At some point in the day, then, the harbor crossing on the **Star Ferry**: ten minutes, less than half a euro, and the best view of the city.

**In practice:** bus 15 to the Peak from Central Exchange Square, paid with the Octopus card · Peak Tram with online tickets and a quicker ride up in the morning · Star Ferry between Central and Tsim Sha Tsui with frequent sailings until late evening · Temple Street Night Market comes alive after 7 p.m.

### Day 13 — Macau in a day
To get there you can take the **fast ferry from Hong Kong (about an hour)** or the bus across the Hong Kong-Zhuhai-Macau Bridge, the longest sea bridge in the world. Another passport check in both directions.

The morning in the **UNESCO historic center**: the Ruins of St. Paul's, Largo do Senado with its wavy Portuguese paving, the Fortaleza do Monte, the A-Ma temple. You can do it all on foot in half a day, and it's smaller than you'd expect.

**Macanese** lunch, the oldest fusion cuisine in the world (galinha à africana, minchi, pastéis de nata), best in **Taipa Village**, along Rua do Cunha.

The afternoon on the **Cotai** strip, with the casinos' free shuttle buses, which everyone uses even without gambling: in twenty minutes you go from a Portuguese town to a life-size replica of the Grand Canal. The point of the day lies in this jump.

In the evening we went back to Hong Kong.

**In practice:** fast ferries from Hong Kong (Sheung Wan or Kowloon) to Macau with frequent sailings, or shuttle buses over the bridge from the terminal near the airport · passport for both controls, Italians need no visa · Macau uses the pataca, but Hong Kong dollars are accepted almost everywhere · for the evening return buy the ticket back on arrival.

### Day 14 — Lantau and the Hong Kong you don't expect
The morning on **Lantau** island: the Ngong Ping panoramic cable car, the Big Buddha and the stilt fishing village of **Tai O**. The cable car flies over the bay and hills for about twenty-five minutes; the Big Buddha is reached by a stairway of over two hundred steps, and next to it is Po Lin Monastery. From Ngong Ping to Tai O it's about twenty minutes down by bus. *In strong wind or storms the cable car stops: in that case go up by bus from Tung Chung.*

Alternatively, for those who prefer walking, there's the **Dragon's Back**: a panoramic trail above the south coast, half an hour by metro and bus from the financial center, with the sea below and not a building in sight. It's the half of Hong Kong nobody puts on the program. The trail takes a couple of hours and ends at Big Wave Bay or Shek O, where you can swim before heading back. Last dinner with Cantonese **roast goose**, among the best things eaten on the whole trip.

**In practice:** Lantau by metro to Tung Chung, then the cable car with online tickets, shorter queue early in the morning · the glass-bottomed cabin costs more · Dragon's Back from Shau Kei Wan metro with bus 9, hiking shoes and water · in summer leave early, before the humid heat.

### Day 15 — Departure
Airport Express from Hong Kong Central to the airport in 24 minutes. With an afternoon flight there's time for a last dim sum and a last Star Ferry crossing. Dim sum is eaten mainly at breakfast and lunch, and at the most popular tea houses people queue from opening time: arrive early.

Many airlines allow in-town check-in at the Airport Express Hong Kong station, dropping your bags there: you wander free until the last moment and go to the airport with just a backpack. *The service isn't available for every airline and closes some hours before the flight: check in advance.*

**In practice:** Airport Express from Hong Kong Station or Kowloon, cheaper as a return ticket or with the Octopus card · in-town check-in at the station for many airlines · for intercontinental flights get to the airport at least two to three hours ahead · any remaining Octopus balance is refundable at MTR counters.

## What to see / what to do
- **The right stretch of Wall.** Jinshanling with a full day and legs in shape, Mutianyu with half a day, Gubei/Simatai for the night version. Badaling only if there's no alternative.
- **The Forbidden City off the central axis.** Thanks to the side courtyards to the east, the visit doesn't turn into a procession.
- **Jingshan right afterward.** Ten minutes of climbing that make everything you've just walked through readable.
- **The Temple of Heaven at seven in the morning**, for the park more than the temple.
- **The Xi'an walls by bike at sunset.**
- **The bronze chariots in Xi'an**, which almost everyone skips.
- **Suzhou on a weekday.**
- **The public ferry on the Huangpu**, which beats any tourist cruise at a twentieth of the price.
- **Lugard Road at the Peak**, instead of the paid platform.
- **Taipa Village in Macau**, more than the Ruins of St. Paul's.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **Beijing:** Dongcheng, between Wangfujing and the hutongs around Houhai lake. Choose based on the subway stop more than the neighborhood: the city is huge and traffic above ground is heavy.
- **Xi'an:** inside the walls, in the quadrant around the Bell Tower. You get around everything on foot and the Muslim Quarter is ten minutes away.
- **Shanghai:** former French Concession, the nicest and greenest area. The Bund and Nanjing Road are more central but chaotic; Pudong makes sense only for transfer convenience.
- **Hong Kong:** Causeway Bay or Wan Chai on the island side, Tsim Sha Tsui in Kowloon. This is where you find the smallest and priciest rooms of the whole trip: it's part of the city, so factor it in.
- **Macau:** usually not needed, you do it as a day trip from Hong Kong. If you stay, you'll find the casino hotels on the Cotai at lower prices than you'd think, because the property makes its money elsewhere.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **Beijing:** lacquered duck at a specialist place, not at a restaurant that just has it on the menu. For the rest: jianbing from the morning carts, steamed jiaozi and baozi, hand-pulled noodles, cumin lamb skewers.
- **Xi'an:** the Muslim Quarter is the culinary reason to stop. Roujiamo, biang biang noodles, cold spicy liangpi, yangrou paomo (the lamb soup in which you crumble the bread by hand before handing it over to the kitchen).
- **Shanghai:** xiaolongbao, done the right way (you bite off a corner, drink the broth, then eat the rest: rushing it means burning yourself), and shengjianbao seared on the griddle. It's also the city with the most developed international scene in China, useful if after two weeks you need a break.
- **Suzhou and the water villages:** river fish and braised pork, which in the towns is the specialty.
- **Hong Kong:** dim sum at lunch with carts between the tables, cha chaan teng for everyday hybrid cooking and roast goose at least once.
- **Macau:** Macanese cuisine, which exists only here. Rua do Cunha in Taipa for tasting as you walk.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Do all the setup at home.** Alipay or WeChat Pay verified and linked to a card, eSIM or VPN installed and tested, 12306 account active, offline maps and translator downloaded. It's the difference between starting to travel on day one or day four.
- **Book the Forbidden City and the Terracotta Army like you book a flight.** Physical ticket offices closed, named entry tied to your passport, daily quotas that sell out. On site there's no plan B.
- **Put the train ticket release in your calendar:** 15 days before, at the opening time of the departure station shown on 12306. If you don't want to think about it, use Trip.com and pay the fee.
- **Turn off the VPN before paying**, or the anti-fraud system blocks the transaction and sometimes the account.
- **Never follow into a venue anyone who stops you on the street.** The tea ceremony and the "art student" exhibition are still the most effective scams in the country.
- **Don't cram the Forbidden City, Temple of Heaven and Summer Palace into the same day.** Beijing is huge and distances on the map are deceiving.
- **Don't visit Suzhou on the weekend.**
- **Remember that Hong Kong and Macau are real borders**, with passport control in both directions. Whoever goes back to mainland China starts a new 30-day period, something to keep in mind if the trip is long.
- **Don't assume Alipay works in Hong Kong:** there you pay with an Octopus Card, international cards or cash.
- **Go prepared for the culture, and take it calmly.** Chinese people can come across as demanding: they elbow their way through, they have a different idea of personal space, sometimes they take photos of you without asking. It's simply a culture different from ours. I adapted and took it calmly: if you go prepared you enjoy it, otherwise you risk spoiling your experience of a nation and a people that are still fairly authentic.
- **At the stalls, always haggle, and hard.** I've managed to get prices down by as much as 70% from the first offer. Alternatively, look up the same items on Taobao and have them shipped to your hotel, or at least use it to get an idea of prices before haggling.

## Chiusura personale
What stays with you from this itinerary isn't the list of monuments, which you already knew before leaving. It's the feeling of having crossed a country that runs on its own rules and that, once you accept those rules, becomes one of the easiest places to travel: trains punctual to the minute, cities safe at any hour, prices that don't scare you. The learning curve is all in the first two days, and it's almost entirely in the phone. Once past that, what's left is an empty wall on a ridge, an army buried for two thousand years, a garden in Suzhou with a fan-shaped window, and a half-euro ferry crossing Hong Kong harbor at blue hour.
