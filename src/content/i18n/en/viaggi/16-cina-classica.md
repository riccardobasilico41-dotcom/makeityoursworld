# Classic China in 15 Days: Beijing, Xi'an, Shanghai, Hong Kong and Macau

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 in progress
**Periodo del viaggio:** spring or fall — the right window is April-May or September-October, avoiding the first week of October
**Durata:** 15 days, 14 nights
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independent, with high-speed trains booked from Italy
**Categoria:** Asia · Cina · grandi classici · alta velocità · megalopoli · storia

---

## Apertura personale
Classic China is the trip everyone pictures when they think of China: the Great Wall, the Terracotta Army, the Bund. It's also the trip that, more than any other, gets decided before you leave, sitting at a computer, because here almost nothing that works elsewhere works: not credit cards, not maps, not tickets bought on the spot. If you show up with four things sorted (trains, payments, connection, museum reservations), you find a country that is surprisingly easy, safe and cheap. If you put them off, you'll sort them out anyway, but you'll lose your first three days. This itinerary is built on the single east-west axis that high-speed rail makes simple, and it ends in two places that are China only on paper: Hong Kong and Macau.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** April-May or September-October. In any case avoid Golden Week (October 1-7) and Chinese New Year: half the country is on the move, trains are sold out weeks ahead and the booking sites become unusable. The first days of May are also holidays and crowded.
- **Documents/visa:** passport valid for at least 6 months. Italian citizens can enter visa-free for up to 30 consecutive days, under an exemption extended through December 31, 2026, with multiple entries allowed. Hong Kong and Macau have their own border: you leave and re-enter, and each re-entry into mainland China starts a new 30-day period. Every foreigner must be registered with the police within 24 hours of arrival. Hotels do it at check-in; if you're staying in a private apartment you have to go in person.
- **Currency:** renminbi/yuan (CNY). Hong Kong uses the Hong Kong dollar, Macau the pataca (but Hong Kong dollars are accepted at par).
- **Payments, critical point number one:** mainland China lives almost entirely without cash. **Alipay and WeChat Pay must be installed, verified and linked to a Visa or Mastercard before you leave**, with a stable connection and your passport at hand: doing it once you've landed is much harder. Physical cards only work in high-end hotels. By law cash can't be refused, but many small vendors don't have change: keep it as a backup, not as your main method. ATMs that accept foreign cards exist (the most reliable are Bank of China and ICBC), but not all of them do. **Turn the VPN off when paying**, otherwise the anti-fraud system blocks the transaction.
- **Connectivity, critical point number two:** the Great Firewall blocks Google in every form, WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook, YouTube and most news sites. The simplest solution is a **foreign roaming eSIM**, which routes your traffic outside China and gets around the problem with nothing else needed. A local SIM costs less but goes through the firewall and needs a VPN, which you must install and test **before** leaving, because app stores in China don't offer one. Download these anyway beforehand: offline maps, a camera translator and, to get around like the locals, Amap.
- **Trains, critical point number three:** tickets go on sale **15 days before departure**, at a time that changes from station to station (the 12306 app tells you), and on the busiest routes they're gone within hours. The official 12306 app has an English version that accepts registration with a passport and payment with a foreign card, but account verification takes time and needs to be done well in advance. Alternatively, Trip.com takes bookings up to two months ahead and converts them as soon as sales open, for a fee. The ticket is electronic and tied to your passport, which becomes your travel document: you touch it to the turnstile reader and walk through. At the station you need 30-40 minutes of margin, and the gates close a few minutes before departure, no exceptions.
- **Mandatory reservations:** the Forbidden City and the Terracotta Army have named entry tied to your passport and **physical ticket offices closed**: without an online reservation you don't get in. The Forbidden City sells out days ahead; the Terracotta Army opens sales only 7 days before. The Shaanxi History Museum is free, but the daily quotas run out in minutes.
- **Approximate budget:** up to Shanghai, much lower than you'd expect: trains 40-90€ per leg, good hotels 40-70€, meals 4-10€. Hong Kong flips the math: it's the most expensive city of the trip, especially for lodging.
- **Language:** outside high-end hotels, English almost doesn't exist, much less than in Japan or Thailand. The camera translator is the tool that makes you independent, and the hotel address saved in Chinese characters solves the taxi problem.
- **Scams:** the tea ceremony and the "art student" are the two constants in Beijing, Xi'an and on Shanghai's Nanjing Road. They've worked for twenty years and still work. There's only one rule: don't follow into a venue anyone who stopped you on the street.
- **Electricity:** 220V, type A and I outlets, often combined. The small Italian two-pin plug fits many outlets but not all, and the Schuko and three-pin ones don't: bring a universal adapter.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Arrival in Beijing
You land at Capital (PEK) or Daxing (PKX): check which one before booking your transfer, because Daxing is much farther out. Both are connected to the center by subway or express trains.

We kept the first day light on purpose, because jet lag hits: a walk through the hutongs around the Drum Tower and Houhai lake and a first dinner at a neighborhood restaurant, to test the camera translator on the menu. It's also the moment to check in a shop that Alipay or WeChat Pay work, before finding out in an awkward situation.

### Day 2 — Forbidden City, Tiananmen and Jingshan
We entered the **Forbidden City** at opening, with the reservation made weeks earlier and passport in hand: without it, you don't get in. The route is one-way from south to north. It's best to leave the central axis early, where ninety percent of visitors walk, and slip into the side courtyards to the east, where the halls are small and almost empty.

Coming out of the north gate, we crossed the street and climbed the steps of **Jingshan Hill**: ten minutes up and you have the whole complex below you, with the golden roofs lined up all the way to Tiananmen. Hardly anyone pairs this stop with the visit, yet without this view the layout of the palace stays incomprehensible.

The afternoon at **Tiananmen Square** (security checks at the entrance, passport required) and, in the evening, lacquered duck at a place that does it for a living.

### Day 3 — The Great Wall at Jinshanling
A full day: early morning departure and about 2h30 of transfer. **Jinshanling** is the only partly restored stretch that is easiest to reach from Beijing, about ten kilometers of wall along a ridge, with half-collapsed watchtowers and grass between the stones.

You walk 3-4 hours on uneven steps, with not a meter of shade and no refreshment points: water, a hat and shoes with a good sole are mandatory. In return, after half an hour of walking you turn around and there's nobody.

*Alternatives if a full day isn't possible: **Mutianyu**, restored but beautiful, with a chairlift up and a toboggan down, half a day; **Gubei Water Town and Simatai**, the only stretch you can visit at night, but it requires an overnight stay outside the city. **Badaling** only as a last resort: it's the most convenient and the most crowded in the country.*

### Day 4 — Temple of Heaven, hutongs and Summer Palace
Wake up early and be at the **Temple of Heaven** at opening, but for the park more than the temple: every morning, hundreds of retirees dance, sing in choirs, play cards and do sword exercises there. It's the most beautiful and least touristy scene in the city, and by ten it's already over.

In the afternoon we went to the **Summer Palace**, on Kunming Lake, less monumental than the Forbidden City and much more pleasant to walk. Then back to the center and a last evening in the hutongs. A warning: the ones around Houhai are now bars and shops, real life is in the alleys two blocks farther on.

### Day 5 — Train to Xi'an and the walls by bike
**High-speed train Beijing-Xi'an, about 4h30.** At the station it's best to have 30-40 minutes of margin: the checks are serious and the stations are as big as airports.

Arriving at Xi'an North, we took the subway to the center and settled in inside the walls. In the late afternoon, the **ride around the Ming walls by bike**: fourteen kilometers of wall, wide enough to pedal on, with the old city on one side and the skyscrapers on the other. The right moment is the last hour of light, when the red lanterns on the gate towers come on.

Dinner in stages in the **Muslim Quarter**: cumin lamb skewers, roujiamo, biang biang noodles. You eat a little at each stall and keep moving, without sitting down.

### Day 6 — Terracotta Army
We spent the morning at the **museum of Qin Shi Huang's mausoleum**, 40 minutes from the center. The reservation is named and tied to your passport, and sales open only 7 days before: put it in your calendar like an appointment.

**Public bus 5 (306)** leaves from the train station, cheap and direct. In the station square, meanwhile, there are "tourist buses" that take you to minor mausoleums and pass them off as the main site: it's the most common rip-off in the city.

On site, some people recommend visiting the pits in reverse order (three, two, one), because after number one the others look like construction sites. Don't skip the **bronze chariots museum** out of tiredness: the two chariots are the most technically impressive pieces of the whole site.

The afternoon is free for the **Giant Wild Goose Pagoda** or the **Shaanxi History Museum** (free, but with daily quotas that run out in minutes).

### Day 7 — Train to Shanghai and the Bund
**High-speed train Xi'an-Shanghai, about 6 hours.** It's the longest leg of the trip and it's best to take it in the morning, to have the afternoon free.

We settled in the former French Concession, the nicest area to sleep in, and our first outing was to the **Bund at blue hour**, when the lights of Pudong come on and the sky isn't yet black. The trick that's worth more than a tourist cruise is the **public ferry** that crosses the river for a few yuan: from the deck you see the colonial buildings on one side and the skyscrapers on the other together.

### Day 8 — Shanghai: French Concession, Yuyuan, Pudong
The morning on foot in the **former French Concession**, among plane trees and low brick houses: it's the Shanghai that matters, and you only discover it by walking.

The afternoon at **Yu Garden** and the old city bazaar (very crowded, better early or late) and then up to a viewpoint in **Pudong**: the Shanghai Tower or a rooftop bar, which costs as much as a cocktail and has the head-on view.

The evening in the bars of the French Concession. Beware of Nanjing Road: it's where the tea scam is most widespread in all of China.

### Day 9 — Suzhou and the classical gardens
A day in **Suzhou**, 25 minutes by high-speed train from Shanghai. It should be done **on a weekday**: the gardens are small and on weekends the crowd erases exactly what they exist for.

Two or three gardens are more than enough. The **Humble Administrator's Garden** is the most famous and requires an online reservation with passport; the **Master of the Nets Garden** is the smallest, the least visited and probably the most beautiful. They are compositions built on a precise rule, that no opening frames the same scene twice, and you understand it right away walking through them.

### Day 10 — A water village
A day in one of the towns built on canals around Shanghai. **Zhujiajiao** is reached by subway in an hour and is the most convenient, but also the most commercial. **Tongli** pairs well with Suzhou and has a UNESCO garden. **Wuzhen** is the most polished and the most photogenic, especially the west section lit at night, but it's a museum-town from which the residents have been relocated. **Nanxun** is the least visited and the only one still partly inhabited.

*The choice changes the day more than it seems: if you just want to see a canal, take Zhujiajiao and don't think about it again; if you have a night to spend, sleep in Wuzhen and see the west section after sunset.*

### Day 11 — To Hong Kong
**High-speed train Shanghai-Hong Kong West Kowloon (about 8 hours)** or a flight from Shanghai (2h30). On paper the train is fascinating; in practice, over this distance, the flight saves you half a day.

Either way you cross a **real border**, with passport control leaving mainland China and entering Hong Kong. Italians enter visa-free for 90 days.

We spent the first evening on the waterfront of **Tsim Sha Tsui**, with the island skyline across the water, and had our first taste of cha chaan teng, the local diners where you eat hybrid Anglo-Cantonese food for a few dollars.

### Day 12 — Hong Kong: Peak, Star Ferry, markets
We went up **Victoria Peak**, which you should do only with a clear sky, because up there the view is what counts. The historic tram has a line that exceeds an hour in the late afternoon; bus 15, which climbs from the same area, leaves right away and costs a fraction. At the top, skip the paid platform and take **Lugard Road**, the flat trail that circles the summit: same view, free and with nobody around.

The afternoon between **Sheung Wan** and the longest outdoor escalators in the world, the evening in the markets of **Mong Kok**, from the flower market to Temple Street.

At some point in the day, then, the harbor crossing on the **Star Ferry**: ten minutes, less than half a euro, and the best view of the city.

### Day 13 — Macau in a day
To get there you can take the **fast ferry from Hong Kong (about an hour)** or the bus across the Hong Kong-Zhuhai-Macau Bridge, the longest sea bridge in the world. Another passport check in both directions.

The morning in the **UNESCO historic center**: the Ruins of St. Paul's, Largo do Senado with its wavy Portuguese paving, the Fortaleza do Monte, the A-Ma temple. You can do it all on foot in half a day, and it's smaller than you'd expect.

**Macanese** lunch, the oldest fusion cuisine in the world (galinha à africana, minchi, pastéis de nata), best in **Taipa Village**, along Rua do Cunha.

The afternoon on the **Cotai** strip, with the casinos' free shuttle buses, which everyone uses even without gambling: in twenty minutes you go from a Portuguese town to a life-size replica of the Grand Canal. The point of the day lies in this jump.

In the evening we went back to Hong Kong.

### Day 14 — Lantau and the Hong Kong you don't expect
The morning on **Lantau** island: the Ngong Ping panoramic cable car, the Big Buddha and the stilt fishing village of **Tai O**.

Alternatively, for those who prefer walking, there's the **Dragon's Back**: a scenic trail above the south coast, half an hour by subway and bus from the financial district, with the sea below and not a building in sight. It's the half of Hong Kong nobody puts on the itinerary.

Last dinner with Cantonese **roast goose**, among the best things eaten on the whole trip.

### Day 15 — Departure
Airport Express from Hong Kong Central to the airport in 24 minutes. If your flight is in the afternoon, you have time for one last dim sum and one last Star Ferry crossing.

## What to see / what to do
- **The right stretch of Wall.** Jinshanling with a full day and legs in shape, Mutianyu with half a day, Gubei/Simatai for the night version. Badaling only if there's no alternative.
- **The Forbidden City off the central axis.** Thanks to the side courtyards to the east, the visit doesn't turn into a procession.
- **Jingshan right afterward.** Ten minutes of climbing that make everything you've just walked through readable.
- **The Temple of Heaven at seven in the morning**, for the park more than the temple.
- **The Xi'an walls by bike at sunset.**
- **The bronze chariots in Xi'an**, which almost everyone skips.
- **Suzhou on a weekday.**
- **The public ferry on the Huangpu**, which beats any tourist cruise at a twentieth of the price.
- **Lugard Road at the Peak**, instead of the paid platform.
- **Taipa Village in Macau**, more than the Ruins of St. Paul's.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **Beijing:** Dongcheng, between Wangfujing and the hutongs around Houhai lake. Choose based on the subway stop more than the neighborhood: the city is huge and traffic above ground is heavy.
- **Xi'an:** inside the walls, in the quadrant around the Bell Tower. You get around everything on foot and the Muslim Quarter is ten minutes away.
- **Shanghai:** former French Concession, the nicest and greenest area. The Bund and Nanjing Road are more central but chaotic; Pudong makes sense only for transfer convenience.
- **Hong Kong:** Causeway Bay or Wan Chai on the island side, Tsim Sha Tsui in Kowloon. This is where you find the smallest and priciest rooms of the whole trip: it's part of the city, so factor it in.
- **Macau:** usually not needed, you do it as a day trip from Hong Kong. If you stay, you'll find the casino hotels on the Cotai at lower prices than you'd think, because the property makes its money elsewhere.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **Beijing:** lacquered duck at a specialist place, not at a restaurant that just has it on the menu. For the rest: jianbing from the morning carts, steamed jiaozi and baozi, hand-pulled noodles, cumin lamb skewers.
- **Xi'an:** the Muslim Quarter is the culinary reason to stop. Roujiamo, biang biang noodles, cold spicy liangpi, yangrou paomo (the lamb soup in which you crumble the bread by hand before handing it over to the kitchen).
- **Shanghai:** xiaolongbao, done the right way (you bite off a corner, drink the broth, then eat the rest: rushing it means burning yourself), and shengjianbao seared on the griddle. It's also the city with the most developed international scene in China, useful if after two weeks you need a break.
- **Suzhou and the water villages:** river fish and braised pork, which in the towns is the specialty.
- **Hong Kong:** dim sum at lunch with carts between the tables, cha chaan teng for everyday hybrid cooking and roast goose at least once.
- **Macau:** Macanese cuisine, which exists only here. Rua do Cunha in Taipa for tasting as you walk.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Do all the setup at home.** Alipay or WeChat Pay verified and linked to a card, eSIM or VPN installed and tested, 12306 account active, offline maps and translator downloaded. It's the difference between starting to travel on day one or day four.
- **Book the Forbidden City and the Terracotta Army like you book a flight.** Physical ticket offices closed, named entry tied to your passport, daily quotas that sell out. On site there's no plan B.
- **Put the train ticket release in your calendar:** 15 days before, at the opening time of the departure station shown on 12306. If you don't want to think about it, use Trip.com and pay the fee.
- **Turn off the VPN before paying**, or the anti-fraud system blocks the transaction and sometimes the account.
- **Never follow into a venue anyone who stops you on the street.** The tea ceremony and the "art student" exhibition are still the most effective scams in the country.
- **Don't cram the Forbidden City, Temple of Heaven and Summer Palace into the same day.** Beijing is huge and distances on the map are deceiving.
- **Don't visit Suzhou on the weekend.**
- **Remember that Hong Kong and Macau are real borders**, with passport control in both directions. Whoever goes back to mainland China starts a new 30-day period, something to keep in mind if the trip is long.
- **Don't assume Alipay works in Hong Kong:** there you pay with an Octopus Card, international cards or cash.
- **Go prepared for the culture, and take it calmly.** Chinese people can come across as demanding: they elbow their way through, they have a different idea of personal space, sometimes they take photos of you without asking. It's simply a culture different from ours. I adapted and took it calmly: if you go prepared you enjoy it, otherwise you risk spoiling your experience of a nation and a people that are still fairly authentic.
- **At the stalls, always haggle, and hard.** I've managed to get prices down by as much as 70% from the first offer. Alternatively, look up the same items on Taobao and have them shipped to your hotel, or at least use it to get an idea of prices before haggling.

## Chiusura personale
What stays with you from this itinerary isn't the list of monuments, which you already knew before leaving. It's the feeling of having crossed a country that runs on its own rules and that, once you accept those rules, becomes one of the easiest places to travel: trains punctual to the minute, cities safe at any hour, prices that don't scare you. The learning curve is all in the first two days, and it's almost entirely in the phone. Once past that, what's left is an empty wall on a ridge, an army buried for two thousand years, a garden in Suzhou with a fan-shaped window, and a half-euro ferry crossing Hong Kong harbor at blue hour.
