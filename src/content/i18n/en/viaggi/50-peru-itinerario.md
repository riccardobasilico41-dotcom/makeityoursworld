# Peru in 31 Days: From the Andes to the Amazon, to the Cordillera Blanca

**Stato bozza:** 🟡 in progress
**Periodo del viaggio:** three trips, in 2018, 2021 and 2024
**Durata:** 31 days, 30 nights
**Compagni di viaggio:** with friends, independently, with domestic flights, tourist and overnight buses, the train to Machu Picchu, organized tours on Titicaca and in the Colca, mandatory guides in the Amazon and trekking agencies in Huaraz
**Categoria:** Sud America · Perù · Ande · trekking · alta quota · Amazzonia · cultura inca

---

## Apertura personale
The moment I carry with me from Peru is Machu Picchu at dawn. Getting in among the first, when the citadel is still almost empty, pays you back for the alarm in the dark and the road to get there. I've been to Peru three times, in 2018, 2021 and 2024, and this itinerary lines up what I saw on those trips into a single thirty-one-day loop. **Part 1**, seventeen days, is Andean Peru: Lima, Cusco, the Sacred Valley, Machu Picchu, Titicaca, Arequipa and the Colca. **Part 2**, fourteen days, adds the Amazon and the Cordillera Blanca, two extensions that both start from Lima.

I've also been to Peru twice as a volunteer: in Cusco with Proyecto Cusimaky, a project I support and invite you to visit, and in Arequipa as a volunteer osteopath.

## Scheda pratica
- **When to go:** this itinerary follows the Andean dry season, **May to September**, which covers both Part 1 (Cusco, Sacred Valley, Titicaca, Arequipa) and the Cordillera Blanca in Part 2. Outside this window the high-altitude trails become muddy or impassable, and in February the Inca Trail closes completely for maintenance. The Amazon has no real dry season: the May-October window (low water) only makes the trails more passable and wildlife easier to spot along the rivers.
- **Documents:** passport valid for at least 6 months. No visa for Italian citizens on tourist stays of up to 183 days.
- **Currency:** nuevo sol (PEN), to be exchanged at the airport, at a bank or at an authorized casa de cambio; US dollars are accepted in many tourist settings. Always keep small bills: change for large notes is a recurring problem, especially outside the cities.
- **Payments:** cards accepted in Lima, Cusco and tourist businesses; much less in markets, on the Titicaca boats, in the Sacred Valley villages and in Huaraz. In Puno, the Amazon and Huaraz, arrive with cash already in your pocket.
- **Connectivity:** Claro, Movistar, Entel and Bitel, with a SIM available at the airport with your passport. Good coverage in cities and along the tourist circuit, absent at the Amazon lodges and on much of the treks (especially toward Choquequirao, but on Santa Cruz and Laguna 69 too the signal is poor or nonexistent).
- **Health, with altitude as the central theme of the trip:** soroche, altitude sickness, is a real risk. Cusco is at 11,200 feet (3,400 meters), Puno at 12,500 (3,812), Huaraz at about 10,000 (3,050): you need at least two days of real acclimatization before any exertion. The itinerary is built precisely to climb in altitude by degrees: Rainbow Mountain, Machu Picchu, Titicaca, Laguna 69 and the Santa Cruz trek all come after days of adjustment, never the day after landing. Yellow fever vaccination is recommended for anyone heading into the Amazon, and required by some lodges.
- **Safety:** the most concrete risk is theft by fake taxi drivers on trips to and from airports: only booked taxis or apps, never the ones who offer themselves at the exit. In Cusco, besides pickpockets in crowded tourist areas, there are street money changers who pass counterfeit or out-of-circulation bills: you change only at a bank or an authorized casa de cambio, never on the street. For long-distance buses (including the overnight to Huaraz), only the main companies: accidents caused by poor vehicles or drivers are a real risk, far more than an annoyance.
- **Domestic transport:** domestic flights (Lima-Cusco, Lima-Arequipa, Lima or Cusco-Puerto Maldonado) save whole days and cost relatively little if booked ahead. The train serves only Machu Picchu and the tourist Cusco-Puno route. For Huaraz there are no convenient flights: you go by road, on the overnight bus from Lima.
- **Electricity:** 220V, 60Hz, type A and C sockets. Italian two-pin plugs almost always fit, but a universal adapter is still useful.
- **Time zone:** UTC-5 all year, the same along the whole itinerary: 7 hours behind Italy during daylight saving time, 6 in winter.
- **Holidays:** Inti Raymi, on June 24 in Cusco, is the most important event of the year: the city is full and prices skyrocket, so book well ahead if your dates coincide.
- **Two-part structure:** this itinerary is written as a single thirty-one-day story, but works just as well split in half: the details are in the note between Day 17 and Day 18.
- **From the airport to the centre:** at **Lima Jorge Chávez (LIM)**, with the new terminal opened in 2025, Miraflores and Barranco are 45-90 minutes away depending on traffic. **Airport Express Lima** bus to Miraflores, about 25-30 soles, ticket at the desk in arrivals or online · **official taxi** from the desks inside the terminal, about 60-90 soles · **Uber, Cabify and inDrive** from the pickup zone shown in the app, usually cheaper · never the drivers who offer rides at the exit: it's the most concrete risk of the trip · at **Cusco (CUZ)** the airport is in town: official taxi or app in 15-20 minutes, about 20-40 soles.

## Itinerario giorno per giorno

### Day 1 — Arrival in Lima
**Part 1 — Classic Peru: Lima, Cusco, Sacred Valley, Machu Picchu, Titicaca, Arequipa and the Colca (17 days).** We landed at Jorge Chávez Airport, the country's only intercontinental hub. *The first mistake to avoid is already here: never an unauthorized taxi at the exit, because it's one of the spots where tourists get robbed most often. Only booked taxis or apps.* Then the transfer to **Miraflores** or **Barranco**, the two most convenient and safest parts of the city. A quiet evening to absorb the jet lag and get ready for a month of travel: Lima is for tomorrow, but a first evening stroll on the Malecón, the oceanfront cliff that lights up at this hour, is worth it.

From the airport to Miraflores the ride takes between forty minutes and an hour and a half, depending on traffic, which in Lima is heavy at almost any hour. At the airport it's worth changing only the minimum for the taxi and the first meals, and buying a SIM with your passport, so your phone already works for ride apps. For dinner, just stay in the neighborhood: in Miraflores and Barranco the restaurants are a few steps from the Malecón and you walk back. *If you land in the evening, don't plan anything else: Lima deserves tomorrow's daylight.*

**In practice:** airport to Miraflores or Barranco about 40-90 minutes depending on traffic · taxi booked through the hotel, from the official airport desk or via app, never the drivers who approach you at the exit · SIM at the airport with your passport · sleep in Miraflores or Barranco.

### Day 2 — Lima: Historic Center, Miraflores and Barranco
We devoted the day to the capital, which too many people visit only in transit, missing the best reason to stay: the food. The morning in the **colonial historic center**, a UNESCO site, between the Plaza Mayor and the convent of San Francisco; at lunch (**at the right time, lunch and not dinner, as is the custom here**) in a neighborhood cevichería, for the first ceviche of the trip, with fish that had arrived that very morning. The afternoon in **Miraflores**, along the Malecón to Huaca Pucllana, the pre-Inca pyramid in the middle of the city, and the close in **Barranco**, the bohemian neighborhood of murals, for an aperitivo at sunset on the Puente de los Suspiros. If you like, add the Larco Museum, the country's most important collection of pre-Columbian art.

Peruvian cooking is one of the things I remember most from these trips, and Lima is where I found it at its best. The city is dangerous, though: you have to get around with care, especially outside Miraflores and Barranco.

**In practice:** Huaca Pucllana closed on Tuesdays, entry about 15 soles with a guided visit · San Francisco convent visited with a guide, catacombs included · cevicherías work mostly at lunch and many close in the afternoon · between the center and Miraflores, a ride-app taxi or the Metropolitano bus, about 30-45 minutes.

### Day 3 — Flight to Cusco and First Day of Acclimatization
Domestic flight to **Cusco**, a little over an hour, landing directly at 11,200 feet (3,400 meters). *Factor this in right away: the first day here is a lost day, and it's best to treat it as such.* No demanding plans: we just walked slowly between the Plaza de Armas and the **San Blas** neighborhood, the steepest in the city, with the classic coca tea in hand. It's advice to take seriously, not local folklore: acclimatization is the condition for the rest of the trip to work. For sleeping, the center or San Blas: San Blas is more characterful but all uphill, and in the first days at altitude you feel it a lot. At dinner, the first pisco sour in the city, but take it slow: at this altitude alcohol hits twice as hard.

The Lima-Cusco flight is best taken in the morning, when the weather over the Andes is usually more stable. On arrival you go to the hotel in a booked taxi, drink plenty of water and avoid rushing up stairs: headache, shortness of breath and broken sleep are normal on the first night. Anyone with health issues should talk to their doctor before leaving, including about medication for altitude sickness. *Several hotels in Cusco keep oxygen and mate de coca at reception: asking is not an overreaction.*

**In practice:** Lima-Cusco flight about 1 hour 20 minutes, better in the morning · airport to the center about 20 minutes by taxi · a day without effort, water and light meals · sleep in the historic center or San Blas, allowing for the climbs.

### Day 4 — Cusco: Qorikancha, Sacsayhuamán and San Blas
The second day of acclimatization, with a bit more activity. **Qorikancha**, the Inca sun temple on which the conquistadors built the convent of Santo Domingo, and **Sacsayhuamán**, the fortress above the city with megalithic blocks fitted together without mortar: the best way to understand, even before getting there, what you'll see at Machu Picchu. The **Cusco Tourist Ticket (Boleto Turístico)** (about 130 soles) covers these sites, those of the Sacred Valley and several museums in a single ticket: it's best to buy it today. We had lunch at San Pedro Market, among fruit juices at the counter and soups for a few soles, for the first real contact with Andean street food. For souvenirs in Cusco, I go to **Assunta**.

It's best to start with Qorikancha in the morning, right in the center, and go up to Sacsayhuamán in the early afternoon, by taxi or on foot along the stairways toward San Cristóbal, which at this altitude should be taken slowly. From the fortress you see all of Cusco, and from there you walk down toward **San Blas**, the artisans' quarter, for sunset. *Qorikancha is not included in the Boleto Turístico: the ticket is paid separately at the entrance.* If you have half a day free in these days, stop by **Proyecto Cusimaky**, the project I volunteered with in Cusco and that I invite you to visit.

**In practice:** full Boleto Turístico about 130 soles, valid 10 days, bought in Cusco or at the site entrances, and it doesn't cover Qorikancha (about 20 soles) or the Maras salt pans · Sacsayhuamán about half an hour uphill on foot from the Plaza de Armas · San Pedro market best in the morning.

### Day 5 — Rainbow Mountain or Humantay Lake
With two days of acclimatization behind us, the day is devoted to one of the two high-altitude excursions near Cusco: **Rainbow Mountain (Vinicunca)**, the hills striped with colored minerals above 16,400 feet (5,000 meters), or the quieter **Humantay Lake**, a turquoise glacial lake at 13,800 feet (4,200 meters) at the foot of the Salkantay. *Neither should be done earlier, when your body hasn't yet reacted to the altitude: it's the most direct way to ruin your trip.* In both cases the wake-up is very early, with an organized tour from Cusco: the last stretches are done on foot out of breath, but the color (the mineral stripes at Vinicunca, the glacier over the lake) repays the effort. Back in Cusco in the late afternoon. I did both, and added another colorful mountain, I think Palcoyo, and a couple of lakes whose names I don't remember.

**In practice:** tours from Cusco leave between 3:30 and 5 a.m. and return in the afternoon · Vinicunca entry about 25-30 soles in cash, paid to the community; Humantay has a separate ticket, to check with the agency · horses available for much of the climb · warm layers, sunscreen and water: at altitude the weather changes fast.

### Day 6 — On to the Sacred Valley: Pisac and Ollantaytambo
We moved to the **Sacred Valley of the Incas**, which sits 1,600-2,000 feet (500-600 meters) lower than Cusco: from here on you sleep better. The first stop was **Pisac**, for the market and the archaeological site on the hill; then **Ollantaytambo**, the only Inca settlement still inhabited according to its original layout, with the terraced fortress above the town. *Sleeping here instead of going back to Cusco is the strategic choice of this whole stretch: the train to Machu Picchu leaves at dawn, and every extra hour of sleep counts.* The evening was free to walk around the town once the tour buses have left and it goes back to its residents: it's the best time to visit it.

Pisac market is biggest on Sundays, but the stalls are there every day; you go up to the archaeological site by taxi and then walk down through the terraces to the village, in an hour or a little more. In Ollantaytambo it's best to climb to the fortress in the late afternoon, when the light warms the stone and the groups have gone. *Both sites are covered by the Boleto Turístico bought in Cusco: keep it with you at all times.* Frequent, cheap colectivos run between the valley's villages, as an alternative to a car with driver for the whole day.

**In practice:** Cusco-Pisac about 1 hour, Pisac-Ollantaytambo about 1 hour 15 minutes by road · Pisac and Ollantaytambo included in the Boleto Turístico · Pisac market biggest on Sundays · sleep in Ollantaytambo, near the station.

### Day 7 — The Maras Salt Pans, Moray and Chinchero
A day for the rest of the valley, which is often skipped by those rushing to Machu Picchu and which, for that very reason, is among the loveliest to take slowly. The **Maras salt pans**, thousands of white pools carved into the mountainside and used for generations by the same families (separate ticket, about 20 soles for foreigners, in cash); **Moray**, the concentric circular terraces the Incas used as an agricultural laboratory; and **Chinchero**, for a traditional weaving workshop with a women's cooperative. You can do it by bike, quad or with a car and driver: the dirt roads to Maras get complicated in the rainy season, but from May to September it's not a problem. Last night in Ollantaytambo.

The order that works best is Chinchero in the morning, Moray around noon and Maras in the afternoon, then back to Ollantaytambo in under an hour. If you're fit, you can walk down from the salt pans toward the river, as far as the Pichingoto bridge, and get picked up there. *For some years now you can no longer walk among the pools: you stay on the marked path, and shoes with good grip are still useful.* The salt sold at the entrance to the pans is the most authentic souvenir of the valley, and it costs a few soles.

**In practice:** Moray and Chinchero in the Boleto Turístico, Maras a separate ticket in cash · car with driver or tour from Ollantaytambo or Cusco, a good half day · muddy dirt roads in the rainy season · second night in Ollantaytambo.

### Day 8 — By Train to Machu Picchu
Morning train from **Ollantaytambo** to **Aguas Calientes** (PeruRail or IncaRail): departures from here are more numerous and cheaper than from Cusco/Poroy. *This itinerary uses the train because time is fixed and counted. Those with 4-5 extra days can replace this day and the next with the Salkantay trek or the Inca Trail, both described in the comparison on the Machu Picchu page, keeping in mind the warning about permits further below.* I've gotten to Machu Picchu in several ways: on a five-day trek, by minibus to Hidroeléctrica and then on foot, and by train, in economy class. Free afternoon in Aguas Calientes, a pure tourist transit town: better to spend little time there and go to bed early, because tomorrow's wake-up is in the dead of night, to enter the site on the first shift.

**In practice:** Ollantaytambo-Aguas Calientes train about 1 hour 30 minutes, PeruRail or IncaRail, to book weeks ahead in high season · hand luggage is limited on the tourist train: leave the rest at your hotel in Ollantaytambo · Machu Picchu entry about 150 soles for the standard circuits, only on tuboleto.cultura.pe · bus tickets for the climb bought online or the day before in Aguas Calientes.

### Day 9 — Machu Picchu at Opening
Wake up in the dark to be among the first at the **shuttle bus** (25 minutes, about $24 round trip), or walk up in an hour and a half to two. *The advice that counts above all others: you enter on the first shift for the light, but above all for the forty minutes when the site is still quiet, before the groups arrive.* The citadel is visited along the circuit assigned when you booked: you can't move freely. Those who booked months ahead (see below) add the climb to **Huayna Picchu** or **Machu Picchu Mountain**, both with separate and much tighter quotas. I did Huayna Picchu and it's beautiful, but you have to book in time, and if you're afraid of heights you should think hard. In the afternoon, return train to Ollantaytambo and then Cusco, where you sleep tonight.

**In practice:** first bus from Aguas Calientes around 5:30 a.m., with the line starting up to an hour earlier in high season · Huayna Picchu and Montaña about 200 soles, citadel included · no food, large backpacks or poles without rubber tips inside · return train to book for the afternoon, then about 2 hours by road from Ollantaytambo to Cusco.

### Day 10 — From Cusco to Puno on the Altiplano
A day that is almost all transfer, but to be lived slowly: the **tourist bus** from Cusco to Puno (about 10 hours with the stops) stops along the way at Andahuaylillas, the "Sistine Chapel of the Andes," at the Raqchi archaeological complex and at the La Raya pass, the highest point on the route, at over 14,100 feet (4,300 meters). Much better than the direct flight to Juliaca, which skips all this. We arrived in **Puno** in the evening, on Lake Titicaca, at 12,500 feet (3,812 meters): *here altitude sickness is statistically more likely than in Cusco, and this should be treated as a new altitude to respect, as well as a stop on the Andean trip.*

The bus leaves Cusco early in the morning with a guide and a buffet lunch along the way; entry to the sites is usually paid separately, in cash. One of the stops is the small museum of **Pukará**, with stone sculptures from the culture that preceded the Incas on the high plateau. After La Raya you cross the puna, an expanse of yellow grass with llamas and alpacas grazing, and before Puno the city of Juliaca, the most chaotic of the route. *On the first evening in Puno, walk slowly and eat early: it's the right moment to book the lake tour with the night on Amantaní.*

**In practice:** Cusco-Puno tourist bus about 10 hours with stops, about 45-60 dollars with guide and lunch · entry to Andahuaylillas, Raqchi and Pukará usually not included, in cash · the alternative is the tourist train, much more expensive · sleep in Puno, in the center or near the port.

### Day 11 — Titicaca: the Uros and Taquile Islands, Night on Amantaní
We set out by boat from the port of Puno toward the floating islands of the **Uros**, built from totora reeds and still inhabited today. *Let's be honest: it's a tourist staging, half an hour of interesting explanation of how the island is built and little more. The real side of the lake comes later.* Then **Taquile**, a UNESCO heritage site for its weaving, with lunch and a view of the Bolivian side of the lake. In the late afternoon we reached **Amantaní**, where you sleep at the home of a local family: dinner together, a climb to the Pachatata temple for sunset and, if it happens, the village's evening party with traditional clothes lent by your hosts. *At 12,500 feet (3,800 meters), with no heating, the night is freezing: heavy clothes are essential.*

The tour is booked in Puno, even the day before, through an agency or the hotel, and it usually includes boat, guide, most meals and the night with a family; the boat is slow, and between one island and the next it can take a couple of hours. On Taquile you walk up from the pier to the square, a climb you feel at this altitude. Bring the Amantaní family something useful, such as fruit, rice or school supplies. *On the lake the sun burns fast even in the cold: sunscreen and a hat are needed all day.*

**In practice:** two-day Uros, Taquile and Amantaní tour about 70-120 dollars depending on the operator, with homestay and most meals · small entry fees to the islands, in cash · departure from Puno port early in the morning · a light backpack for the night, the rest at the hotel in Puno.

### Day 12 — Amantaní at Dawn and Back to Puno
The morning with the host family, then back on the boat toward Puno, where you arrive midday. *It's the moment of the lake that stays with you most: no comforts, shared bathroom and hot water not guaranteed, but it's the only point of the tour where you step out of the tourist circuit.* Free afternoon: if you like, reach the **funerary towers of Sillustani**, just outside town, pre-Inca and overlooking another lake. Last night in Puno before the transfer to Arequipa.

On Amantaní the day starts early, with a simple breakfast at home and a goodbye at the pier, where the boats leave at a fixed time. *Ask the evening before what time you leave: the groups don't wait.* **Sillustani** is about forty-five minutes by road from Puno, on Lake Umayo: the chullpas, funerary towers of the Colla and later of the Incas, are best visited in the late afternoon, when the stone takes on the color of sunset. Back in Puno, a hot dinner, with lake trout or quinoa soup, and early to bed, because tomorrow you set off again by bus.

**In practice:** return from the islands to Puno around midday · Sillustani about 45 minutes from Puno, half a day by tour or taxi, entry about 15 soles · bus to Arequipa to book today with a major company · second night in Puno.

### Day 13 — From Puno to Arequipa
About 6 hours by bus to **Arequipa**, at 7,500 feet (2,300 meters): a real relief after days spent above 11,200 feet (3,400 meters). The body feels it, and you sleep better from the very first night. We arrived in the afternoon, with time for a first walk through the historic center built of **sillar**, the light volcanic stone that gives the city its nickname, the "white city," with the Misti volcano in the background of every view.

The bus from Puno usually leaves in the morning and crosses the high plateau again, along the **Salinas y Aguada Blanca** reserve, with vicuñas and high-altitude lagoons a few meters from the road, before the long descent to the city. *It's worth traveling by day: the landscape is half the reason to do this stretch by road.* Arequipa's center is all walkable, and on the first evening the lit-up **Plaza de Armas** and a quiet dinner are enough. I also came back to Arequipa as a volunteer osteopath, one of the two times I volunteered in Peru.

**In practice:** Puno-Arequipa about 5-6 hours by bus, only major companies, morning departures · from Arequipa's bus terminal to the center about 15-20 minutes by taxi · sleep in the historic center, near the Plaza de Armas · altitude drops to about 7,500 feet (2,300 meters).

### Day 14 — Arequipa, the White City
A day for the city. The **Santa Catalina Monastery**, a city within a city with alleys and courtyards painted blue and orange, bigger and more beautiful than you expect: it's the place that stays with you most in Arequipa. The **Plaza de Armas**, with the cathedral in white sillar, and the **Museo Santuarios Andinos** with the Juanita mummy, the "Ice Maiden" found on the Ampato volcano. For dinner, **Arequipeña** cuisine, different from the rest of Peru: rocoto relleno and chupe de camarones in the traditional picanterías.

It's best to enter **Santa Catalina** at opening time, when the courtyards are empty and the light on the colored walls is softer, and to allow at least a couple of hours; the guide, paid separately, tells the story of the cloistered nuns who lived there for centuries. The Museo Santuarios Andinos can only be visited with the museum's guide. *In the first months of the year Juanita is usually taken off display for conservation and replaced by another find: if it matters to you, check beforehand.* At sunset, the view of Misti from the **Yanahuara viewpoint**, a quarter of an hour by taxi from the center.

**In practice:** Santa Catalina about 40 soles, guide extra, open every day from 9 a.m., with evening closing on some days · Museo Santuarios Andinos by compulsory guided tour, about 20-30 soles plus a tip, shorter hours on Sundays · Colca tour to book today with an agency in the center.

### Day 15 — On to the Colca Canyon
We moved toward the **Colca Canyon**, one of the deepest in the world, with a stop at the **La Calera** hot springs in Chivay to loosen the legs after the journey. *Squeezing the Colca and Arequipa into a single round-trip day from the city, as the cheapest tours do, means leaving at 3 a.m. and spending almost all the time on the bus: the right choice is to sleep in the canyon.* You stay overnight in Chivay or Cabanaconde, depending on the tour chosen, with the colonial villages and pre-Inca terraces of the valley already visible along the road.

The road from Arequipa crosses the Salinas y Aguada Blanca reserve, with vicuñas close to the asphalt, and climbs to the **Patapampa** pass, over 15,700 feet (4,800 meters), the highest point of the trip so far, with the Mirador de los Volcanes and the stone cairns left by travelers. *After the days in Arequipa the altitude makes itself felt again: water, coca tea or candies, and no exertion at the stops.* The Chivay hot springs are best enjoyed in the late afternoon, when the air cools, and in the evening the canyon villages are quiet and dark.

**In practice:** Arequipa-Chivay about 3-4 hours by road, on a two-day tour or a regular bus · Colca Boleto Turístico about 70 soles for foreigners, checked along the road · La Calera hot springs about 15 soles · sleep in Chivay, or Cabanaconde if you're doing the trek.

### Day 16 — The Cruz del Cóndor and Back to Arequipa
Early wake-up for the **Cruz del Cóndor**, the viewpoint where Andean condors take flight using the morning thermals. *Know this beforehand: it's not guaranteed, and the viewpoint is packed with people. But when it happens, seeing them glide a few meters away without a wingbeat remains the most impressive thing about the Colca.* Those with more time add the two-day trek to the Sangalle oasis, with a climb of about 3,600 feet (1,100 meters) at dawn; in this itinerary, instead, you head back to Arequipa in the afternoon, for the last night.

The condors are usually seen in the early morning hours, when the sun warms the canyon walls and the thermals form: that's why tours from Chivay leave at dawn. It's worth moving a little away from the main viewing platform along the rim path, where there are fewer people and your eye reaches the bottom of the canyon. On the way back tours usually stop in the villages of **Maca** and **Yanque**, with colonial churches and terraces along the valley. *In the morning it's cold on the canyon rim: you need warm layers even if by noon you're in a T-shirt.*

**In practice:** Cruz del Cóndor about 1 hour by road from Chivay, condors most active early in the morning · the trek to the Sangalle oasis from Cabanaconde only if you're fit and carry plenty of water · Chivay-Arequipa about 3-4 hours · last night in Arequipa, in the center.

### Day 17 — From Arequipa to Lima, End of Part 1
Morning flight Arequipa-Lima. **Those with two and a half weeks stop here, and it's a complete trip:** seventeen days from Lima to Cusco, from Machu Picchu to Titicaca to the Colca, without the feeling of having skipped anything. Those continuing leave the Andes for two extensions very different from each other, the Amazon and the Cordillera Blanca, which **don't touch geographically and work well even taken one at a time**, both reachable from here. Free afternoon in Lima, for a last stroll in Barranco or to catch up on something left over from the first day.

The flight to Lima is short, and morning flights leave more margin in case of delays; from the airport you go back to Miraflores or Barranco, the same neighborhoods as the first day. *If you're continuing with Part 2, use the afternoon for practical things: laundry, cash for the Amazon and for Huaraz, where cards are of little use, and confirming flights and lodges.* If the flight lands early there's still time for lunch at a cevichería, at the right hour. The evening is a chance for dinner at one of the restaurants that made Lima's cooking famous, to book well in advance.

**In practice:** Arequipa-Lima flight about 1 hour 30 minutes · airport to Miraflores about 40-90 minutes depending on traffic · cash for Part 2 to withdraw in Lima · the best-known restaurants need booking weeks ahead.

### Day 18 — Into the Amazon: Flight to Puerto Maldonado
**Part 2 — Amazon and Cordillera Blanca (14 days): two extensions via Lima that don't meet each other, but that together add rainforest and tropical high mountains to the Andean trip.** Direct flight Lima-**Puerto Maldonado**, about an hour and three quarters, and then by boat along the river to the lodge in the **Tambopata** reserve. *Of the three doors into the Amazon (Puerto Maldonado, Iquitos, Manu Park) this itinerary chooses the first: it's reached with a short hop without upending the rest of the loop, while the other two want a dedicated week and different logistics, via Lima or overland.* We settled into the lodge and did the first evening excursion in the forest, when wildlife is most active.

The Tambopata lodges organize everything from Puerto Maldonado: the airport transfer, a stop at the office to leave your big luggage, and then the boat along the **Madre de Dios** or the Tambopata, from half an hour to a few hours depending on how far the lodge is. *Bring only a small backpack: space is tight in the boat and in the rooms, and the humidity gets everywhere.* The first outing after dark, with the guide and a headlamp, is for spotting frogs, spiders and insects that go unnoticed by day.

**In practice:** direct Lima-Puerto Maldonado flight about 1 hour 45 minutes · package lodge with boat, meals and excursions, to book in advance · Tambopata National Reserve entry about 30 soles, separate or included depending on the package · repellent, long sleeves and a headlamp.

### Day 19 — Tambopata: Canopy Walkway and Lake Sandoval
A full day in the forest: the **canopy walkway**, the bridges suspended among the treetops, and the boat trip to **Lake Sandoval**, an oxbow lake where giant otters live, among the finest sightings in the whole Peruvian Amazon. *The variable that counts more than the luxury of the lodge is the distance from the city: the closest ones are comfortable but you see very little, those a few more hours by boat change the experience. It's worth choosing carefully when you book.* Along the way caimans, monkeys and hundreds of bird species; repellent, a headlamp and waterproof bags for electronics are needed all day.

The best outings are at dawn and dusk, when the animals move and the forest is cooler; in the middle of the day you rest at the lodge. Lake Sandoval is reached by a walk on a boardwalk and trail, then you continue by canoe, in silence, along the banks where giant otters live in family groups. *The otters must not be chased with the canoe: the guide keeps a distance, and rightly so.* In the muddy stretches you need rubber boots, which the lodge usually provides; for spotting in the canopy, binoculars are worth more than a big zoom lens.

**In practice:** Lake Sandoval about half an hour by boat from Puerto Maldonado, then about 45 minutes on foot and the canoe · outings at dawn and dusk, a break in the hot hours · rubber boots usually provided by the lodge · dry bags for electronics and documents.

### Day 20 — Dawn at the Collpa and the Flight Back to Lima
At dawn we headed out to a **collpa**, a clay wall where hundreds of parrots and macaws gather to supplement their minerals. *It's the moment that turns the whole visit around: in the dense forest of the previous days you hear much more than you see, here instead for twenty minutes you can't look at anything but the moving wall of color.* Then back to the lodge, a boat to Puerto Maldonado and an afternoon flight to **Lima**.

To reach the collpa you leave in the dark, by boat or on foot depending on the lodge, and settle in silence on a platform or in a hide facing the clay wall. The birds arrive in waves in the early morning hours, and sometimes don't come down at all if there's a predator around or if it's raining. *When booking, ask which collpa you'll visit and how far it is: some require an extra night.* Back at the lodge, breakfast and packing, then the boat to the port and the transfer to Puerto Maldonado's small airport.

**In practice:** wake up before dawn, binoculars and a light jacket · boat back to Puerto Maldonado by late morning · flight to Lima about 1 hour 45 minutes · sleep in Miraflores or Barranco.

### Day 21 — Lima and the Night Bus to Huaraz
A free day in Lima, as a buffer between the two extensions: useful even just for washing clothes and recovering from the humid heat of the Amazon, which you never fully get used to. In the late afternoon, the **night bus to Huaraz** (about 8 hours): for the Cordillera Blanca there are no convenient regular flights, you go by road, and at night you save a whole day. *Only the main bus companies, never the cheapest ones without a recognizable name: it's the same rule that applies to every other overland stretch of this trip.*

The morning is for resting and washing clothes, which need it after the forest, and at lunch there's time for one last ceviche in Miraflores or Barranco. If you still have energy, go back to the center or to the Larco Museum, if you skipped it on the second day. *On night buses it's worth paying a little more for seats that recline almost fully, and keeping documents and money on you, never in the hold.* In Lima each company leaves from its own terminal: check the address on the ticket and get there by booked taxi, at least half an hour early.

**In practice:** Lima-Huaraz bus about 8 hours, evening departures around 10 p.m. and arrival at dawn · tickets about 80-150 soles in the better classes, online or at the terminal · Cruz del Sur and Movil Tours among the major companies · keep a sweater handy: the air conditioning is strong.

### Day 22 — Arrival in Huaraz and First Acclimatization Day
We arrived in **Huaraz** in the morning, at about 10,000 feet (3,050 meters): after the Amazon at sea level, the altitude has to be picked up from scratch, as if arriving for the first time. A light day in town, which is the staging base for the whole Cordillera Blanca, with trekking agencies on every corner, and has little else going for it. A walk to the central market for caldo de gallina and the high-altitude herbal infusions that help in the first days, and time to choose the right agency for the Santa Cruz trek in the coming days: check guides and equipment, not just the price.

The bus usually arrives at dawn: choose accommodation that allows early check-in, or at least lets you drop your backpack and have breakfast, and sleep a few hours. *Huaraz has a huge number of agencies with very different prices: compare two or three, ask whether they're authorized by SERNANP and look at the tents and sleeping bags before paying.* In the afternoon, if you're handling the altitude well, you can walk up to the **Rataquenua viewpoint** above the city; otherwise the Plaza de Armas and an early hot dinner are enough.

**In practice:** arrival from Lima at dawn, ask for early check-in in advance · Huaraz at about 10,000 feet (3,050 meters), a light day · withdraw cash in town, cards are useless on the trails · agencies for Laguna 69 and Santa Cruz to choose among the authorized ones.

### Day 23 — Acclimatization: Laguna Churup
The second acclimatization day, with a more demanding excursion than the first but still within reach: **Laguna Churup**, a glacial lake at about 14,600 feet (4,450 meters), reachable in a day from Huaraz with a good hour of steep walking on the last stretch. It's the natural test before Laguna 69: if you handle this altitude well, the rest of the Cordillera Blanca will be within reach. Back in Huaraz in the afternoon, with time to organize your backpack and equipment for the Santa Cruz trek, which starts in two days.

You leave Huaraz early in the morning by colectivo or taxi to **Pitec**, the village where the trail starts, about forty-five minutes by road. The climb takes two to three hours, and the last stretch before the lake is a rock face fitted with cables, where you also use your hands; there's an easier, slightly longer alternative. *In rain or ice the rock gets slippery: in that case take the alternative, or stop.* At the lake, with Nevado Churup reflected in the water, you eat and head back by early afternoon, when mountain weather tends to turn.

**In practice:** Huaraz-Pitec about 45 minutes by colectivo or taxi, colectivos back in the afternoon · Huascarán National Park entry about 30 soles for one day · climb about 2-3 hours, descent a little less · water, packed lunch and warm layers.

### Day 24 — The Archaeological Site of Chavín de Huántar
A day on wheels instead of on foot: the trip to the **Chavín de Huántar** site, a UNESCO site, a pre-Inca ceremonial center over three thousand years old with underground galleries and the famous carved stele of the Lanzón. In effect it's also a third day of passive acclimatization before tomorrow's effort at Laguna 69, and a welcome change of pace after the uphill trails of the previous two days. Back in Huaraz in the evening.

The tour leaves Huaraz in the morning and crosses the Cordillera Blanca eastward, past **Querococha lake** and the Kahuish tunnel, before descending into the valley where the site lies; it's about three hours by road each way, with constant bends. Inside you go down into the underground **galleries**, narrow and dark, to the Lanzón, still in its original place. *If you get carsick, take something before leaving: the road is long and winding.* The **Museo Nacional de Chavín**, a couple of kilometers from the temple, keeps the stone heads that once decorated its walls.

**In practice:** day tour from Huaraz, leaving in the morning and back in the evening · site closed on Mondays, open from 9 a.m. until the afternoon, entry about 15 soles · the museum has separate tickets and hours · if this day falls on a Monday, swap it with another.

### Day 25 — Laguna 69
The classic trek of the area: early wake-up, a transfer with an authorized agency to the starting point in Huascarán National Park, then the climb to **Laguna 69**, at 15,100 feet (4,600 meters), a nearly unreal turquoise at the foot of the glaciers. *The last forty minutes above 14,800 feet (4,500 meters) are a fight for breath, twenty steps and a rest, but after three days of acclimatization between Huaraz, Churup and Chavín the body now responds.* It's one of the cheapest and most spectacular excursions in all of Peru, at a fraction of the price and the crowds of the Cusco circuit. Back in Huaraz in the afternoon, for the last night before the Santa Cruz trek.

The trail starts at **Cebollapampa**, in the Llanganuco valley, about three hours by road from Huaraz, after a stop at the Chinancocha and Orconcocha lakes. The climb usually takes between two and a half and three and a half hours, the descent a couple; tours set a fixed time to be back at the minibus, to respect. *Since February 2026, Laguna 69 can only be visited with an agency authorized by SERNANP: hiking it independently is no longer possible.* Swimming in the lake is forbidden, and anyone who ignores the rule risks a fine.

**In practice:** tour from Huaraz leaving around 5 a.m. and back in the evening · Huascarán National Park entry about 30 soles, handled by the agency · climb about 3 hours, descent about 2 · packed lunch, water, sunscreen and warm layers.

### Day 26 — Santa Cruz Trek, Day 1
Transfer to the starting point and first day of the **Santa Cruz trek**: four days among some of the 20,000-foot (6,000-meter) peaks of the Cordillera Blanca. *I did it independently, with no guide or agency, along a well-marked route. Today, though, an operator should be required: SERNANP, the Peruvian parks agency, asks that you enter Huascarán National Park with an authorized operator. It's therefore best to arrange it with a Huaraz agency (guide, mules for the luggage, cook) and check the rules before leaving.* The first day of walking is relatively gentle, along the valley, among rock walls and the first glimpses of the glaciers, up to the first camp.

With the pass on the second day, the trek starts at **Vaquería**, on the eastern side of the Cordillera, and ends at Cashapampa: it's the direction with less uphill. To get there you drive up the Llanganuco valley and over the Portachuelo pass, several hours of road from Huaraz that take up most of the morning. The first camp is usually at Paria, in the Huaripampa valley. *The big luggage goes on the mules: on your back stays only the day pack, with water, jacket and lunch.* Nights in the tent above 12,100 feet (3,700 meters) are cold, and the agency's sleeping bag should be checked before setting off.

**In practice:** 4-day trek with an authorized agency, guide, cook and mules, to book in Huaraz or before · Huascarán National Park entry with the multi-day ticket, check whether it's included · departure from Huaraz at dawn and several hours of road to Vaquería · no phone signal on almost the whole route.

### Day 27 — Santa Cruz Trek, Day 2: the Punta Unión Pass
The hardest day of the trek: the climb to **Punta Unión**, the pass at almost 15,600 feet (4,750 meters) that is the highest point of the entire route, with views of the snow-capped peaks of the Cordillera on both sides. *The comparison with Laguna 69 two days earlier doesn't hold: there you climb for an hour, here the pass is earned with hours of steady uphill walking, and the air at this altitude makes itself felt at every step.* Then the descent on the other side to the next camp, with the landscape changing before your eyes from one slope to the other.

You leave camp early to reach the pass by late morning, before the wind and clouds rise; the climb usually takes three to four hours, at a slow, steady pace. From the pass, with a clear sky, you see **Taulliraju** and, toward the Santa Cruz valley, Artesonraju, with the valley you've just climbed behind you. *Above 14,800 feet (4,500 meters), a strong headache, nausea or confusion are signs not to ignore: you go down, you don't push on.* The descent toward Taullipampa is steep and rocky, and trekking poles are a big help for the knees.

**In practice:** Punta Unión at about 15,600 feet (4,750 meters), climb of about 3-4 hours from camp · start at dawn to avoid the afternoon wind · evening camp at Taullipampa or lower in the valley · poles, gloves and a hat even in sunshine.

### Day 28 — Santa Cruz Trek, Day 3
The third day is more relaxed: the trail descends gently along the Santa Cruz valley, among turquoise glacial lakes and pastures where the mules of the local communities still graze. Less elevation change than the pass day, but after two demanding days the legs feel it all the same. The last camp of the trek, with the Cordillera now behind you and the plain getting closer.

From Taullipampa the trail follows the valley floor and runs alongside the **Jatuncocha** and **Ichiccocha** lakes; if your legs still have it, you can take the detour toward the **Alpamayo** base camp, one of the most photographed mountains in the Andes, which adds a few hours to the day. *The detour is decided in the morning with the guide, based on the weather and how tired the group is.* The evening camp is usually at Llamacorral or near the lakes, lower than the previous ones: you sleep better and it's less cold. It's also the right evening to get the tip ready for the cook and arrieros, to hand over at the end of the trek.

**In practice:** about 5-6 hours of walking, almost all gently downhill · detour to Alpamayo base camp only in good weather · water to filter or boil, never drunk straight from the streams · tips for guide, cook and arrieros to have ready in cash.

### Day 29 — Santa Cruz Trek, Day 4: Back to Huaraz
The last stretch of walking to the end of the trail, then the colectivo back to **Huaraz**. Four demanding but non-technical days, repaid by scenery that holds its own against any Andean trek of this trip, at a fraction of the price and the crowds of the Cusco circuit: that's exactly why it's worth coming all the way here. A hot shower, a real meal and early to bed: the legs have given everything they had.

The last stage descends from Llamacorral through an ever narrower gorge to **Cashapampa**, at about 9,500 feet (2,900 meters), in three or four hours: you lose altitude fast and the vegetation changes, with the first cultivated fields. Here the agency's vehicle takes the group back to Huaraz via **Caraz**, a quiet little town where there's usually a break. *After four days your legs want stretching and a long night: better not to plan anything demanding for tomorrow.* The afternoon in Huaraz is for washing clothes and returning any rented gear to the agency.

**In practice:** Llamacorral-Cashapampa about 3-4 hours downhill · return with the agency via Caraz, about 2-3 hours by road · tips for the staff at the end, in cash · night in Huaraz.

### Day 30 — Rest Day in Huaraz
A free day to recover before returning to Lima. There's little else to do here besides strolling around town, between a last visit to the central market and maybe the **Nevado Pastoruri**, one of the few glaciers of the Cordillera reachable in a day without a demanding trek, for those who haven't had enough altitude yet. Otherwise, rest: hot soups and trucha at every meal, as you eat at every mountain base.

Pastoruri is visited on a day tour: the road heads south along the Callejón de Huaylas and then climbs into the area of the **Puya raimondii**, giant plants that flower only once after decades, up to the parking area; the last stretch, about an hour on foot, reaches the glacier at 16,400 feet (5,000 meters). Pastoruri has retreated a great deal in recent decades, and today the route is presented as a climate change trail. *After the trek there's no need to add more effort: it's an option, not an obligation.* If you stay in town, treat yourself to a long lunch and time to buy tomorrow's bus ticket.

**In practice:** Pastoruri tour about 8-10 hours, leaving Huaraz in the morning, about 35-65 dollars depending on whether entry is included · Huascarán National Park entry about 30 soles · last stretch on foot at about 16,400 feet (5,000 meters), at a slow pace · night bus ticket to Lima to buy today.

### Day 31 — Night Bus to Lima and Departure
The last overnight bus of the trip, this time to **Lima**, for the flight home. Thirty-one days, two extensions beyond the classic Andean circuit, a lake at almost 12,500 feet (3,800 meters), an Inca citadel seen at dawn and a mountain pass at 15,600 feet (4,750 meters): the Peru receding from the window of the last bus no longer resembles the one of the first day in Lima, a month earlier.

Before the bus there's almost a whole day left in Huaraz: ask the hotel to keep your backpack, or for a room until the afternoon so you can shower before leaving. Night buses leave in the evening and reach Lima at dawn, and with an intercontinental flight the same day the margin is tight. *Better to book a flight from the afternoon onward, or keep a spare night in Lima: delays happen on the road down from the mountains.* From the terminal to Jorge Chávez airport, only a booked taxi or ride app, allowing for morning traffic.

**In practice:** Huaraz-Lima night bus about 8 hours, evening departure · Cruz del Sur and Movil Tours among the major companies · terminal to airport an hour or more in traffic · return flight better from the afternoon onward.

## Dove abbiamo dormito
- **Lima:** Miraflores or Barranco, the most convenient and safest areas, away from the historic center but well connected, used both on arrival and on the two buffer days before the extensions.
- **Cusco:** historic center or San Blas, the latter more characterful but all uphill, which you feel in the first days at altitude.
- **Sacred Valley:** Ollantaytambo, the most strategic choice for the morning train to Machu Picchu.
- **Machu Picchu:** one night in Aguas Calientes, the only base that lets you enter on the first shift.
- **Titicaca:** Puno for logistics, Amantaní for the family homestay, the main reason to come all this way.
- **Arequipa:** historic center; Chivay or Cabanaconde as a base for the Colca.
- **Amazon:** lodge in the Tambopata forest, as a package with transfers, meals and excursions included.
- **Cordillera Blanca:** Huaraz as a base for everything (hostels and small hotels, agencies on every corner), plus three nights in a tent during the Santa Cruz trek.

## Dove abbiamo mangiato
- **Lima:** ceviche at lunch in a neighborhood cevichería, never in the evening; then tiradito, causa, lomo saltado and anticuchos from the evening carts. The haute cuisine the city is famous for has to be booked weeks ahead.
- **Cusco and the Sacred Valley:** San Pedro Market for juices and cheap soups; cuy, grilled alpaca and chicharrón in town; trucha (trout) and the classic menú del día at the countryside restaurants of the valley, where you eat better than at the tourist places in Pisac.
- **Titicaca:** dinner with the host family on Amantaní, simple and genuine.
- **Arequipa and the Colca:** Arequipeña cuisine, different from the rest of the country: rocoto relleno, adobo on Sunday morning, chupe de camarones in the traditional picanterías; in the canyon only the set menus of the small village restaurants.
- **Amazon:** meals included in the lodge packages, simple and based on river fish, rice and local fruit.
- **Cordillera Blanca:** hot soups at every meal, trucha from high-altitude farms, cuy and pachamanca (meat cooked underground with red-hot stones) on weekends; the Huaraz central market in the morning for caldo de gallina. For trekking days, a packed lunch prepared by the agency or bought at the market: on the trails there is nothing.

## Practical tips
- **Altitude first.** At least two full days of acclimatization in Cusco before any exertion, and the same goes for Puno and Huaraz: they're three different altitudes, and a single acclimatization isn't enough for the whole trip.
- **Book Machu Picchu well ahead, depending on the season.** The classic Inca Trail sells out 5-6 months ahead for May through August, and even earlier (8-12 months) for the peak June-August dates; permits for the year are released in October of the previous year. Huayna Picchu and Machu Picchu Mountain in high season sell out 3-4 months ahead, even earlier for the dawn shifts: without booking well in advance, better to plan on not climbing them.
- **Tickets only on the official portal, tuboleto.cultura.pe.** All others are resellers with a markup.
- **In Cusco, watch out for street money changers:** better to change only at a bank or an authorized casa de cambio, never on the street, where distracted tourists are handed counterfeit or out-of-circulation bills.
- **Taxis only booked or via app**, in Lima as in Cusco: fake taxi drivers are the most widespread concrete risk of the trip, especially on trips to and from airports.
- **Only buses of the main companies** for every long overland leg, including the overnight to Huaraz: accidents caused by poor vehicles or drivers are a real risk.
- **Choquequirao is left out on purpose:** it's a trek of its own, 4-5 days, harder than the Inca Trail and the Salkantay, that deserves a dedicated trip, maybe before the cable car project, still with no firm date in 2026, changes the place forever.
- **Amazon: Puerto Maldonado, not Iquitos or Manu.** It's the door that fits into a trip of this length without upending it; the other two deserve a whole week to themselves.
- **Two-part structure:** this itinerary also works split in half, see the note at Day 17.

## Chiusura personale
The mistake I wouldn't make again is underestimating altitude, especially on multi-day treks. And you need comfortable shoes, always. The advice I give to anyone leaving is not to get ripped off by the agencies, which you find on every corner in Cusco and Huaraz. Then, if you can, give the Amazon some time: it's incredible.
