## Apertura personale
The Dolomites have been a UNESCO site since 2009, and the official reasoning doesn't talk about beauty: it talks about geology. They are the coral reefs of a tropical sea from two hundred and fifty million years ago, lifted, split and left standing as vertical walls that keep their layering in plain view. They are also, technically, nine separate mountain systems spread over three provinces (Bolzano, Trento, Belluno), and that's the point most guides skip: the Dolomites aren't a place, they're an archipelago of places that resemble each other in the substance of the rock and are completely different in atmosphere, price and number of people.

That's why below you won't find an itinerary with fixed days. You'll find ten areas treated as places in their own right, each with what you need to know to decide whether and when to fit it into your own loop, plus three ready-made roadtrip proposals for anyone who just wants a starting point. The car roadtrip is the format that makes the most sense here: the areas are close as the crow flies but separated by mountain passes, and getting from one to another is already part of the experience, or of the problem, if it happens at the wrong hour in August.

## Scheda pratica

**When to go, summer:** from June to mid-September, with **September as the best month by far**: a third of the people compared to August, clear air, colors, huts still open until the end of the month. July and August are the most crowded and most expensive months, and in June the leftover snowfields on the high passes are a serious variable.

**When to go, winter:** from December to March. The ski season usually starts in early December and closes between late March and early April, depending on snowfall. New Year's and the Carnival weeks are the most expensive and most crowded periods; January on weekdays is the time with the best ratio between open slopes and lift lines.

**The problem of summer overtourism, said without beating around the bush:** Lago di Braies and Tre Cime di Lavaredo between ten and four on an August day are not the experience in the photographs. They're parking lots full before eight in the morning, queues at the trailheads and an atmosphere closer to a theme park than to the mountains. It's no exaggeration: it's the reason why since 2026 both sites have mandatory booking and quota systems. The countermeasures that work are three: go in September, arrive by six thirty in the morning, or choose one of the less photographed areas in this guide, which offer essentially the same geology with a fraction of the crowd.

**Car or public transport:** the car is strongly recommended and is the only practical way to connect the areas to each other: there's no train from Ortisei to San Candido, and buses between different valleys require changes and long times. Within each single valley, though, **public transport works fairly well**: Val Gardena and Val Badia have a frequent SAD bus network, often included for free in the Mobil Card that many accommodations give their guests, and it lets you leave the car in the garage for entire days. Anyone who doesn't drive can build a solid stay in one or two areas well connected by bus (usually Gardena-Badia, or Pusteria); the full roadtrip across all ten areas remains a project for your own car.

**Parking and cable cars, the part that weighs on the budget more than you'd expect:** in the most photographed areas a day of parking in high season costs 15-25€, a cable car round trip 25-38€, and in a day between the two you easily reach 40-60€ per person without having had lunch yet. It has to be factored into the budget: on a week-long loop touching four or five areas, parking and lifts can weigh as much as lodging.

**Reservations, the part that decides everything in summer:**
- **Huts are booked from January-February** for the summer, and for private rooms even earlier. You book directly with each hut or through the CAI booking portal where available.
- **Tre Cime di Lavaredo:** since 2026, car access to the Rifugio Auronzo parking lot requires **mandatory online booking with a time slot**, in addition to the toll of about 40€ per car valid for 12 hours.
- **Lago di Braies:** in high season car access to the valley is allowed **only with online booking between 9 and 16**. Outside that window it's free, if there are spaces.

**CAI membership:** it costs little, gives **significant discounts on half board in huts** (it pays for itself in about three nights) and includes mountain rescue coverage. In Italy **mountain rescue is paid**, and a helicopter intervention can cost thousands of euros: it's the most sensible expense of the whole trip.

**Cash:** in many huts cards aren't used because there's no signal. You go up with cash, always.

**The summer thunderstorm rule:** afternoon thunderstorms in the Dolomites are regular and violent, and are the most frequent cause of accidents. **You start early and are safe before two in the afternoon.**

**Elevation gain:** kilometer figures are deceiving. Here you walk counting meters of elevation gain, not kilometers: it's the criterion used throughout this guide to tell demanding routes from those for everyone.

### Three ready-made roadtrip proposals

**5 days: the compact classic, fixed base in South Tyrol.** Designed for those going for the first time who don't want to move lodging every night: you sleep in Ortisei or Corvara and go out in a star pattern. Day 1: arrival and Val Gardena, ascent to Seceda in the afternoon. Day 2: Alta Badia and the Fanes Park. Day 3: Four Passes loop by car, with a stop at Nuvolau or the Cinque Torri. Day 4: Braies Valley (booking access) and, if the pace allows, a first taste of the Tre Cime in late afternoon. Day 5: loop around the Tre Cime di Lavaredo starting early, then return. Four areas, zero huts, the bare minimum to get a real idea.

**7 days: the extended loop, with one night up high.** It adds the Lagazuoi and the Marmolada, and moves the base midweek from Alta Badia to Brunico to cover the eastern part better. Day 1: Val Gardena and Seceda. Day 2: Alta Badia and Fanes. Day 3: Four Passes, Cinque Torri and ascent to the Lagazuoi; if you want, you can sleep here in a high-altitude hut, the night you'll remember most. Day 4: the Marmolada, cable car to Punta Rocca and Lago di Fedaia. Day 5: transfer to Brunico, free afternoon in the Val Pusteria. Day 6: Braies Valley at dawn to avoid the crowd, then Tre Cime di Lavaredo in the afternoon. Day 7: San Candido and return.

**10 days: the full loop, from the Odle to the Brenta.** It adds the two "wings" of the guide: Vipiteno to the north, for a taste of the less touristy Upper Isarco Valley, and Val di Sole to the west, which technically isn't Dolomites but is two hours by car and offers the contrast: different mountains, different prices, nobody around. Day 1: arrival and Vipiteno. Day 2: Val Gardena and Seceda. Day 3: Alta Badia and Fanes. Day 4: Four Passes, Cinque Torri and Lagazuoi. Day 5: Marmolada. Day 6: transfer to Brunico. Day 7: Braies Valley at dawn. Day 8: Tre Cime di Lavaredo and San Candido. Day 9: transfer toward Trentino and Val di Sole. Day 10: Val di Sole (Pejo or Passo del Tonale) and return.

## Itinerario per zone

### Val Gardena, Ortisei and Seceda
The most accessible and most scenic area for those short on time: from Ortisei a gondola takes you in a few minutes to Seceda, where the grassy ridge suddenly breaks into vertical walls with a view of the Odle: one of the most reproduced images of the Dolomites, and for once reality holds up against the photo. Below, the Alpe di Siusi is the largest alpine plateau in Europe, almost flat, perfect for walking without suffering. Val Gardena is also the center of Ladin wood-sculpting craft, if you want something other than mountains for an afternoon.
*Hidden gem: the Puez-Odle Nature Park, just behind Seceda, where people thin out suddenly as soon as you move away from the gondola station.*

### Alta Badia and the Fanes-Sennes Park
Bordering Val Gardena beyond the Passo Gardena, Alta Badia has a stronger Ladin identity and a slightly less touristy rhythm. The Fanes-Sennes-Braies Park, reachable from San Vigilio di Marebbe, is a system of high-altitude basins between vertical walls, lakes and mountain farms: the part of the Alta Via 1 that almost no day hiker reaches, even though it's within a day's reach.
*Hidden gem: Lago di Fanes itself, often ignored by those who stop at the more photographed Lago di Braies not far away.*

### The Four Passes and the Sella Ronda
The loop of the four passes around the Sella Group (Gardena, Sella, Pordoi, Campolongo) is the classic car outing in the Dolomites, about 55 kilometers in a loop with views that change every few minutes. In summer it can also be done by bike (it's a regular stage of the Giro d'Italia almost every year) or on foot over several days along the Sella Ronda via ferrata. Along the way, the detour to the Cinque Torri (an easy loop among isolated rock spires, with the Rifugio Nuvolau on top of a panoramic knoll) is the stop almost everyone makes and nobody regrets.
*Hidden gem: driving the loop at dawn, before nine, when the passes are still empty of motorbikes and campers.*

### Lagazuoi and the Great War trenches
At Passo Falzarego, fifteen minutes from Cortina, the Lagazuoi cable car takes you to the top of a massif that still bears the marks of the First World War: tunnels dug by hand into the rock between 1915 and 1917, when Austrians and Italians fought over the mountain centimeter by centimeter. You can go up by cable car and come down on foot through the tunnels. In this form it exists in no other Alpine area: military history and geology in the same route.
*Hidden gem: the Tunnels Trail in its downhill version, much less demanding and less exposed than going up, suited to those who want to see the trenches without facing serious elevation gain.*

### The Marmolada, Queen of the Dolomites
The highest mountain in the Dolomites (3,343 meters) and the only one with what remains of a real glacier, now much reduced and monitored every summer after the collapse of 2022. The cable car from Malga Ciapela climbs to Punta Rocca in three sections, with a view that on clear days reaches the Julian Alps. At its foot, Lago di Fedaia is an artificial reservoir that gives the classic reflection of the glacier in the water.
*Hidden gem: the Malga Ciapela side is the touristy one; the Passo Fedaia side toward Canazei, less trodden, offers the same view with a fraction of the people.*

### The Braies Valley
The most photographed lake in the Dolomites, and the one where overtourism shows the most: emerald green water, wooden rowboats, walls closing the valley on three sides. Since 2026 it is also the starting point of the Alta Via 1 toward Belluno. **Car access is limited in high season with mandatory booking between 9 and 16**. Outside that window, or with the shuttle bus, access is simpler.
*Hidden gem: arriving by seven in the morning, before the quota begins: the lake is the same, the light is better and for an hour you have it almost to yourself.*

### The Tre Cime di Lavaredo and San Candido
The ultimate symbol of the Dolomites, three vertical walls visible from dozens of kilometers away. The loop around the base (Rifugio Auronzo, Rifugio Lavaredo, Forcella Lavaredo, Rifugio Locatelli, from which you get the classic view of the north faces) is about ten kilometers with 350 meters of elevation gain, doable by anyone who walks with a minimum of fitness. San Candido, at the foot of the valley, is a tidy town with one of the most important Romanesque collegiate churches in South Tyrol and an old town worth a stroll of its own.
*Hidden gem: walking up from Val Fiscalina instead of driving, avoiding toll and booking altogether: it's a full day, and it's how it was done before the road existed.*

### Brunico and the Val Pusteria
The most important urban center of the Val Pusteria, and the natural hub between Braies, San Candido and the rest of the eastern Dolomites. The old town, with the Stadtgasse climbing toward the bishop's castle, is among the best preserved in South Tyrol and feels like a real city more than a mountain village. It's also the most convenient logistical base for those who want to sleep in one place and move by day to Braies, San Candido and Plan de Corones.
*Hidden gem: the Ethnographic Museum of Teodone, just outside town, a farmhouse museum that tells South Tyrolean farm life better than any information panel.*

### Vipiteno and the Upper Isarco Valley
The northernmost point of this guide, almost at the Austrian border, and the least "Dolomitic" area in the strict sense (here the mountains are rounder, less vertical), but with a medieval old town among the most beautiful in South Tyrol, dominated by the Tower of the Twelve (Zwölferturm), 46 meters high and built in 1472. It deserves a stop for those arriving or leaving from the north, more than being a destination in itself.
*Hidden gem: the Val di Vizze, which opens right east of Vipiteno, with mountain farms and trails almost empty even in August.*

### Val di Sole, the other face of Trentino
The only area in this guide that is not technically Dolomites: geologically it belongs to the Ortles-Cevedale group and looks onto the Brenta Dolomites, a dolomitic massif in its own right that UNESCO includes in the same recognition. It's worth the detour for the contrast: considerably lower prices, almost empty trails, and the Pejo and Rabbi thermal baths as a final reward for the legs. It's the right area for those who have already done the "postcard" Dolomites and are looking for something closer to how they must have been thirty years ago.
*Hidden gem: the Stelvio National Park, which starts right here and offers a wilder high mountain than the South Tyrolean one.*

## What to do in summer

### For strong walkers: passes, via ferratas and traverses
![Hikers on the ridge of the Puez-Odle park, with the dolomitic walls in the background](/images/viaggi/dolomiti-roadtrip/giorno-5-puez-odle.jpg)

The most demanding proposals in this guide, with elevation gain and difficulty given, always **approximate**, because weather conditions and personal fitness change everything:
- **Fanes-Sennes traverse toward Braies** (a stretch of the Alta Via 1): 6-7 hours, about 800 meters of elevation gain, marked trail but long. Requires fitness and an early start.
- **Lagazuoi via ferrata (Tunnels Trail, full version)**: 4-5 hours, variable elevation gain depending on the starting point, medium-high exposure in the tunnel sections. Approved via ferrata kit mandatory.
- **Sella loop on foot (hiking Sella Ronda)**: multi-day, stages of 5-6 hours each with 600-900 meters of elevation gain, for those who want to do the four-pass loop walking instead of driving.
- **Ferrata toward the Marmolada (Ivano Dibona ferrata side or similar on nearby groups)**: hard, strong exposure, reserved for those who already have ferrata experience and to be done with a mountain guide the first time.

**The first time on a ferrata you go with a mountain guide.** It's the most frequent cause of calls to mountain rescue in the Dolomites, and it's not boilerplate advice: it's what hut keepers say to anyone who arrives without proper kit.

### For those who want the view without suffering
![The panorama of the Alpe di Siusi with the Dolomites in the background](/images/viaggi/dolomiti-roadtrip/alpe-di-siusi.jpg)

The same walls, with much less effort:
- **Alpe di Siusi**: almost flat, 1-3 hours depending on the loop chosen, less than 200 meters of elevation gain. The largest alpine plateau in Europe, reachable by gondola from Ortisei or Siusi.
- **Cinque Torri loop**: 1.5-2 hours, about 150 meters of elevation gain, an almost flat loop trail among the rock spires.
- **Lago di Braies loop**: 1 hour, flat, suitable for anyone: the simplest way to enjoy the lake without hurry.
- **Loop around the Tre Cime di Lavaredo**: about 4 hours, 10 kilometers, 350 meters of elevation gain. Demanding for those who never walk, but within reach of anyone with a minimum of fitness.
- **Seceda by gondola**: minimal elevation gain on foot, the cable car does the heavy work and only short ridge stretches remain for the best viewpoints.

## What to do in winter
The Dolomites in winter are a different world from the summer one, with a largely different public and crowding concentrated on weekends, the Christmas holidays and the Carnival week, not across the whole season.

**Skiing and the Sella Ronda:** in winter the four-pass circuit becomes the most famous ski loop in the Alps, connected by the Dolomiti Superski pass (one of the largest ski areas in the world, over 1,200 km of slopes). On high-season weekends, lines at the most popular lifts can exceed thirty minutes; on January weekdays it's a whole other experience.

**Snowshoes and winter walks:** the Alpe di Siusi and the Braies Valley, which in summer suffer from too many people, in winter become quiet snowshoe routes. The frozen Lago di Braies, in particular, is a different spectacle from the summer one, often with far fewer people.

**Christmas markets:** Brunico and Vipiteno have among the most authentic Christmas markets in South Tyrol, less scenic than those of Bolzano but also less crowded.

**Ice climbing and frozen waterfalls:** a niche but growing activity, especially in the Val Pusteria and Val Gardena, for those looking for something different from skiing.

## Where to sleep
The choice of area matters more than the single property, for the reason explained above: public transport works within a valley, not between different valleys. In general, **huts** are the experience to have at least once and are booked from January-February for summer: shared dormitories, dinner at 7, quiet at 10, sleeping bag liner mandatory, almost always cash only. In the valley floor, South Tyrol's **farm stays (masi)** have a quality-to-price ratio that hotels can't match, but they have to be booked months ahead for July and August. Brunico, Ortisei and Corvara are the most convenient logistical bases for those who want to move by day across several areas without changing lodging every night.

## Where to eat
In a hut you eat a precise, repetitive cuisine that after three days you love: canederli in broth, goulash, polenta with melted cheese and mushrooms, Kaiserschmarrn for dessert, and barley soup, the high-altitude dish par excellence. In the valley floor, South Tyrol has its own cuisine that is neither Italian nor Austrian: speck, Schlutzkrapfen (rye ravioli with spinach), strudel, and the autumn Törggelen in the farmhouses, when the new wine comes out. In Val di Sole and in Trentino more generally the cuisine turns toward speck canederli, Storo polenta and mountain-farm cheeses. To drink, Lagrein and Schiava in South Tyrol, Teroldego in Trentino.

## Practical tips / mistakes not to repeat
- **Choose the areas before the lodging**, not the other way around: where you sleep determines which areas you reach comfortably.
- **Go in September instead of August**, the single choice that changes the experience most.
- **Book the huts in February**, not in June.
- **Book the Tre Cime road and the access to Braies**, mandatory in high season.
- **Start at seven in the morning** in the most photographed areas, both to avoid the crowd and for the summer afternoon thunderstorms.
- **Count in meters of elevation gain, not kilometers.**
- **Go up with cash**, because up high cards often don't work.
- **Approved ferrata kit with energy absorber**, and a mountain guide the first time.
- **Get the CAI membership**, for the discount and above all for the rescue coverage, which in Italy is paid.
- **Don't underestimate the costs of parking and cable cars** in the daily budget.
- **Consider the less photographed areas** (Val di Sole, Vipiteno, Fanes) when the main areas are full: same geology, far fewer people.

## Chiusura personale
What stays with you from the Dolomites, in the end, isn't a single view: it's the feeling of having seen ten different places that share the same rock. Val Gardena and Val di Sole are less than two hours apart by car and seem like two different countries: one full of gondolas and boutiques, the other empty and quiet, with the same mountain. That's why it makes more sense to think of them as an archipelago to explore area by area, returning several times, than as a single itinerary to exhaust in a week. The best part, the enrosadira on the walls at sunset, in any case never changes: it happens every evening for two hundred and fifty million years, in every area, whether anyone watches it or not.
